Not every problem is solved by a linear boundary or by a tree. Sometimes you have only a few hundred labelled examples and a curved boundary. Sometimes you need a baseline in ten minutes, with no training at all. Sometimes you must classify a million emails with only counts of words. This chapter gives you the tool for each of those jobs. Support vector machines (SVMs) find the boundary with the widest safety margin, and kernels let that boundary bend. k-nearest neighbours (k-NN) labels a new point by looking at the points most like it. Naive Bayes multiplies simple probabilities and is astonishingly good for text. Beyond the tools themselves, each of them teaches an idea that returns later: margins and hinge loss (ranking losses and deep learning), distance and the curse of dimensionality (Chapters 1 and 13), and generative modelling (Chapter 13). You will build an SVM, a k-NN and a Naive Bayes classifier from scratch and compare each one with scikit-learn.

Prerequisites: Chapter 4 (decision boundary, loss functions), the dot product and vector length (Math PDF, Pillar 1), Bayes' rule (the probability pillar).

5.1 Support Vector Machines: The Widest Street

BASIC EXPLANATION
A support vector machine (SVM) is a classifier that finds the decision boundary with the largest margin, meaning the widest gap to the nearest training points. Those nearest points are called support vectors.
INTUITION
Two villages sit on opposite sides of a field and you must build a road between them. Many roads separate the villages, but the best one is the road with the widest empty strip of land on both sides, because a road that squeezes close to one village will be wrong the first time a new house is built nearby. The SVM finds the boundary that leaves the widest empty "street" (the margin) between the classes. Only the houses touching the street edges matter: they are the support vectors. You could demolish every other house and the road would not move.

Words you need

WordPlain meaning
Marginthe empty strip around the boundary; its total width is 2/‖w‖
Support vectora training point exactly on the margin edge (or inside the strip, or misclassified)
Hard marginno point is allowed inside the strip; works only if classes are separable
Soft marginpoints may enter the strip or cross the boundary, at a price controlled by C
Hinge lossmax(0, 1 − y·f(x)), the price for a point that is not safely outside the margin
Chow expensive a margin violation is (big C means strict, small C means relaxed)

Formal definition

Labels are y ∈ {−1, +1}. The model computes f(x) = w·x + b and predicts the sign. We want every point to satisfy y·f(x) ≥ 1 ("outside the street"). Two parallel lines w·x + b = +1 and w·x + b = −1 bound the street. The distance between them is 2/‖w‖ (the distance between two parallel lines w·x = c₁ and w·x = c₂ is |c₁ − c₂|/‖w‖). So maximizing the margin means minimizing ‖w‖:

hard margin:   minimize ½‖w‖²   subject to   yᵢ (w·xᵢ + b) ≥ 1   for all i
soft margin:   minimize ½‖w‖² + C Σ max(0, 1 − yᵢ (w·xᵢ + b))

The second form is regularization again (Chapter 3): ½‖w‖² is the L2 penalty and the hinge terms are the data loss. Dividing by C·n shows it equals mean hinge + (λ/2)‖w‖² with λ = 1/(C n); this is the same C convention as logistic regression in Chapter 4.

Why the name "support vector"? In the exact solution w = Σ αᵢ yᵢ xᵢ, where the multipliers αᵢ are zero for every point except those on or inside the margin. Only those points "support" the boundary.

A four-point problem, solved

Read the picture: blue points are class −1, orange points are class +1. The solid line is the boundary, the dashed lines are the edges of the street, and the circled points sit exactly on the edges. Point A is far away and is not circled: removing it changes nothing.

Problem 5.1.1 Points: A(0,0) and B(1,0) have y = −1; C(2,2) and D(3,1) have y = +1. A hard-margin SVM gave w = (2/3, 2/3) and b = −5/3. Verify the margin conditions, find the support vectors and the street width.

Formulascore f = w·x + b; margin condition y·f ≥ 1; width = 2/‖w‖.
Plug inA: 0 + 0 − 5/3 = −1.667. B: 2/3 − 5/3 = −1.0. C: 4/3 + 4/3 − 5/3 = 8/3 − 5/3 = 1.0. D: 2 + 2/3 − 5/3 = 1.0.
Computey·f: A (−1)(−1.667) = 1.667; B (−1)(−1) = 1; C 1; D 1. All are ≥ 1. ‖w‖ = √(4/9 + 4/9) = √(8/9) = 0.9428, so width = 2/0.9428 = 2.121.
AnswerB, C and D have y·f = 1 exactly: they are the three support vectors. A has 1.667 > 1, so it is not. The street is 2.12 units wide.
Checkscikit-learn's SVC(kernel="linear", C=1e6) returned w = (0.6669, 0.6666), b = −1.667, support-vector indices [1, 2, 3] (B, C, D) and width 2.1212. The same numbers.
Why it mattersMove point A anywhere on its own side of the street and the model does not change at all. That is what "the solution depends only on the support vectors" means in practice. It is also why SVMs are robust to many easy points and sensitive to the hard ones at the edge.

Problem 5.1.2 Two points only: (0,0) class −1 and (2,2) class +1. Find the maximum-margin boundary by reasoning, then check the width.

Formulawith only two points the best boundary is the perpendicular bisector of the segment; both points are support vectors.
Plug inThe midpoint is (1,1); the segment direction is (1,1), so the boundary is x₁ + x₂ = 2. Use w = (a, a), b = −2a. Support-vector conditions: at (2,2): 4a − 2a = 1, so a = 0.5.
Computew = (0.5, 0.5), b = −1. Width = 2/‖w‖ = 2/0.7071 = 2.828.
Answerw = (0.5, 0.5), b = −1, width 2.83, equal to the distance between the two points (√8 = 2.83).
CheckSVC returned w = [0.5 0.5], b = [−1.], margin width 2.8284.
Why it mattersWith two points the street is exactly as wide as the gap between them: the best you can do. More points make the street narrower.

Soft margin and the meaning of C

Real data overlap, so a hard margin may not exist. The soft margin lets point i violate the margin by an amount ξᵢ = max(0, 1 − yᵢ f(xᵢ)) (the hinge loss) and charges C·ξᵢ.

Hinge value for one pointMeaning
y·f ≥ 1 → loss 0outside the street, correct, no cost
0 < y·f < 1 → loss between 0 and 1correct side, but inside the street
y·f = 0 → loss 1exactly on the boundary
y·f < 0 → loss above 1on the wrong side; loss grows linearly

For instance, if a class +1 point appeared at (1.5, 0.5), its score under the solution above is (2/3)(1.5) + (2/3)(0.5) − 5/3 = 1 + 0.333 − 1.667 = −0.333, so y·f = −0.333 and hinge loss = 1 − (−0.333) = 1.333.

  • Large C: violations are expensive, the margin shrinks, the model fits the training data tightly (low bias, high variance).
  • Small C: violations are cheap, the margin widens, the model is smoother (more bias, less variance).

Compare with logistic loss: both are convex upper bounds of the 0/1 error. Log loss never reaches exactly 0, so every point keeps pulling; hinge loss is exactly 0 for safe points, which is why only support vectors matter.

SVM from scratch (subgradient descent)

The hinge is not smooth at the corner y·f = 1, but it has a subgradient: for points inside the margin (y·f < 1) the gradient of the hinge term with respect to w is −y·x; for points outside it is 0. With the mean-hinge form λ/2 ‖w‖² + mean(hinge):

import numpy as np
def svm_sgd(X, y, lam=0.01, lr=0.1, epochs=2000):      # y must be -1 or +1
    n, p = X.shape; w = np.zeros(p); b = 0.
    for e in range(epochs):
        active = y * (X @ w + b) < 1                    # points inside or beyond the margin
        gw = lam * w - (y[active, None] * X[active]).sum(0) / n
        gb = -y[active].sum() / n
        w -= lr * gw; b -= lr * gb
    return w, b

On the breast-cancer data (569 tumours, standardized, stratified 75/25 split, ys = 2*y − 1):

scratch SVM test acc 0.958041958041958 train hinge 0.038322269873857034 |w| 1.8429720135092937
sk SVC lin test acc 0.958041958041958 nSV 40 |w| 1.885256183294272

scikit-learn's SVC(kernel="linear", C=1/(0.01·n)) has the matching objective. Our loop reaches the same test accuracy (95.8 percent, the same as scikit-learn); the weight lengths are close (1.84 vs 1.89), not identical because plain subgradient descent with a fixed step size only approaches the exact minimizer. Of 426 training rows, scikit-learn kept just 40 as support vectors.

Connection to the real job

Hinge loss and "margin" are everywhere: ranking losses, contrastive learning and triplet losses in embedding models are margin-based. Linear SVMs (LinearSVC, SGDClassifier(loss="hinge")) are fast, strong text classifiers.

Applications

  • Text classification: linear SVM on TF-IDF features is a famous strong baseline.
  • Bioinformatics: small-sample classification of gene or protein data.
  • Computer vision (before deep learning): HOG features plus SVM.
  • Fraud / risk: one-class SVM for "normal" behaviour (Chapter 14).
COMMON MISTAKE
Thinking the SVM is a probability model. It outputs a score f(x), not a probability. If you need probabilities, calibrate them (Chapter 9); scikit-learn's probability=True does an internal Platt scaling which costs extra time.

Check yourself

  1. In 2/‖w‖, what happens to the margin when ‖w‖ gets smaller? Answer: the margin gets wider.
  2. If you delete a point that is not a support vector, does the boundary change? Answer: no.
  3. Does a larger C mean a wider or narrower margin? Answer: narrower (violations cost more).

5.2 The Kernel Trick, RBF, and When Kernel SVMs Are Practical

BASIC EXPLANATION
A kernel computes the similarity of two points as if they were mapped into a higher-dimensional space, without doing the mapping. The RBF kernel is a common choice that allows curved boundaries. Kernel SVMs become slow on very large datasets.
INTUITION
Suppose red and blue dots are arranged as a bullseye: no straight line separates them. Now lift each dot into the third dimension by its distance from the centre: the red dots in the middle stay low, the blue ones on the ring rise. A flat sheet now separates them. A kernel gives you that lifted, higher-dimensional world without ever visiting it: it computes the dot product between two lifted points directly from the original points, which is all the SVM needs.

Why only dot products?

The SVM solution can be written so that data appears only through dot products xᵢ·xⱼ. If φ(x) maps a point into a bigger space, we need only K(x, z) = φ(x)·φ(z). If we can compute K cheaply, the lifting costs nothing.

KernelFormulaBehaviour
Linearx·za straight boundary
Polynomial (degree 2)(x·z + c)^2curves up to degree 2, includes interactions
RBF (Gaussian)exp(−γ ‖x − z‖²)smooth bumps around support vectors; infinite-dimensional lift

Problem 5.2.1 Let x = (1, 2) and z = (3, 1). Show that the kernel K(x,z) = (x·z)² equals the dot product after the explicit map φ(v) = (v₁², √2·v₁v₂, v₂²).

FormulaK = (x·z)²; φ(x)·φ(z) computed term by term.
Plug inx·z = 1·3 + 2·1 = 5, so K = 25. The map: φ(x) = (1, √2·2, 4) = (1, 2.828, 4); φ(z) = (9, √2·3, 1) = (9, 4.243, 1).
Computeφ(x)·φ(z) = 1·9 + 2.828·4.243 + 4·1 = 9 + 12 + 4 = 25.
AnswerBoth give 25. The kernel computed a dot product in 3-dimensional space using only the 2-dimensional dot product and a squaring.
CheckThe middle product is exactly 12, because (√2·1·2)·(√2·3·1) = 2 · 2 · 3 = 12. So 9 + 12 + 4 = 25 matches (x·z)² = 25, and the code printed kernel 25.0 phi dot 25.0.
Why it mattersFor the RBF kernel the lifted space has infinitely many dimensions, but K is one line of code. That is the trick.

Problem 5.2.2 RBF kernel with γ = 0.5 for x = (1,2) and z = (3,1).

FormulaK = exp(−γ‖x − z‖²)
Plug in‖x − z‖² = (1−3)² + (2−1)² = 4 + 1 = 5; γ‖·‖² = 2.5.
Computeexp(−2.5) = 0.0821.

Why it mattersThe RBF kernel is a similarity score. The classifier is a weighted vote of similarities to the support vectors. γ sets how local "similar" is: a large γ means only very close points count.

KEY INSIGHT
γ is the radius of influence of a single point (large γ means small radius) and C is how hard you try to fit. Tune them on a log scale, such as γ, C ∈ {10⁻³, …, 10³}.

A circle problem and the scaling requirement

For the "circles" data (one class inside a ring), a linear SVM scores 0.525 (chance level), while RBF and degree-2 polynomial kernels both score 1.0 (circles linear 0.525 rbf 1.0 poly2 1.0). The circle x₁² + x₂² < r² is a linear boundary in the lifted features (x₁², x₂²), which is exactly what the degree-2 kernel supplies.

Scaling is mandatory for kernels, because they use distances. With a signal only in feature 0 and a noise feature on a scale of 1000:

SVC unscaled 0.578    scaled (StandardScaler in a pipeline) 0.989

The unscaled model is almost at chance (0.58) because the huge noise feature dominates every distance. Always use make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), SVC()).

When are kernel SVMs practical?

Training takes between O(n²) and O(n³) time and memory for the kernel matrix, so kernel SVMs suit small to medium data (up to roughly tens of thousands of rows). Measured here for SVC() on moons data: n = 1,000 took about 0.00 s, n = 4,000 about 0.04 s, n = 16,000 about 0.66 s (times depend on the machine; the growth from 0.04 to 0.66 s for 4× more rows shows the super-linear scaling). For big data use LinearSVC, SGDClassifier, or gradient boosting.

Other kernel methods

  • SVR (support vector regression): fits a tube of half-width ε around the data; points inside the tube cost nothing. Only points outside the tube become support vectors. On 80 noisy sine points with one outlier (+3 added to a single value), SVR(C=10, epsilon=0.1) used 40 support vectors and had error 0.073 against the noise-free truth; with epsilon=0.5 only 4 support vectors and error 0.206 (too wide a tube, so too coarse).
  • Kernel ridge regression: ridge regression (Chapter 3) in the lifted space; closed-form, same RBF kernel. On the same data its error against the truth was 0.125.
  • One-class SVM: learns the boundary around "normal" data only, to flag outliers. On 300 normal points with nu=0.05 it called 93 percent of the training points inliers, and, tested on [[4,4],[0,0]], it returned [−1, 1] (outlier, inlier). Chapter 14 builds on this.
  • Gaussian processes (GP): the Bayesian cousin. Instead of one best curve, a GP gives a distribution over curves, so every prediction comes with an uncertainty. We fitted five points of sin(x) (x = −4, −3, −1, 0, 2):
GP kernel: RBF(length_scale=1.78) + WhiteKernel(noise_level=1e-05)
x     : -2      1       3       6
mean  : -0.9282  0.7584  0.6303  0.0313
std   :  0.0664  0.1072  0.3927  0.9942
true  : -0.9093  0.8415  0.1411 -0.2794

Reading it: between the data points (x = −2, 1) the GP is accurate and sure (standard deviation 0.07 and 0.11). At x = 3 (one unit past the last point) it is less sure (0.39) and already wrong by 0.49. At x = 6, far from any data, the standard deviation is 0.99, about the prior's width, which means "I know nothing here", and indeed the mean 0.03 is wrong by 0.31. A model that knows where it does not know is the reason GPs power Bayesian optimization (Chapter 11). Cost is O(n³), so GPs suit small data.

Connection to the real job

You will rarely deploy a kernel SVM on millions of rows, but small labelled datasets (medical, industrial inspection) are common, and the "similarity to stored examples" idea returns in embeddings and attention. Gaussian processes are the standard tool for expensive experiments, such as tuning a slow benchmark with few trials.

Applications

  • Small-data ML: RBF-SVM as a strong default with a few hundred examples.
  • Engineering: GP surrogate models for expensive simulations or lab experiments.
  • Hyperparameter tuning: Bayesian optimization with GP surrogates.
  • Anomaly detection: one-class SVM on "healthy" sensor readings.
COMMON MISTAKE
Forgetting to scale, or running SVC(kernel="rbf") on 500,000 rows and waiting hours. Check the size first, and scale inside a pipeline.

Check yourself

  1. What does the kernel trick avoid? Answer: computing the high-dimensional features explicitly.
  2. High γ and high C: bias or variance problem? Answer: variance (overfitting).
  3. Why does a GP's standard deviation grow far from the data? Answer: it falls back to the prior, which has no information about that region.

5.3 k-Nearest Neighbours: Ask Your Neighbours

BASIC EXPLANATION
k-nearest neighbours (kNN) predicts for a new point by looking at the k closest training points and taking their majority class (or average value). It stores the data instead of learning weights, and it depends on the distance measure and feature scaling.
INTUITION
You move to a new city and want to know whether a house is expensive. You look at the five most similar houses nearby and average their prices. That is k-NN: no training, no formula, no model. All the "learning" is memorizing the data, and all the work happens at prediction time. It is called a lazy learner for that reason, and instance-based because it stores the instances.

Algorithm

  1. Choose k and a distance (Euclidean unless there is a reason otherwise).
  2. For a query point, compute its distance to every training point.
  3. Take the k closest. Classification: majority vote (or distance-weighted vote). Regression: the average (or median) of their targets.

Cosine1 − a·b/(‖a‖‖b‖)text and embeddings (direction, not length)
Hammingnumber of differing positionscategorical codes

Problem 5.3.1 Training points (1,1)→0, (2,1)→0, (4,4)→1, (5,4)→1, (3,3)→1. Classify the query q = (2.4, 2.0) with k = 1, 3, 5.

FormulaEuclidean distance d = √((x₁ − 2.4)² + (x₂ − 2)²), then vote.
Plug in(1,1): √(1.96 + 1) = 1.720. (2,1): √(0.16 + 1) = 1.077. (4,4): √(2.56 + 4) = 2.561. (5,4): √(6.76 + 4) = 3.280. (3,3): √(0.36 + 1) = 1.166.
ComputeOrder by distance: (2,1) 1.077 → class 0; (3,3) 1.166 → class 1; (1,1) 1.720 → class 0; (4,4) 2.561 → class 1; (5,4) 3.280 → class 1. For k=1: votes {0:1} → class 0. k=3: labels 0,1,0 → two zeros → class 0. k=5: labels 0,1,0,1,1 → three ones → class 1.
AnswerThe prediction flips from class 0 (k = 1 and 3) to class 1 (k = 5).
CheckCode printed distances [1.72 1.077 2.561 3.28 1.166] and the order [1 4 0 2 3].
Why it mattersk controls the bias-variance trade-off: k = 1 follows the single closest point, so it is very flexible and noisy (variance); a huge k averages over everything and becomes close to "always predict the majority class" (bias).

From scratch and with scikit-learn

import numpy as np
class MyKNN:
    def __init__(s, k=5): s.k = k
    def fit(s, X, y): s.X, s.y = X, y; return s
    def predict(s, Q):
        D = ((Q[:, None, :] - s.X[None, :, :]) ** 2).sum(2)     # squared distances, all queries vs all points
        idx = np.argsort(D, 1)[:, :s.k]                          # indices of the k smallest
        return np.array([np.bincount(s.y[i]).argmax() for i in idx])

(Squared distances give the same order as distances, so we skip the square root.) On breast cancer with k = 5:

Speed: KD-trees and approximate search

Brute force costs O(n) per query. A KD-tree (a tree that splits space along coordinate axes) or ball tree prunes most of the points in low dimensions. Measured on this machine, 5 neighbours for 2,000 queries among 50,000 points:

3 dimensions : brute 0.043 s   kd_tree 0.004 s   ball_tree 0.042 s
50 dimensions: brute 0.021 s   kd_tree 2.115 s

In 3-D the KD-tree is ten times faster. In 50-D it is a hundred times slower than brute force (matrix tricks in brute force are very fast, and the tree cannot prune). For high-dimensional embeddings (millions of vectors), practitioners use approximate nearest neighbour (ANN) libraries (FAISS, HNSW, Annoy; not run here), which trade a little accuracy for large speed. This is the heart of vector search in modern AI systems.

Connection to the real job

k-NN is a great baseline (no training, immediate feedback), a recommender building block ("customers like you bought…"), an anomaly detector (points far from their neighbours, Chapter 14) and the way vector databases find similar documents.

Applications

  • Recommenders: similar users or items.
  • Anomaly detection: distance to the k-th neighbour.
  • GenAI: nearest-neighbour search over embeddings in RAG systems.
  • Imputation: KNNImputer fills missing values from similar rows (Chapter 12).
COMMON MISTAKE
Using k-NN with unscaled features, with many irrelevant columns, or on very large data without an index. Also, never use k = 1 accuracy on the training set to judge the model.

Check yourself

  1. Why is k-NN called lazy? Answer: it does no work at training time; it just stores the data.
  2. What happens to k-NN if one feature has a range 1000 times bigger than the others? Answer: that feature dominates the distance; scale first.
  3. Small k leads to bias or variance? Answer: variance.

5.4 Naive Bayes: Multiplying the Evidence

BASIC EXPLANATION
Naive Bayes is a classifier based on Bayes' theorem that assumes features are independent given the class. It multiplies the per-feature likelihoods with the class prior, which makes it fast and effective for text and small datasets.
INTUITION
A doctor sees a fever, a cough and a headache. Each symptom alone changes the odds of flu. The "naive" assumption says: once we know the disease, the symptoms do not influence each other, so we can simply multiply each symptom's likelihood. It is not true (a cough and a sore throat are related) but it makes the model tiny and fast, and for ranking classes it often still works. It is called "Bayes" because it uses Bayes' rule, and "naive" because of the independence assumption.

Formal definition

By Bayes' rule, for class c and features x = (x₁,…,x_p):

P(c | x) ∝ P(c) · P(x₁ | c) · P(x₂ | c) · … · P(x_p | c)

The naive step is writing P(x | c) as a product. We compute the right side for each class and pick the largest (or normalize to get probabilities). To avoid multiplying many tiny numbers (underflow), add logs: log P(c) + Σ log P(xⱼ | c).

Gaussian NBa normal curve per class and feature (mean, variance)numeric features
Multinomial NBword frequencies `P(wordclass)`text counts, TF-IDF

Problem 5.4.1 Gaussian NB with one feature. Class A: mean 2, variance 1, prior 0.5. Class B: mean 4, variance 1, prior 0.5. Find P(B | x = 3.5).

FormulaP(c | x) = P(c) N(x; μ_c, σ²_c) / Σ_c' P(c') N(x; μ_c', σ²_c'), with N(x; μ, σ²) = exp(−(x − μ)²/(2σ²)) / √(2πσ²).
Plug inFor A: (3.5 − 2)² = 2.25, so N = exp(−1.125)/2.5066 = 0.3247/2.5066 = 0.1295. For B: (3.5 − 4)² = 0.25, N = exp(−0.125)/2.5066 = 0.8825/2.5066 = 0.3521.
ComputeMultiply by 0.5: A 0.06476, B 0.17603. P(B | x) = 0.17603/(0.06476 + 0.17603) = 0.731.
AnswerThe model says 73.1 percent class B.
CheckCode printed 0.06475879783294587 0.17603266338214976 0.7310585786300048. Also 0.731 = σ(1): with equal variances and priors the log-odds is (μ_B − μ_A)(x − (μ_A + μ_B)/2)/σ² = 2 × 0.5 = 1. (Gaussian NB with equal variances is a linear boundary.)
Why it mattersThe model is just "which bell curve is this value more typical for?". Training means computing means and variances per class: one pass over the data.

Gaussian NB from scratch

class MyGNB:
    def fit(s, X, y):
        s.c = np.unique(y); s.pr = np.array([np.mean(y == c) for c in s.c])
        s.mu = np.array([X[y == c].mean(0) for c in s.c])
        s.var = np.array([X[y == c].var(0) + 1e-9 for c in s.c]); return s
    def logp(s, X):
        return np.stack([np.log(s.pr[i]) + (-0.5*np.log(2*np.pi*s.var[i]) - (X - s.mu[i])**2 / (2*s.var[i])).sum(1)
                         for i in range(len(s.c))], 1)
    def predict(s, X): return s.c[s.logp(X).argmax(1)]

On the iris data (70/30 split): GNB mine 0.9778, sk 0.9778, agree 1.0. The learned class mean of the setosa flowers is (5.03, 3.42, 1.43, 0.25) with variances (0.148, 0.173, 0.027, 0.010): setosa petals are small and very uniform.

Multinomial NB for text, with smoothing

Five tiny documents: spam "buy cheap pills now", "cheap pills cheap"; not spam "meeting at noon", "lunch meeting now", "buy lunch". The vocabulary has V = 8 words (at, buy, cheap, lunch, meeting, noon, now, pills). Spam has 7 word tokens in total; not-spam has 8.

Without smoothing, P(lunch | spam) = 0/7 = 0. One unseen word then makes the product zero and wipes out all other evidence. Laplace (additive) smoothing adds α (usually 1) to every word count: P(w | c) = (count(w, c) + α)/(total_c + α·V). For spam with α = 1: cheap = (3+1)/(7+8) = 0.267, pills = (2+1)/15 = 0.2, lunch = (0+1)/15 = 0.067.

Problem 5.4.2 Classify "cheap lunch pills". Priors: spam 2/5 = 0.4, not spam 3/5 = 0.6. Use α = 1.

Formulalog P(c) + Σ over words log P(w | c).
Plug in (spam)ln 0.4 = −0.916; ln 0.267 = −1.322; ln 0.0667 = −2.708; ln 0.2 = −1.609. Sum = −6.556.
Plug in (not spam)P(w | ham) = (count + 1)/(8 + 8): cheap = 1/16 = 0.0625, lunch = (2+1)/16 = 0.1875, pills = 1/16 = 0.0625. ln 0.6 = −0.511; ln 0.0625 = −2.773; ln 0.1875 = −1.674; sum = −0.511 − 2.773 − 1.674 − 2.773 = −7.730.
ComputeP(spam) = 1/(1 + e^(−6.556 + 7.730)) = 1/(1 + e^(−1.174)) = 1/(1 + 0.309) = 0.764.
AnswerThe message is 76.4 percent likely to be spam: two spammy words (cheap, pills) outweigh one hammy word (lunch).
CheckMultinomialNB(alpha=1) printed 0.7639474972606253, identical to our 0.7639474972606254.
Why it mattersWithout smoothing the unseen-in-spam word lunch would have forced P(spam) = 0. Smoothing is the difference between a usable and a broken text classifier.

Why does "naive" often still work, and when it does not

For classification we only need the correct class to win, not the probabilities to be exact. But the independence error distorts the probabilities. Test: copy one informative feature five times (adding tiny noise) so the evidence is counted five times:

dup-features acc  NB 0.738  LR 0.745   mean max-prob NB 0.93  LR 0.75

Accuracy is nearly the same (0.738 vs 0.745), but Naive Bayes is wildly overconfident: its average top probability is 0.93, while the true accuracy is 0.74. Logistic regression's 0.75 is honest. Rule: use Naive Bayes for ranking and fast baselines, never trust its probabilities without calibration (Chapter 9).

Connection to the real job

Multinomial NB is the classic spam filter, a strong short-text baseline (ticket routing, intent), and it trains in one pass over the data, so it suits streaming data (partial_fit).

Applications

  • NLP: spam, sentiment, topic, ticket routing.
  • Medicine: quick screening models with few data.
  • Real-time systems: very low-latency classifiers.
  • Baselines: the first model to beat in any text project.
COMMON MISTAKE
Reading Naive Bayes probabilities as real probabilities. They are usually pushed toward 0 and 1. Also forgetting smoothing, which causes zero probabilities.

Check yourself

  1. What is the "naive" assumption? Answer: features are independent given the class.
  2. Why add logs instead of multiplying probabilities? Answer: to avoid numerical underflow (and sums are easier).
  3. What does Laplace smoothing fix? Answer: zero probabilities for words not seen in a class.

5.5 Generative vs Discriminative, and Models to Know by Name

BASIC EXPLANATION
A generative model learns the distribution of the inputs for each class, while a discriminative model learns the boundary or p(y | x) directly. This section also names other standard models (such as LDA, QDA and Gaussian processes) you should recognize.
INTUITION
Two ways to learn to recognize cats and dogs. The generative way: learn what a typical cat looks like and what a typical dog looks like, then compare a new animal with both pictures. The discriminative way: learn only the difference, the line between cats and dogs, and never bother with what a cat looks like. The second wastes no effort on detail that does not help the decision.

Connection to the real job

Interviewers ask "generative vs discriminative?" to test whether you know there are two ways to model a problem. In practice: if you need a probability for a decision and have enough data, use a discriminative model with calibration. If you have few labels but lots of unlabeled data, or need to simulate data, think generative (Chapter 14).

Applications

  • Healthcare / industry: HMMs for equipment-state estimation; Kalman filters in sensors.
  • Finance: Kalman filters for hedge ratios and state estimation.
  • Operations: Bayesian networks for fault diagnosis.
  • Science: Bayesian regression for small experiments.

Memorizing slogans like "generative models are better with small data" without checking. As the table above shows, it depends on the data.

Chapter recap

  • SVM: choose the boundary with the widest margin, width = 2/‖w‖. Only support vectors determine it. Soft margin with hinge loss; C trades margin for fit.
  • Kernels compute dot products in a lifted space without leaving the original one. RBF (γ) is the default. Always scale features. Tune γ, C on a log scale. Kernel SVMs suit small and medium data.
  • SVR uses an ε-tube. One-class SVM models "normal". Kernel ridge is ridge in the lifted space. Gaussian processes give uncertainty and are the engine of Bayesian optimization.
  • k-NN: no training, predict from the k nearest points. Scale first. k small means high variance. Irrelevant features and high dimensions hurt it badly. KD-trees help in low dimensions only; ANN for embeddings.
  • Naive Bayes: P(c) ∏ P(xⱼ | c) in log space. Use smoothing. Fast and good for text; its probabilities are overconfident when features are correlated.
  • Generative models learn P(x | c); discriminative models learn P(c | x). Test claims on your data.
  • Know HMM, Kalman, Bayesian networks, GP, Bayesian regression at "explain in one sentence" level.

Practice set

  1. (easy) w = (3, 4), b = 0. What is the margin width? Answer: ‖w‖ = 5, so width = 2/5 = 0.4.
  2. (easy) Hinge loss when y = +1 and f = 0.3? And f = 1.5? Answer: max(0, 1 − 0.3) = 0.7; max(0, 1 − 1.5) = 0.
  3. (easy) What is the output? np.exp(-0.5 * 0) for the RBF kernel of a point with itself. Answer: 1.0.
  4. (medium) Distance between (0,0) and (3,4) in Euclidean and Manhattan. Answer: 5 and 7.
  5. (medium) With α = 1, V = 10, a class total of 40 tokens, and a word count of 0, find P(word | class). Answer: (0 + 1)/(40 + 10) = 0.02.
  6. (medium) Find the bug: KNeighborsClassifier(1) gives training accuracy 100 percent, so the author reports 100 percent. Answer: with k = 1 each training point is its own neighbour. Evaluate on held-out data.
  7. (medium) Gaussian NB, class means 10 and 20, equal variance 25, equal priors. Where is the decision boundary? Answer: at the midpoint, x = 15 (equal variances and priors).
  8. (medium) An RBF SVM has train accuracy 100 percent, test 85 percent, and 90 percent of training points are support vectors. What do you change? Answer: reduce γ and/or C (a smoother model), and check with cross-validation.
  9. (hard, scenario) You have 20 million labelled sessions and want an SVM. What do you do? Answer: do not use a kernel SVM (O(n²) to O(n³)). Use LinearSVC or SGDClassifier(loss="hinge") on good features, or gradient boosting; consider a subsample for kernel experiments.
  10. (hard) Show that with k = n (all training points) k-NN predicts the majority class everywhere. Answer: every query's k neighbours are the whole training set, so the vote is the same for all queries: the overall majority.
  11. (interview) "Explain the kernel trick to a non-technical person." Answer: "Some data cannot be separated by a straight line. If we add new descriptors computed from the old ones, such as squares or distances, a straight line may work. The kernel trick lets the algorithm use those richer descriptors without ever building them, because it only needs a similarity score between two points, which is cheap to compute."
  12. (interview) "Why does Naive Bayes work for text despite the independence assumption being false?" Answer: classification needs only the right class to win. Word dependencies affect all classes similarly, so the ranking of classes is often preserved, even though the probabilities become overconfident. There are many features and few examples per feature, so a low-variance model with a strong assumption does well.
