A bank has a model that gives each loan a default probability. Four questions follow. Which number tells the manager the model is good (accuracy? AUC?)? Above which probability do we reject a loan? When the model says 0.30, do 30 out of 100 such loans really default? And for a loan amount forecast, how wide is the safe range? Each question has a different tool, and using the wrong one is a very common reason why "good" models fail in business. In this chapter you will compute every classification, regression and ranking metric by hand on tiny examples, choose a threshold from costs, measure and repair calibration (Platt, isotonic, temperature), and build prediction intervals that really cover 90 percent of the cases (conformal prediction), all with real, reproducible outputs.

Prerequisites: Chapter 4 (log loss, thresholds, class weights), Chapter 8 (honest validation), Chapter 7 (boosting). Percentiles, expectation and the normal curve: Math PDF, probability pillar. Outputs come from NumPy and scikit-learn 1.9.1 with fixed seeds. The MAPIE library is named in the skill map but was not installable here, so we build conformal prediction by hand (about ten lines).

9.1 The Confusion Matrix and Threshold Metrics

BASIC EXPLANATION
A confusion matrix counts true positives, false positives, true negatives and false negatives. From it come the threshold metrics accuracy, precision, recall, specificity and F1, which describe different kinds of error.
INTUITION
A smoke alarm can be wrong in two ways: it rings with no fire (false alarm), or stays silent during a fire (a miss). The two errors cost different amounts, so one number for "how often is it right" hides what matters. The confusion matrix counts all four outcomes; every classification metric is a different way to read those four counts.

Words you need

WordMeaning
Positivethe case we look for (default, fraud, disease)
TP (true positive)predicted positive, truly positive
FP (false positive)predicted positive, truly negative (false alarm)
FN (false negative)predicted negative, truly positive (a miss)
TN (true negative)predicted negative, truly negative
Thresholdthe probability cut-off above which we predict positive
                    truly positive   truly negative
predicted positive       TP               FP
predicted negative       FN               TN

The metrics

MetricFormulaQuestion it answers
Accuracy(TP + TN) / nhow often right overall
PrecisionTP / (TP + FP)of those we flagged, how many were real
Recall (sensitivity, TPR)TP / (TP + FN)of the real ones, how many did we catch
Specificity (TNR)TN / (TN + FP)of the real negatives, how many did we leave alone
F12PR / (P + R)harmonic mean of precision and recall
Fβ(1+β²)PR / (β²P + R)recall counts β times as much as precision
MCC(TP·TN − FP·FN) / √((TP+FP)(TP+FN)(TN+FP)(TN+FN))correlation between prediction and truth, from −1 to 1
Cohen's kappa(p_o − p_e)/(1 − p_e)agreement beyond chance

The harmonic mean punishes imbalance: F1 of precision 1.0 and recall 0.01 is 0.02, not 0.5.

Problem 9.1.1 A disease screen on 1,000 patients: TP = 30, FP = 10, FN = 20, TN = 940. Compute every metric.

Step 1Positives = 30 + 20 = 50; predicted positives = 40. Accuracy = (30 + 940)/1000 = 0.97.
Step 2Precision = 30/40 = 0.75. Recall = 30/50 = 0.60. Specificity = 940/950 = 0.9895.
Step 3F1 = 2 × 0.75 × 0.6 / (0.75 + 0.6) = 0.9/1.35 = 0.6667. F2 = 5 × 0.45 / (4 × 0.75 + 0.6) = 2.25/3.6 = 0.625. F0.5 = 1.25 × 0.45 / (0.25 × 0.75 + 0.6) = 0.5625/0.7875 = 0.7143.
Step 4MCC = (30·940 − 10·20) / √(40 · 50 · 950 · 960) = 28000 / 42709 = 0.6556.
Step 5Kappa: p_o = 0.97; p_e = (40·50 + 960·950)/1000² = 0.914; kappa = (0.97 − 0.914)/(1 − 0.914) = 0.6512.
AnswerAccuracy 0.97, precision 0.75, recall 0.60, F1 0.667, F2 0.625, F0.5 0.714, MCC 0.656, kappa 0.651. scikit-learn gave the same values.
CheckF2 (recall-heavy) is below F1 because recall (0.6) is lower than precision (0.75); F0.5 is above F1 for the same reason.
InterpretationThe screen misses 40 percent of the sick (recall 0.6), although "97 percent accuracy" sounds great.

Problem 9.1.2 (the accuracy trap) In the same 1,000 patients, 50 are sick. A "model" that says "healthy" to everyone: accuracy?

Compute950/1000 = 0.95, recall 0.
Answer95 percent accuracy with zero usefulness. Our real screen (0.97) is only 2 points better on accuracy but catches 60 percent of the sick.
CheckThe majority-class baseline always equals 1 − prevalence. Any metric must be compared to its baseline.

Overall numbers hide subgroups. A model with 900 rows from region A at accuracy 0.95 and 100 rows from region B at accuracy 0.60 has overall accuracy (900·0.95 + 100·0.60)/1000 = 0.915. The weakness in B is invisible. Always report segment-level metrics (new versus old customers, regions, rare classes).

Connection to the real job. In a review meeting the first questions are "what is the baseline?" and "what happens to the segment we care about?". Precision and recall map to business words: precision is "wasted reviews", recall is "missed cases".

COMMON MISTAKE
Choosing accuracy with a rare event. Look at precision, recall and the baseline instead.

Check yourself.

  1. TP = 8, FP = 2, FN = 12. Precision and recall? Answer: 0.8 and 8/20 = 0.4.
  2. When is F2 preferred to F1? Answer: when a miss costs more than a false alarm (screening).
  3. Why compare to the majority baseline? Answer: accuracy of "always negative" equals 1 − prevalence.

9.2 Scores That Do Not Need a Threshold: ROC-AUC, PR-AUC, Log Loss, Brier

BASIC EXPLANATION
ROC-AUC and PR-AUC summarize ranking quality across all thresholds. Log loss and the Brier score measure the quality of predicted probabilities themselves.
INTUITION
Thresholds are a decision you make later. Before that, ask: does the model put the truly positive cases above the truly negative ones? ROC-AUC measures exactly this ranking quality, like a teacher checking whether the students she believes are strong really outscored the weak ones.

ROC curve and AUC. Vary the threshold from high to low. At each, plot the true positive rate (recall) against the false positive rate FP/(FP + TN). The area under this curve is the AUC. Its meaning: the probability that a random positive case gets a higher score than a random negative case. 0.5 is guessing, 1.0 is perfect ranking.

Problem 9.2.1 Three positives with scores 0.9, 0.6, 0.55 and four negatives with 0.7, 0.4, 0.3, 0.2. Compute the AUC by counting pairs.

FormulaAUC = (number of positive-negative pairs where the positive scores higher) / (all pairs).
ComputePairs: 3 × 4 = 12. Positive 0.9 beats all 4 negatives. Positive 0.6 beats 0.4, 0.3, 0.2 (not 0.7): 3. Positive 0.55 beats 0.4, 0.3, 0.2: 3. Total 4 + 3 + 3 = 10.
AnswerAUC = 10/12 = 0.8333; scikit-learn agrees.
InterpretationIn 83 percent of random (positive, negative) pairs the model ranks the positive higher.

PR curve and average precision. Plot precision against recall as the threshold varies. The area summary is average precision (AP, "PR-AUC"). The baseline of a random model on this plot is the prevalence, not 0.5.

Why ROC-AUC hides rare-class trouble. The false positive rate divides by the huge number of negatives, so many false alarms still look small. We kept the same score distribution (positives centred 1.5 above negatives) and changed only the prevalence, with 100,000 rows each:

prevalenceROC-AUCPR-AUC (AP)PR baseline
0.500.8550.8530.501
0.200.8560.6400.200
0.010.8570.1060.010

ROC-AUC does not move (0.855 to 0.857), which is its strength (it measures ranking independent of prevalence) and its weakness (it does not tell you how many of your flags will be wrong). At prevalence 1 percent the PR-AUC is 0.106: most flagged cases will be false alarms. Use PR-AUC (and precision at the operating point) when positives are rare.

Probability-quality scores

  • Log loss = −mean[ y·ln p + (1−y)·ln(1−p) ]: heavily punishes confident mistakes.
  • Brier score = mean[(p − y)²]: the mean squared error of probabilities; bounded between 0 and 1, gentler than log loss.

Problem 9.2.2 Labels y = 1, 0, 1, 1, probabilities p = 0.9, 0.2, 0.6, 0.3. Compute log loss and Brier.

Log lossterms: −ln 0.9 = 0.1054, −ln 0.8 = 0.2231, −ln 0.6 = 0.5108, −ln 0.3 = 1.2040. Sum 2.0433, mean 0.5108.
Brier((0.1)² + (0.2)² + (0.4)² + (0.7)²)/4 = (0.01 + 0.04 + 0.16 + 0.49)/4 = 0.175.
AnswerLog loss 0.5108, Brier 0.175 (scikit-learn gives the same).
CheckThe 4th row, p = 0.3 for a true positive, supplies 1.204 of the 2.043 total log loss: one confident error dominates.
InterpretationUse AUC for "does it rank", log loss and Brier for "are the probabilities good" (Section 9.6). A model can have good AUC and bad log loss.
COMMON MISTAKE
Quoting ROC-AUC alone for fraud at 0.1 percent prevalence. Add PR-AUC and the precision at the review capacity.

Check yourself.

  1. AUC 0.5 means? Answer: ranking no better than random.
  2. Which score changes with prevalence for the same model? Answer: PR-AUC (and precision), not ROC-AUC.
  3. A model gives p = 0.01 for a true positive. Log loss on that row? Answer: −ln 0.01 = 4.6.

9.3 Choosing the Threshold from Cost

BASIC EXPLANATION
Choosing a threshold means picking the score cut-off that minimizes the total cost of errors. It uses the different costs of false positives and false negatives instead of defaulting to 0.5.
INTUITION
Airport security sets the alarm level by the cost of missing a threat versus the cost of stopping a harmless traveller. There is no "natural" 0.5. The model gives scores; you choose where to cut, by what each error costs.

The rule. Let a miss cost c_FN and a false alarm cost c_FP. Flag a case if the expected cost of not flagging exceeds that of flagging: p·c_FN > (1 − p)·c_FP, i.e.

flag if  p > c_FP / (c_FP + c_FN)

This needs calibrated probabilities (9.6). For c_FN = 19, c_FP = 1: threshold 1/20 = 0.05.

Problem 9.3.1 20,000 cases, 5 percent positive (1,030 positives). A logistic model gives probabilities. A miss costs 19, a false alarm 1. Find the best threshold from a table.

  • Compute (row 0.05) 19 × 204 + 4,050 = 3,876 + 4,050 = 7,926.
  • Answer The cheapest cut is 0.05, exactly the theory 1/(1+19). The cost at the default 0.5 is more than double (16,344 versus 7,926).
  • Check At 0.05 the true positives caught are 1,030 − 204 = 826, plus 4,050 false alarms, so 4,876 flags in all and precision 826/4,876 = 0.169, matching the table.
  • Interpretation The best operating point has a low precision (only 17 percent of flags are real) because misses are 19 times as costly. Whether that is acceptable depends on whether the team can handle 4,876 flags.

When capacity is limited, use top-K. If reviewers can check only K cases a day, rank by score and flag the top K. On the same data: K = 100 gives precision@K 0.75 and recall@K 0.073; K = 500: 0.54 and 0.262; K = 1000: 0.419 and 0.407. This is how you answer "if we can review 500 per day, how many frauds will we catch?" (26 percent of all positives here). A related summary is recall at a fixed precision, for example "recall when we require at least 50 percent precision".

COMMON MISTAKE
Leaving the threshold at 0.5 after changing class weights or resampling. The probabilities changed, so the correct cut-off changed too.

Check yourself.

  1. c_FN = 9, c_FP = 1. Threshold? Answer: 1/(1+9) = 0.1.
  2. Is the best threshold the one that maximises F1? Answer: only if the costs happen to match F1's balance; usually not.
  3. Reviewers can handle 200 cases per day. Which metric? Answer: precision@200 and recall@200.

9.4 Regression Metrics

BASIC EXPLANATION
Regression metrics measure the size of prediction errors. Common ones are MAE (mean absolute error), MSE and RMSE (squared error based), MAPE (percentage error) and R-squared (share of variance explained).
INTUITION
A delivery app predicts arrival in minutes. Being 10 minutes late is annoying; 60 minutes late is a complaint. Different metrics punish big errors differently, so the right one depends on how the business feels about a big miss.

Problem 9.4.1 Actual y = 100, 200, 300, 400, predicted ŷ = 110, 180, 330, 400. Compute the metrics.

Errorsŷ − y = 10, −20, 30, 0.
MAE(mean absolute error) = (10 + 20 + 30 + 0)/4 = 15. In the same unit as y; every error counts in proportion.
RMSE(root mean squared error) = √((100 + 400 + 900 + 0)/4) = √350 = 18.71. Squaring makes large errors count more.
MAPE(mean absolute percentage error) = mean(0.10, 0.10, 0.10, 0) = 7.5 %.
WAPE(weighted absolute percentage error) = Σ|e|/Σy = 60/1000 = 6 %.
sMAPE= mean(2|e|/(|y| + |ŷ|)) = 7.39 %.
R²= 1 − SSE/SST: SSE = 1400; mean of y is 250, SST = 150² + 50² + 50² + 150² = 50,000; R² = 1 − 0.028 = 0.972.
RMSLE(root mean squared log error) = √mean((ln(1+ŷ) − ln(1+y))²) = 0.0850; it measures ratio errors and is gentler to large values.
AnswerMAE 15, RMSE 18.7, MAPE 7.5 %, WAPE 6 %, sMAPE 7.4 %, R² 0.972, RMSLE 0.085. The code matches.
MetricUse it whenWatch out
MAEerrors cost in proportion; robust to outliersdoes not punish rare big errors

Outliers decide MAE versus RMSE. Ten errors: nine equal to 1 and one equal to 50. MAE = 59/10 = 5.9, RMSE = √((9 + 2500)/10) = 15.84. RMSE is nearly three times MAE because of one bad miss. If one rare miss is truly catastrophic (a flight arrival time), RMSE reflects that; if outliers are measurement glitches, MAE is fairer.

R² is not a quality score by itself. We used the same good model (the true line plus noise of standard deviation 3) on two datasets. RMSE: 2.99 and 2.97, essentially identical. R²: 0.299 and 0.919, only because the second target had a larger spread. A low R² can be a perfectly fine model (the noise is just large) and a high R² can hide a poor model on a narrow range. Look at RMSE or MAE in the real unit and compare with a baseline.

Pinball (quantile) loss for quantile τ: max(τ·(y − q), (τ − 1)·(y − q)). For τ = 0.9, y = 10: a forecast q = 8 (too low by 2) costs 0.9 × 2 = 1.8; q = 12 (too high by 2) costs 0.1 × 2 = 0.2. Under-forecasting is punished nine times as much, so the model learns the 90th percentile (the "stock enough" level).

Residual analysis by segment. Plot residuals against each feature and group. Look for: a funnel shape (errors grow with the value: use a log target or a percentage metric), a pattern (missing feature), and subgroups with biased errors (always under-predicting for new customers).

COMMON MISTAKE
Using MAPE when the target can be zero or near zero (sales of rarely sold items). The score explodes. Use WAPE or MAE.

Check yourself.

  1. One huge error; MAE or RMSE reacts more? Answer: RMSE.
  2. Same RMSE, R² of 0.3 and 0.9: why? Answer: the target spread differs.
  3. Pinball loss with τ = 0.5? Answer: half the absolute error (the median).

9.5 Ranking and Recommendation Metrics (and a Word on Clustering)

BASIC EXPLANATION
Ranking metrics such as precision@k, MAP, MRR and nDCG judge how well relevant items are placed near the top of a list. Clustering has its own metrics, such as the silhouette score and the adjusted Rand index.
INTUITION
In a search page nobody scrolls to result 50. What matters is whether the right items are near the top. Ranking metrics reward good items at high positions.

Words you need. Relevance (a grade for each item: 0 = useless to 3 = perfect). @K: only the first K results count.

  • Recall@K = relevant items in the top K / all relevant items.
  • MRR (mean reciprocal rank): average of 1/rank of the first relevant item.
  • MAP (mean average precision): for each list, average the precision at each position of a relevant item; then average over lists.
  • NDCG (normalised discounted cumulative gain): gain of an item discounted by log₂(position + 1), divided by the best possible ordering: DCG = Σ gain_i / log₂(i + 1), NDCG = DCG / IDCG. With graded relevance it is the standard.

Problem 9.5.1 A search returns six items in this order with relevance 3, 2, 3, 0, 1, 2. Compute NDCG (gain = relevance).

FormulaDCG = Σ rel_i / log₂(i + 1).
Compute DCG3/1 + 2/1.585 + 3/2 + 0/2.322 + 1/2.585 + 2/2.807 = 3 + 1.262 + 1.5 + 0 + 0.387 + 0.712 = 6.861.
Compute IDCG(best order 3, 3, 2, 2, 1, 0): 3 + 3/1.585 + 2/2 + 2/2.322 + 1/2.585 + 0 = 3 + 1.893 + 1 + 0.861 + 0.387 = 7.141.
AnswerNDCG = 6.861/7.141 = 0.9608, equal to scikit-learn's ndcg_score.
CheckThe second 3 sits at position 3 instead of 2; that small misplacement costs about 4 percent.
NoteMany industry systems use gain 2^rel − 1. The same list gives 13.848/14.595 = 0.9488. Always state which version you use.

Problem 9.5.2 Three queries; relevant items (1) appear at: query A [0,1,0,1,0], B [1,0,0,0,0], C [0,0,0,0,1]. Compute MAP and MRR.

APA: relevant at ranks 2 and 4: (1/2 + 2/4)/2 = 0.5. B: 1/1 = 1.0. C: 1/5 = 0.2.
MAP(0.5 + 1.0 + 0.2)/3 = 0.5667.
MRRfirst relevant at ranks 2, 1, 5: (0.5 + 1 + 0.2)/3 = 0.5667.
AnswerMAP and MRR are both 0.5667 here (a coincidence of this tiny example, since each list has one relevant item except A).
InterpretationOn average the first good result is at about rank 1.8 (1/0.5667, roughly).

Clustering is judged by the silhouette score (−1 to 1) and, with known labels, the adjusted Rand index (0 chance, 1 perfect).

COMMON MISTAKE
Computing ranking metrics on a test set where the model could see the same user's later clicks. Split by time or user (Chapter 8).

Check yourself.

  1. NDCG of a perfect order? Answer: 1.0.
  2. MRR if the first relevant item is at rank 4? Answer: 0.25 for that query.
  3. Which metric ignores order inside the top K? Answer: recall@K.

9.6 Calibration: Can You Trust the Probability?

BASIC EXPLANATION
Calibration means that predicted probabilities match observed frequencies, so among cases predicted at 70 percent, about 70 percent are positive. It is checked with reliability curves and can be fixed with Platt scaling or isotonic regression.
INTUITION
A weather app says "70 percent chance of rain" on 100 days. If it rained on about 70 of them, the app is calibrated. If it rained on 40, the app ranks days well (higher number, more rain) but its numbers are wrong. Decisions that multiply a probability by a cost (9.3) need calibrated numbers; a ranking-only use does not.

Reliability diagram. Group predictions into bins (for example 0 to 0.1, 0.1 to 0.2). For each bin plot the mean predicted probability (x) against the observed fraction of positives (y). A calibrated model lies on the diagonal. Expected calibration error (ECE) = Σ_bins (n_bin/n)·|observed − predicted|.

Problem 9.6.1 12,000 rows, 18 percent positive, a real mixture of effects, split 6,000 train, 3,000 calibration, 3,000 test. Three models, measured on the test set:

ModelAUCLog lossBrierECE
Logistic regression0.79410.39090.12240.0138
Random forest (300 trees)0.78130.40520.12460.0238
Boosting, 400 rounds at rate 0.2 (overfit)0.76550.65780.15060.1239
  • Step 1 Logistic regression is naturally close to calibrated (its loss is the likelihood), ECE 0.014.
  • Step 2 The overfit boosting model has a decent AUC (0.77) but a terrible log loss (0.66, worse than predicting the base rate, about 0.48) and ECE 0.124. Its reliability line: predicted 0.000, 0.001, 0.009, 0.072, 0.602 against observed 0.033, 0.093, 0.143, 0.238, 0.432 in five equal-sized bins. The lowest bin says "almost never" when 3 percent happen; the top bin says 60 percent when 43 percent happen. Overconfident.
  • Step 3 The forest shows the opposite classic pattern in mild form: it averages trees, so predictions shrink toward the middle (predicted 0.018 vs observed 0.039 in the lowest bin).
  • Answer Ranking (AUC) and calibration are different properties. The boosting model ranks well enough but its probabilities cannot be used for expected-cost decisions as they are.
  • Check A model that always predicts the base rate 0.183 has log loss −0.183 ln 0.183 − 0.817 ln 0.817 = 0.311 + 0.165 = 0.476; 0.658 is worse than that.

Three repair tools (fit on a separate calibration set, never on the training rows)

  • Platt scaling: fit a logistic regression p' = σ(a·logit(p) + b) on the calibration set. Two parameters, safe on small data. Works when the distortion is S-shaped.
  • Isotonic regression: fit a step-like, monotonic mapping from score to probability. More flexible, needs more data (rule of thumb: thousands of rows), can overfit if small.
  • Temperature scaling: divide the logits by one number T: p' = σ(logit(p)/T). T > 1 softens overconfident predictions. One parameter; it never changes the ranking, so AUC stays the same. Standard for neural networks.

Results on the test set:

ModelAUCLog lossECE
Forest, raw0.78130.40520.0238
Forest + Platt0.78130.40140.0181
Forest + isotonic0.77850.39880.0055
Boosting, raw0.76550.65780.1239

  • Read it Temperature scaling with one number improved the boosting log loss from 0.658 to 0.419 and left AUC unchanged (0.7655). Isotonic cut the forest's ECE from 0.024 to 0.006 and moved AUC slightly (ties created by steps). T = 3.2 says: shrink the logits by a factor of 3.
  • Check AUC for Platt and temperature are identical to raw: monotone transformations keep order.

Calibration after resampling

If you undersample the majority class to balance the data (Chapter 12), the model learns a 50/50 world: on our data the average predicted probability became 0.413 while the true prevalence is 0.188 (the AUC stayed 0.794). The fix is a formula: with β = the fraction of negatives kept, p_true = β·p / (β·p + 1 − p). After correction the mean prediction is 0.177 and ECE fell from 0.225 to 0.014. Class weights have the same effect on probabilities as resampling.

COMMON MISTAKE
Calibrating on the training set (where an overfit model is already too confident) or on the test set (using it up). Use a separate calibration fold or cross-validated calibration (CalibratedClassifierCV).

Check yourself.

  1. Does Platt scaling change the AUC? Answer: no, it is monotone.
  2. A model says 0.9 and is right 60 percent of the time. Over or under confident? Answer: overconfident.
  3. Why not calibrate on training data? Answer: the training predictions are already too confident, so the fix is biased.

9.7 Uncertainty: Intervals, Bootstrap, Quantiles and Conformal Prediction

BASIC EXPLANATION
Uncertainty methods give a range instead of a single number. Examples are confidence intervals, the bootstrap, quantile regression and conformal prediction, which gives intervals with a guaranteed coverage rate.
INTUITION
"The delivery will take 35 minutes" is less useful than "between 28 and 49 minutes, with 90 percent confidence". A prediction interval states how sure the model is. A good interval has two properties: it covers the truth the stated fraction of the time (coverage), and it is narrow when the case is easy (efficiency).

Uncertainty on the metric itself: bootstrap confidence intervals

The test score is only an estimate. Bootstrap it: resample the test rows with replacement 1,000 times, recompute the metric each time, and take the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles. For a test set of 400 rows with 65 positives, the measured AUC was 0.736 with a 95 percent interval [0.672, 0.800]. A model that scores 0.75 on the same data is not clearly better. Report 0.74 (0.67 to 0.80) and compare models on the same rows (Chapter 10).

Two kinds of uncertainty

KindMeaningReducible by more data?Example
Aleatoricrandomness in the worldnotwo identical customers behave differently
Epistemicthe model's ignoranceyesa region with few training rows

An ensemble of models trained on bootstrap samples shows epistemic uncertainty as the spread between members. Warning from our test: 30 boosted models trained on x from 0 to 5 predicted at x = 2.5 with spread 0.101, at x = 4.9 with 0.058, and at x = 8.0 (outside the data) with only 0.182 while the truth was 0.99 and the ensemble predicted −1.08. Tree ensembles are confidently wrong outside the training range (Chapter 6), so ensemble spread alone does not warn you about extrapolation.

Quantile regression

Train with the pinball loss (9.4) for τ = 0.05 and τ = 0.95 to get a 90 percent interval directly: GradientBoostingRegressor(loss="quantile", alpha=0.95). Works with heteroscedastic noise (noise that changes with x), but the coverage is not guaranteed.

Conformal prediction: coverage with a guarantee

Conformal prediction wraps any model and gives intervals with a distribution-free guarantee: if the calibration and test rows are exchangeable (roughly, "same kind of data, in any order"), the interval covers the truth with at least the chosen probability, whatever the model is. The split version needs only a separate calibration set:

  1. Fit the model on the training set.
  2. On the calibration set (size n) compute scores sᵢ = |yᵢ − ŷᵢ|.
  3. Let k = ⌈(n + 1)(1 − α)⌉ and q̂ = the k-th smallest score.
  4. For a new case the interval is ŷ ± q̂.

Problem 9.7.1 n = 1,000 calibration rows, α = 0.1 (target 90 percent). Find k; then check the real coverage on 5,000 test rows. The data have noise that grows with x (standard deviation 0.3 + 0.4x).

Plug ink = ⌈1001 × 0.9⌉ = ⌈900.9⌉ = 901.
ComputeThe 901st smallest absolute error was q̂ = 4.581; the interval is ŷ ± 4.581 (width 9.163).
Answer (overall)Test coverage was 0.910: the guarantee holds (at least 0.90).
Answer (by region)Coverage by x range: [0, 2.5) 1.000; [2.5, 5) 0.980; [5, 7.5) 0.886; [7.5, 10) 0.763.
InterpretationThe average is right but the intervals are too wide where the noise is small and too narrow where it is large. This is marginal coverage, not coverage in every segment.

Conformalized quantile regression (CQR) fixes this. Fit the lower and upper quantile models (τ = 0.05, 0.95), compute the score max(lower − y, y − upper) on the calibration set, take the same k-th smallest value q̂, and widen the interval to [lower − q̂, upper + q̂].

Conformal for classification. Use the score 1 − p(true class). On a calibration set of 1,500 rows, k = ⌈1501 × 0.9⌉ = 1351 gave q̂ = 0.678, so a class enters the prediction set when its probability is at least 1 − 0.678 = 0.322. On 2,000 test rows: coverage 0.886 (target 0.90; with 2,000 rows the standard error is about 0.007, so slightly low but close), average set size 1.43; 57 percent of cases have a single class and 43 percent get both. A set with both classes is the model saying "I cannot decide"; these are the cases to send to a human.

MAPIE (a library for this) was not installable here; its use is standard but "not run here". The hand version above is the same algorithm.

Limits. The guarantee needs exchangeable data: under drift (Chapter 8 and monitoring) the coverage can fail. It covers averages over cases, not a promise for one particular case. The intervals are only as useful as the base model's score.

COMMON MISTAKE
Using the standard deviation of residuals times 1.96 as a "95 percent interval" when the noise is not constant or not normal. Check coverage on held-out data, by segment.

  2. Does conformal prediction need a separate calibration set? Answer: yes in the split version.
  3. What does a prediction set with both classes mean? Answer: the model cannot separate them with the chosen confidence.

Chapter recap

  • The confusion matrix has four counts; every classification metric reads them differently. Accuracy misleads when classes are rare; compare with the majority baseline.
  • Precision is "how many flags are real", recall is "how many real ones we catch", F-beta weighs them, MCC and kappa correct for chance.
  • ROC-AUC is ranking quality and does not depend on prevalence; PR-AUC does, and is the right view for rare events. Log loss and Brier judge the probabilities.
  • Choose the threshold from costs: flag if p > c_FP/(c_FP + c_FN). Our table's best cut (0.05) matched the theory. With limited capacity use precision@K.
  • Regression metrics: MAE (proportional), RMSE (punishes big errors), WAPE (portfolio percent), RMSLE (ratios), pinball (quantiles). R² depends on the spread of the target.
  • Ranking metrics reward good items near the top: NDCG, MAP, MRR, recall@K. State the gain formula.
  • Calibration is separate from ranking. Repair with Platt, isotonic or temperature scaling on a separate calibration set. Resampling and class weights distort probabilities.
  • Report uncertainty: bootstrap intervals on metrics, and conformal intervals with coverage checked by segment (CQR gives adaptive widths).
  • Tree ensembles do not signal extrapolation by their spread.

Practice set

  1. (easy) TP = 40, FP = 10, FN = 10, TN = 40. Accuracy, precision, recall? Answer: 80/100 = 0.8, 40/50 = 0.8, 40/50 = 0.8.
  2. (easy) Spam rate is 2 percent. A filter that passes everything has what accuracy? Answer: 0.98, so accuracy is useless here.
  3. (easy) Actual 10, 20, predicted 12, 15. MAE and RMSE? Answer: errors 2, −5; MAE 3.5; RMSE √((4 + 25)/2) = √14.5 = 3.81.
  4. (medium) Miss costs 4, false alarm costs 1. Threshold? Answer: 1/(1+4) = 0.2.
  5. (medium) Find the bug: precision_score(y_true, y_pred) returns 0.0 with a warning, though the model flags 30 cases. Answer: probably y_true and y_pred swapped or no flagged case is truly positive; check the confusion matrix and the label coding (pos_label).
  6. (medium) AUC 0.90, but log loss is worse than the baseline. What does it mean? Answer: the ranking is good but the probabilities are badly calibrated (overconfident); apply temperature scaling or isotonic on a calibration set.
  7. (medium) A demand forecast reports MAPE 12 percent but many items sell 0 or 1 unit per day. Which metric instead? Answer: WAPE (Σ|e|/Σy) or MAE, because MAPE explodes for tiny actuals.
  8. (medium) A model is trained on a 1:1 undersampled set (kept 1 in 19 negatives, β = 1/19) and says p = 0.5. True probability? Answer: β·p/(β·p + 1 − p) = 0.0263/(0.0263 + 0.5) = 0.05.
  9. (hard, scenario) A loan model has AUC 0.82 overall, but the manager notes complaints from self-employed applicants. What do you do? Answer: compute AUC, calibration and the approval error rates for that segment; if it is poor, check the features available for them, the training counts, and consider segment-aware thresholds or a separate model; report with bootstrap intervals because the segment may be small.
  10. (hard) n = 200 calibration scores, α = 0.05. Which sorted score is q̂? Answer: k = ⌈201 × 0.95⌉ = ⌈190.95⌉ = 191, the 191st smallest.
  11. (interview) "When would you use PR-AUC over ROC-AUC?" Answer: with rare positives. ROC-AUC does not depend on prevalence and the false positive rate is diluted by the many negatives; PR-AUC shows how many flags are correct. Example: same ROC-AUC 0.856 gave PR-AUC 0.64 at 20 percent prevalence and 0.106 at 1 percent.
  12. (interview) "Your model has AUC 0.9, but the business says the probabilities are wrong. Explain and fix." Answer: AUC measures ranking; calibration measures whether 0.3 means 30 percent. Draw a reliability diagram, compute ECE, calibrate on held-out data with Platt, isotonic or temperature scaling, and re-check log loss and the cost-based threshold.
