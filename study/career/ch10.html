A new paper appears and your manager asks, "Can we use this?" A team shows a chart where the new model is 0.5 points better and asks for budget. A recruiter for an applied scientist role asks about your last experiment. In all three cases the skill is the same: reading claims critically and producing evidence honestly. You already do this in performance work: you do not trust one test run, you check the baseline, you look for what else changed. This chapter turns that instinct into a method for papers and ML experiments. By the end you will read a paper in three passes within a time budget, reproduce a result and judge it against run-to-run noise, design an experiment and an ablation, write a paper-style summary or a blog post, and know the ethical rules that keep your work honest. A running example appears throughout: a pretend paper called "Paper X" that claims a retrieval trick improves a question-answering score.

10.1 Reading Papers: The Three-Pass Method

BASIC EXPLANATION
The three-pass method is a way to read a research paper in three stages: a quick overview, a read of the main ideas, and a detailed check. It is used to decide quickly whether a paper is useful.
INTUITION
You do not read a map by walking every street. First you look at the whole city, then the district you need, then the one street you must drive. A paper is the same. Most papers need only the first look. A few deserve the whole street.

The method

The three-pass method comes from S. Keshav's short article "How to Read a Paper". Each pass has a goal and a stop rule.

PassWhat you readTime (rough guide)Question it answersOutput
1Title, abstract, headings, figures, conclusion5 to 10 minutesIs this relevant and worth more time?Yes or no, plus a 3-line summary
2Introduction, method, experiments; skip proofsup to about 1 hourWhat exactly did they do and show?You can explain the main figure and the claim
3Everything; rebuild the method in your head or codeseveral hours, longer for beginnersWould I get the same result? Where is it weak?Notes, doubts, a list of tests you would run

Times are rough and differ by reader and field.

Ten questions to ask of every paper (write the answers in your log):

  1. What problem do they solve, and why does it matter?
  2. What is new compared with earlier work (the novelty)?
  3. What do they assume (data, scale, compute)?
  4. What are the baselines (the older methods they compare against), and are those fair and well tuned?
  5. On which datasets, and how big?
  6. Which metric, and does it match the real goal?
  7. Do they report variance (several runs, error bars)?
  8. Do the ablations (removing parts one by one) support the story?
  9. What are the limitations, stated and unstated?
  10. Would it work on my data and budget?

Where to find papers

arXiv (categories cs.LG, cs.CL, cs.CV, stat.ML) is where most preprints (papers shared before formal review) appear. Others: Papers with Code (papers linked to code), Semantic Scholar (search and citations), the ACL Anthology (language papers), conference proceedings (NeurIPS, ICML, ICLR, CVPR, KDD), Hugging Face Daily Papers, and Distill-style explanatory blogs. A preprint has not been checked by reviewers, so read it with extra care.

A starter reading list

Attention Is All You Need (the Transformer), BERT, ResNet, Adam, Dropout, GPT-3, LoRA, InstructGPT (RLHF), the Chinchilla scaling paper, the RAG paper (Lewis et al.), ReAct, Chain-of-Thought, CLIP, Latent Diffusion, XGBoost, and the classics of your chosen specialisation. Read one a month early on; one a week from the stage where you build ML projects (the study plan in Chapter 7).

Problem 10.1.1 You have 7 hours per week for reading. You scan 5 papers at pass 1 (10 minutes each) and take one to pass 2 and a half of pass 3 (3 hours total). Does it fit?

Formulatotal minutes = passes × minutes each
Plug in5 × 10 = 50 minutes for pass 1; plus 180 minutes for the deep paper
Compute50 + 180 = 230 minutes = 3 hours 50 minutes
AnswerIt uses 230 of your 420 minutes, about 55%. You still have 190 minutes for coding and writing the summary.
Check7 hours × 60 = 420 minutes; 420 − 230 = 190.
Why it mattersWithout a time box, one paper can eat a week. Pass 1 is a filter, and most papers should die there.

Problem 10.1.2 Pass 1 of Paper X (pretend). Abstract: "We add a reranking step to retrieval and improve answer F1 from 79.5 to 84.2 on one benchmark." Figure 1 is a bar chart with no error bars. Conclusion: "reranking is essential." What is your pass-1 decision and what do you note?

Step 1Relevant? Yes, you are building RAG (Chapter 9.5).
Step 2Red flags from questions 5 and 7: one benchmark, no error bars, and a strong word ("essential").
Step 3Three-line summary: "Adds reranker to retrieval. Claims 4.7 F1 points on one dataset. No variance reported."
AnswerGo on to pass 2, with a doubt list: does the baseline use hybrid search? how many seeds? is the gain real beyond noise (see 10.2)?
Check84.2 − 79.5 = 4.7, the claimed gain.

Where it is used

Applied scientists and research engineers read weekly. ML and AI engineers use papers to choose methods (for example whether to add reranking). Data scientists read papers on experiment design and causal methods.

Applications

  • Machine learning: choosing a loss, an optimizer or an architecture.
  • GenAI: retrieval, fine-tuning and evaluation methods.
  • Data engineering: system papers (stream processing, storage formats).
  • Interviews: "tell me about a paper you read recently" is common for ML roles.
COMMON MISTAKE
Reading every paper start to finish, or taking the abstract as truth. The abstract is advertising. Check the experiments table before you believe it.

Check yourself

  1. What is the goal of pass 1? Answer: decide in 5 to 10 minutes whether the paper is relevant.
  2. What is a baseline? Answer: an older or simpler method the new method is compared against.
  3. Why is a preprint treated carefully? Answer: reviewers have not checked it yet.

10.2 Reproducing Results and Reading Noise

BASIC EXPLANATION
Reproducing a result means running the method again to get the same outcome. Because results vary from run to run, you compare differences with the normal noise before trusting them.
INTUITION
If a bus takes 48 minutes on Monday and 52 minutes on Tuesday with the same route, you know a single trip is not the truth. ML training has the same noise: random starting weights and random data order. A 0.5-point win from one run may be luck.

Reproducibility checklist

To reproduce a result (get the same outcome from the same method), version everything:

  • Code: a Git commit hash.
  • Data: a dataset version or checksum, and the exact split.
  • Config: every hyperparameter (settings you choose, such as learning rate).
  • Environment: library versions (a lock file or container image), hardware, compute time.
  • Seeds: the random-number seeds. Fixing a seed makes one run repeatable, but results should still be shown over several seeds.
  • Report: compute used, number of runs, mean and spread.

Tools: Git, DVC or similar for data, wandb or MLflow-style trackers for runs, Hugging Face Hub for models and datasets, lm-evaluation-harness for standard language-model evaluations. Cheap compute: Colab, Kaggle notebooks, Lightning AI, cloud credits and spot (discounted, interruptible) machines. Check each service's current free limits, which change.

Problem 10.2.1 (simulation) Two models are truly identical: accuracy has a mean of 91.0% and a standard deviation of 0.4 points from run to run. Train 5 seeds of each and compare.

import random, statistics as st, math
random.seed(0)
base = [random.gauss(91.0, 0.4) for _ in range(5)]
new  = [random.gauss(91.0, 0.4) for _ in range(5)]
print("baseline runs:", [round(x, 2) for x in base])
print("new runs     :", [round(x, 2) for x in new])
mb, mn = st.mean(base), st.mean(new)
sb, sn = st.stdev(base), st.stdev(new)
print(f"baseline {mb:.2f} +- {sb:.2f}   new {mn:.2f} +- {sn:.2f}   diff {mn-mb:+.2f}")
se = math.sqrt(sb**2/5 + sn**2/5)
t = (mn - mb) / se
print(f"Welch t = {t:.2f}  (|t| below about 2.3 for 5 runs each means no clear difference)")
wins = 0; N = 100000
for _ in range(N):
    if random.gauss(91, 0.4) - random.gauss(91, 0.4) >= 0.5: wins += 1
print("single-run luck: P(new beats base by >= 0.5 with no real gain) =", round(wins / N, 3))
baseline runs: [91.38, 90.44, 90.73, 91.15, 90.59]
new runs     : [90.97, 91.07, 90.67, 90.48, 91.08]
baseline 90.86 +- 0.39   new 90.85 +- 0.27   diff -0.00
Welch t = -0.02  (|t| below about 2.3 for 5 runs each means no clear difference)
single-run luck: P(new beats base by >= 0.5 with no real gain) = 0.189
  • Step 1 Look at the baseline runs alone: they range from 90.44 to 91.38, almost a full point, with no change in the method at all.
  • Step 2 The means are 90.86 and 90.85, a difference of about zero. The standard error of the difference is √(0.39²/5 + 0.27²/5) = √(0.0450) = 0.21, so t = diff / se, about −0.02.
  • Step 3 The last line runs 100,000 pretend single-run comparisons. In 18.9% of them the "new" model beat the baseline by 0.5 or more with no real improvement.
  • Answer One run each would have a nearly 1-in-5 chance of showing a fake 0.5-point win. With 5 runs each, the means agree and no gain is visible.
  • Check The difference of two runs has a spread of 0.4 × √2 = 0.566. A gain of 0.5 is 0.5 / 0.566 = 0.88 spreads above zero, and the chance of exceeding that is about 0.19 on the normal curve. It matches 0.189.
  • Why it matters A gain smaller than the run-to-run spread is not evidence. Report the mean and standard deviation over several seeds, and use a test or confidence interval (see the learner's statistics document).

Problem 10.2.2 You try to reproduce Paper X. They report 84.2 F1. You get 83.1, 83.6, 82.9, 83.4, 83.0 over 5 seeds. Is this a successful reproduction?

Step 1Mean = (83.1 + 83.6 + 82.9 + 83.4 + 83.0) / 5 = 416.0 / 5 = 83.2.
Step 2Deviations from the mean: −0.1, 0.4, −0.3, 0.2, −0.2. Squares: 0.01, 0.16, 0.09, 0.04, 0.04; sum 0.34. Sample variance 0.34 / 4 = 0.085, standard deviation about 0.29.
Step 3The paper's 84.2 is (84.2 − 83.2) / 0.29 = 3.4 standard deviations above your mean. That is far outside your run-to-run spread.
AnswerYou reproduced the direction of the result but not the size: 83.2 versus 84.2. Report it honestly as "partial reproduction, about 1 point lower", then look for differences (data version, preprocessing, tuning, tokenizer).
CheckCompute the mean twice; 83.2 × 5 = 416.0, matching the sum.

The picture: how to write a reproduction report

Claim: +4.7 F1 on benchmark B          Paper: 84.2
My setup: commit abc123, data v2, 5 seeds, 1 GPU-hour each
My result: 83.2 +- 0.3                  Gap: -1.0
Possible causes: baseline tuning, preprocessing, undisclosed trick
Verdict: partially reproduced

Where it is used

ML engineers reproduce before adopting a method. Research roles reproduce as the first step of every project. Engineers already do the same when they re-check a benchmark.

Applications

  • Model selection: avoid shipping a change that is only noise.
  • MLOps: run tracking makes experiments repeatable.
  • Data science: check that an analysis can be rerun on fresh data.
COMMON MISTAKE
Comparing single runs, or tuning your own method for hours while leaving the baseline at default settings. Give the baseline the same tuning budget.

Check yourself

  1. Why use several seeds? Answer: results vary from run to run, so you need the spread.
  2. What goes into a reproducibility record? Answer: code version, data version, config, environment, seeds, compute.
  3. A gain of 0.2 with run spread 0.4: evidence? Answer: no; it is smaller than the noise.

10.3 Experimental Design

BASIC EXPLANATION
Experimental design is the planning of an experiment with a clear question, a fair comparison, controlled conditions and a chosen metric. It is used to get results you can trust.
INTUITION
A school testing a new teaching method keeps the class size, the syllabus and the exam the same, changes one thing, and repeats. If you change the method and the class size together, you cannot say which one caused the improvement. An experiment is a fair race with one difference.

The parts of a good experiment

  • Question and hypothesis. A question: "Does reranking help?" A falsifiable hypothesis (one that data could prove wrong): "Adding a reranker raises F1 by at least 1 point on our test set." Decide the success threshold before running.
  • Baseline. The method to beat. Include a simple baseline (keyword search) and the current best you can run.
  • Controls. Everything except the change stays the same: data, split, budget, tuning effort.
  • Confounders. Hidden causes that move the result: a bigger model, more training time, a different data version. List them and neutralise them.
  • Randomization. Random seeds, random assignment to groups in online tests, shuffled data.
  • Power and sample size. Power is the chance of detecting a real effect. Small test sets and few runs give weak power. A tiny test set makes a 1-point gain meaningless.
  • Multiple comparisons. Each extra test is another chance of a false positive.
  • Reporting. Mean and spread over seeds, the exact numbers, the negative results too.

Multiple comparisons, with numbers

If you test one idea at the 5% level, the chance of a false positive is 5%. If you test 20 ideas and keep the best, the chance that at least one looks significant is 1 − 0.95²⁰. One fix is the Bonferroni correction: divide the threshold by the number of tests.

for m in (1, 5, 20, 100):
    print(m, "tests: P(at least one false positive at 0.05) =", round(1-0.95**m,3), " Bonferroni alpha =", 0.05/m)
1 tests: P(at least one false positive at 0.05) = 0.05  Bonferroni alpha = 0.05
5 tests: P(at least one false positive at 0.05) = 0.226  Bonferroni alpha = 0.01
20 tests: P(at least one false positive at 0.05) = 0.642  Bonferroni alpha = 0.0025
100 tests: P(at least one false positive at 0.05) = 0.994  Bonferroni alpha = 0.0005

Problem 10.3.1 A teammate tried 20 feature ideas on one validation set and reports the best, which "improves AUC significantly (p = 0.03)". Should you believe it?

Step 1With 20 tries and a 0.05 threshold, the chance that at least one looks significant by luck is 0.642 (see the output).
Step 2Bonferroni for 20 tests needs p < 0.05 / 20 = 0.0025. The reported p = 0.03 does not pass.
Step 3The honest fix: choose a final candidate, then confirm it on a held-out test set that was never used for choosing.
AnswerDo not believe it yet. A p-value of 0.03 after 20 attempts is weak evidence; test the winner once on fresh data.
Check0.95²⁰ = 0.358, and 1 − 0.358 = 0.642.
Why it mattersThis is called "p-hacking" or "researcher degrees of freedom", and it explains many results that fail to replicate.

Problem 10.3.2 You must decide how many seeds you need. The run-to-run standard deviation is 0.4 and you want to detect a real gain of 0.5. Using the rough rule that the standard error of a mean difference is 0.4 × √(2 / n), find n so that 0.5 is about 2 standard errors.

Formulase = 0.4 × √(2 / n); we need 0.5 / se ≥ 2, so se ≤ 0.25.
Plug in0.4 × √(2 / n) ≤ 0.25 gives √(2 / n) ≤ 0.625, so 2 / n ≤ 0.3906.
Computen ≥ 2 / 0.3906 = 5.12, so n = 6.
AnswerAbout 6 seeds per method. With 5 the margin is thin (se = 0.253).
CheckFor n = 6: 0.4 × √(1/3) = 0.231, and 0.5 / 0.231 = 2.2. For n = 5: 0.4 × √0.4 = 0.253, and 0.5 / 0.253 = 1.98, just under 2.
Why it mattersSmaller gains need many more runs: halving the gain to 0.25 needs four times as many seeds. This is a rule of thumb, not a full power analysis.

Where it is used

Data scientists design A/B tests (the learner's statistics document), ML engineers compare models, applied scientists design studies. The same discipline applies to any measurement: control the environment, change one variable, repeat.

Applications

  • A/B testing: randomised assignment and guardrail metrics.
  • Model comparison: same splits, same tuning budget, several seeds.
  • Offline evaluation: held-out test sets used once.
  • Engineering benchmarks: one change per test, repeated runs.
COMMON MISTAKE
Tuning on the test set, then reporting the same set as your result. Keep a train set, a validation set for choices, and a test set touched once.

Check yourself

  1. What is a falsifiable hypothesis? Answer: a claim that data could show to be wrong, with a threshold set beforehand.
  2. Why do 20 tests inflate false positives? Answer: each test is a new chance of luck; the total chance adds up to 64%.
  3. What is a confounder? Answer: a hidden cause that changes the result along with the thing you test.

10.4 Ablations and Applied Research Projects

BASIC EXPLANATION
An ablation removes or changes one part of a system at a time to see how much that part contributes. It is used in applied research projects to show what really matters.
INTUITION
To find which ingredient makes the soup taste good, you cook it again leaving out one ingredient at a time. If the soup is still good without the herbs, the herbs were not the point. An ablation removes one part of a system at a time and measures what is lost.

How to run an ablation

  1. Fix the full system and measure it (mean over seeds).
  2. Remove or replace one component, keep everything else, remeasure.
  3. Also measure the baseline with all components removed.
  4. Compare the drop of each component to the noise from 10.2. A drop smaller than the noise is not a finding.

The numbers below are illustrative (made up for teaching), as the mean of 5 seeds.

rows = [("full model", 84.2), ("- reranker", 82.9), ("- hybrid search", 83.1), ("- query rewrite", 84.0), ("- all three (baseline)", 79.5)]
full = rows[0][1]
for name, f1 in rows:
    print(f"{name:24s} F1 {f1:5.1f}   drop vs full {full - f1:4.1f}")
print("sum of single drops:", round(sum(full - f for _, f in rows[1:4]), 1), " vs total gain over baseline:", round(full - rows[4][1], 1))
full model               F1  84.2   drop vs full  0.0
- reranker               F1  82.9   drop vs full  1.3
- hybrid search          F1  83.1   drop vs full  1.1
- query rewrite          F1  84.0   drop vs full  0.2
- all three (baseline)   F1  79.5   drop vs full  4.7
sum of single drops: 2.6  vs total gain over baseline: 4.7

Problem 10.4.1 Read the table. Which components matter, given a run-to-run standard deviation of 0.3?

Step 1Query rewrite: drop 0.2, below the noise of 0.3. Not shown to matter.
Step 2Reranker: 1.3 is about 4 standard deviations (1.3 / 0.3 = 4.3). Hybrid search: 1.1 is about 3.7. Both matter.
Step 3The single drops add to 2.6 but the total gain is 4.7, so the parts interact (together they give more than the sum), or other parts of the pipeline matter too.
AnswerKeep the reranker and hybrid search; drop the query rewrite unless a larger test shows value. Report the interaction honestly.
Check1.3 + 1.1 + 0.2 = 2.6, and 84.2 − 79.5 = 4.7.
Why it mattersAblations tell you where to spend engineering time and where to cut cost. A component that adds latency and no accuracy should go.

Applied research projects for you

An applied project follows the same steps as a paper: question, baseline, experiment, ablation, write-up. Ideas that match the skill maps: improve retrieval quality for a chatbot; evaluate a small domain fine-tune; Indian-language NLP; efficient inference (latency and cost per 1,000 requests); better evaluation methods for LLM answers; and anomaly detection on operational data, such as detecting a delivery-time slowdown across weeks with a statistical test and reporting precision and recall. That last one is a signature project because it joins real operational data with ML. Reproduce a paper at small scale first, run ablations, try one improvement, and document honestly even if the improvement fails.

Algorithm design in one paragraph

Research also includes designing algorithms: paradigms (divide and conquer, greedy, dynamic programming, randomization, approximation), proving correctness (induction, invariants), and analysing complexity. Resources: CLRS, Skiena's Algorithm Design Manual, and the MIT 6.006 and 6.046 lectures. Use them after the DSA practice in Chapter 7.

Where it is used

Applied scientist roles, ML platform teams and any team that must justify a cost with evidence.

Applications

  • GenAI: which of retrieval, reranking and prompt changes earn their latency.
  • ML engineering: which features justify their pipeline cost.
  • Performance and AIOps: which detector settings give acceptable false alarms.
COMMON MISTAKE
Reporting only the full model and not the ablation, or claiming a component "helps" when its effect is inside the noise.

Check yourself

  1. What does an ablation do? Answer: removes one part at a time and measures the loss.
  2. A component's drop is 0.2 and noise is 0.3. Conclusion? Answer: not shown to matter.
  3. Why can the single drops not add to the total? Answer: components interact.

10.5 Writing Papers and Technical Blog Posts

BASIC EXPLANATION
A technical paper or blog post reports a problem, method, results and limits in a clear structure. It is used to share work and let others check it.
INTUITION
A good paper is a report you would hand to a busy colleague: the problem, the idea, the proof, the limits, in that order. A good blog post is the same story told to a friend. Writing is how you find out whether you really understood. Chapter 4 gave the general rules of clear writing; here are the shapes specific to research.

Structure of a research paper

SectionJobTypical content
TitleSay what and for whomPlain claim, not a slogan
AbstractFull story in about 150 to 250 wordsProblem, gap, method, result with a number, limit
IntroductionWhy this mattersProblem, what is missing, contributions as a short list
Related workWhere you sitWhat others did and how you differ
MethodWhat you didEnough detail to repeat it; define notation
ExperimentsEvidenceData, baselines, setup, metrics, seeds
ResultsWhat happenedTables, plots, ablations, error bars
LimitationsHonestyWhere it fails, what you did not test
ConclusionCloseOne paragraph, no new claims

Tools: LaTeX (a typesetting language) on Overleaf (an online editor), Zotero (a reference manager), clear figures with labelled axes, and consistent notation. Writing rules: short sentences, define every term, state contributions in plain words ("We show X. We release code and data."), and ask a colleague to read it for feedback. Learn the review process: reviewers judge novelty, correctness, clarity and reproducibility; many venues use a reproducibility checklist.

Venues, easiest start first: a workshop paper (shorter, friendlier), an arXiv preprint, a conference, a journal. A technical blog post with code is a perfectly good start.

Technical blog posts

Post types: tutorials, "what I learned" retrospectives, project write-ups, paper summaries, and benchmark comparisons. Platforms: Medium, dev.to, Hashnode or a personal blog. A schedule of 2 posts a month is plenty. Upgrade over time to local meetup talks, workshop papers, open-source docs and Kaggle write-ups.

Craft in five steps: (1) name the reader ("a data analyst who knows SQL"), (2) outline in 5 headings, (3) code that runs, with the output pasted, (4) one diagram, (5) edit ruthlessly, then pick a clear title.

Problem 10.5.1 Rewrite this weak abstract in four sentences: "We propose a novel approach for improving chatbots. Results are good. Our method is better than others on many datasets."

Step 1Find the missing parts: problem (which chatbot, what failure), method, result with a number, and limits.
Step 2Write: "Company-document chatbots often fail because the right passage is not retrieved. We add a reranking step that rescores the top 50 search results and keeps 5. On one internal question set of 200 questions, answer F1 rises from 79.5 to 84.2 (mean of 5 seeds, standard deviation 0.3). We have not tested other languages or documents longer than 20 pages."
AnswerThe rewrite names the problem, method, a number with its spread, and a limit, in four sentences. (Numbers belong to the pretend Paper X.)
CheckEach of the four jobs of an abstract is present: problem, method, result, limit.
Why it mattersReaders and recruiters decide in the first 30 seconds; vague words ("novel", "good") are not evidence.

Problem 10.5.2 A blog post draft is 1,800 words: introduction 600, method 200, code 100, results 100, history of the field 800. What is wrong, and what is the budget fix?

Step 1The history takes 800 / 1,800 = 44% and the results take 100 / 1,800 = 5.6%. The reader came for the method and the results.
Step 2Target: introduction 250, method 500, code (with output) 300, results 350, lessons and limits 250. Total 250 + 500 + 300 + 350 + 250 = 1,650 words.
AnswerCut history to a short paragraph in the introduction and move the space to method and results; the post becomes 1,650 words with results at 21% of the text.
Check350 / 1,650 = 0.212.

Where it is used

Applied scientists write reports and papers; ML and data engineers write design docs and blog posts that win interviews (Chapter 11 uses blog posts as portfolio pieces).

Applications

  • Hiring: a write-up of a reproduction is stronger than a certificate.
  • Teams: design docs and decision records (Chapter 4).
  • Community: meetup talks, Kaggle write-ups, open-source docs.
COMMON MISTAKE
Hiding limitations. Reviewers and readers trust you more when you say where the method fails.

Check yourself

  1. What goes in the abstract? Answer: problem, method, result with a number, and a limit.
  2. What is the easiest first venue? Answer: a workshop paper, an arXiv preprint or a blog post.
  3. Why paste real output in a post? Answer: so readers can trust and rerun it.

10.6 Ethics and Honest Reporting

BASIC EXPLANATION
Honest reporting means describing all results, including failures and limits, and not overstating claims. Research ethics covers fairness, privacy and correct use of data.
INTUITION
A hospital would never hide a failed lab test because the report looks better without it. Research ethics is that same rule applied to data, people and results.

The rules

  • Consent and data rights. Use data you are allowed to use. For personal data, follow the consent given, your company's policy and the law (India's Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023, and GDPR for European users; check current text and your compliance team). Do not scrape or use personal data without checking terms.
  • Privacy. Remove or hash identifiers, keep only what you need, and watch for re-identification (joining "anonymous" data to find a person).
  • Bias and fairness. Check performance across groups (for example by region, gender, language). A model with a high average can be poor for a smaller group.
  • Dual use. Some methods help attackers (deepfakes, surveillance, malware). Think about misuse before publishing and what safeguards you can add.
  • Responsible disclosure. If you find a security flaw or a harmful model behaviour, tell the owner privately and give time to fix it before publishing details.
  • Honest reporting. Report negative results, all seeds, all tuned baselines, failed ideas, and the limits. Do not drop bad runs. Do not cherry-pick the benchmark. Credit others' work and cite sources.
  • Applied versus fundamental research. Fundamental research asks "how does this work?" and may take years; applied research asks "does this solve our problem under our constraints?" Industry research is usually applied, academic often fundamental, and the best work in either is honest.

Problem 10.6.1 Your anomaly detector catches 90% of regressions on the 5 services where you tuned it and 55% on the other 15 services. The report headline says "90% recall". What is the honest number?

Step 1Services: 5 + 15 = 20. Weighted recall = (5 × 0.90 + 15 × 0.55) / 20.
Step 2Numerator: 4.5 + 8.25 = 12.75. Divide by 20: 0.6375.
Step 3The 90% was measured where you tuned; the fair number is overall 64% (and you should say so).
AnswerReport 64% overall (assuming equal numbers of regressions per service), and give the split (90% tuned services, 55% others).
Check0.6375 lies between 0.55 and 0.90, and is closer to 0.55 because 15 of 20 services have it.
Why it mattersHeadlines from the best subset are a quiet form of dishonesty. You would be the one on call when the detector misses.

Problem 10.6.2 You find that a public chatbot leaks other users' chat titles when you vary a URL parameter. What do you do?

Step 1Stop testing as soon as you have confirmed the problem; do not read or save other people's data.
Step 2Report privately to the owner through their security contact, with steps to reproduce and no real user data.
Step 3Give reasonable time to fix before any public write-up; publish details only after the fix, or not at all.
AnswerPrivate report with a reproducible description, minimal access, and delayed disclosure.
CheckYou never published user data, and the owner could act on your report.

Where it is used

Every data and ML role handles personal data. Interviews for ML roles include fairness and privacy questions (Chapter 7). Regulated domains (banking, health) audit these practices.

Applications

  • Banking and insurance: fairness checks on credit and claims models.
  • Healthcare: consent and de-identification.
  • GenAI: data licensing, prompt-injection and leakage risks.
  • Open source: licences and attribution.
COMMON MISTAKE
Treating ethics as a final checkbox. Raise data rights, bias and misuse at the design stage (Chapter 9, step 11), when changes are cheap.

Check yourself

  1. Name two honest-reporting habits. Answer: report all seeds and negative results; keep the baseline fairly tuned.
  2. What is responsible disclosure? Answer: telling the owner privately and allowing time to fix before publishing.
  3. Why check fairness by group? Answer: a good average can hide poor results for a smaller group.

Chapter recap

  • Read in three passes: filter in 10 minutes, understand in about an hour, rebuild only the few that matter.
  • Keep a paper log: title, 3-line summary, key idea, what you would test.
  • Reproduce by versioning code, data, config, environment and seeds, and report mean and spread over several seeds.
  • A gain smaller than run-to-run noise is not evidence; single runs fooled us about 19% of the time in our simulation.
  • A good experiment has a falsifiable hypothesis, a fair baseline, controls, randomization and a final untouched test set.
  • Many tests inflate false positives (64% at 20 tests); correct or confirm on fresh data.
  • Ablations remove one part at a time; compare each drop to the noise.
  • Write papers and posts in a clear structure with numbers and limits, and keep ethics in the design from the start.

Practice set

  1. (Easy) Pass 1 takes 10 minutes. How many papers can you scan in 2 hours? Answer: 120 / 10 = 12.
  2. (Easy) What do you note in the paper log? Answer: title, link, 3-line summary, key idea, what you would test, date.
  3. (Easy) Five runs: 90, 91, 92, 91, 91. Mean? Answer: 455 / 5 = 91.
  4. (Medium) Five runs above, sample standard deviation? Answer: deviations −1, 0, 1, 0, 0; squares 1, 0, 1, 0, 0 sum 2; variance 2 / 4 = 0.5; standard deviation 0.707.
  5. (Medium) False-positive chance for 10 tests at 0.05? Answer: 1 − 0.95¹⁰ = 1 − 0.599 = 0.401.
  6. (Medium) What is the output? Ablation: full 88.0, without part A 87.9, noise 0.4. Answer: The drop is 0.1, below the noise; no evidence that A matters.
  7. (Medium) Find the bug: "We tuned the learning rate on the test set and report test accuracy 92%." Answer: The test set was used for choices, so the number is optimistic. Tune on validation data and test once.
  8. (Hard) A paper claims +1.0 over baseline with one run each. You measure a run-to-run standard deviation of 0.6. How likely is a gap of at least 1.0 from luck alone? Answer: Spread of the difference 0.6 × √2 = 0.849; 1.0 / 0.849 = 1.18; the chance of exceeding is about 0.12 (one tail), so luck alone could do it about 1 time in 8.
  9. (Hard, interview) "Tell me about a paper you read recently." Answer: Use problem, idea, evidence, doubt, use: "Problem X; they do Y; they show Z on one dataset without variance; I doubted the baseline tuning; I reproduced a small version and saw about 1 point less; I would use it only with testing on our data."
  10. (Hard, interview) "Your offline metric improved 2% but the online A/B test shows no change. Why?" Answer: Possible reasons: the offline metric does not match the business goal, leakage in the offline set, a time or population shift, noise in either test, or too little power online. Check logs for feature skew and run the test longer.
