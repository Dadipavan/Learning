1.1 Counting and Sample Spaces

BASIC EXPLANATION
Counting methods find how many outcomes are possible, and a sample space is the set of all possible outcomes of an experiment. The probability of an event is the number of favourable outcomes divided by the total when all outcomes are equally likely.
INTUITION
Think of a restaurant menu: 3 starters, 4 mains and 2 desserts. How many different meals can you order? You do not list them all. You multiply: 3 × 4 × 2 = 24. Many probability questions are secretly counting questions. If every outcome is equally likely, then the probability of an event is (number of good outcomes) divided by (number of all outcomes). So the real work is counting without missing or double-counting. In a shop: 4 customer types, 3 regions and 5 parcel sizes give 4 × 3 × 5 = 60 delivery scenarios.

The rules

  • Axioms (the three basic laws): P(A) ≥ 0; P(S) = 1; if A and B cannot happen together, P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B).
  • Multiplication rule: if a task has steps with n₁, n₂, ... choices, the total is n₁ × n₂ × ...
  • Permutations: choosing k from n with order: n! / (n − k)!
  • Combinations: choosing k from n without order: C(n, k) = n! / (k! (n − k)!). We divide by k! because each group of k items was counted k! times in different orders.
  • Complement trick: P(at least one) = 1 − P(none). "None" is usually one simple product.

Problem 1.1.1 A company has 10 depots. You pick 3 at random (no repeats) to audit. How many different sets of 3 exist? How many ordered line-ups? What is the probability that one specific depot is in your set?

Formulasets = C(10, 3) = 10! / (3! · 7!); line-ups = 10 · 9 · 8
Plug inC(10, 3) = (10 · 9 · 8) / (3 · 2 · 1)
Compute720 / 6 = 120 sets. Line-ups = 720. Sets that contain your depot: choose the other 2 from the remaining 9, C(9, 2) = 36. Probability = 36 / 120 = 0.3
AnswerThere are 120 sets and 720 ordered line-ups. Your depot is chosen with probability 0.3.
CheckSimple logic: you pick 3 of 10 seats, so each depot has a 3/10 = 0.3 chance. Both ways agree. Also 720 / 120 = 6 = 3!, which matches "each set can be ordered 6 ways".
Why it mattersTwo methods giving the same answer is the habit of a good analyst.

Predict: what do the three numbers below print?

from math import comb, perm
print(comb(10, 3), perm(10, 3), comb(9, 2) / comb(10, 3))
120 720 0.3

Line by line: comb(10, 3) counts sets, perm(10, 3) counts ordered line-ups, and the last expression divides the favourable sets by all sets.

Problem 1.1.2 (the birthday problem) In a team of 23 people, what is the chance that at least two share a birthday? Assume 365 equally likely days.

FormulaP(match) = 1 − P(all different)
Plug inP(all different) = (365/365)(364/365)(363/365) ... (343/365). The first person has any day, the second must avoid 1 day, the third must avoid 2 days, and so on. There are 23 factors.
ComputeMultiply the 23 factors, then subtract from 1. We use code, because 23 multiplications by hand is slow.
AnswerAbout 0.507: a little more than 50 percent.
CheckWe simulate 100,000 random teams of 23 and count matches. The simulated fraction must be near the exact value.
Why it mattersHumans guess "about 6 percent" (23/365). The truth is nearly 50 percent, so your intuition for collisions is poor.

Predict: will the simulated number be exactly equal to the exact one?

import numpy as np
def p_match(n):
    p = 1.0
    for i in range(n):
        p *= (365 - i) / 365
    return 1 - p
print("exact n=23:", round(p_match(23), 4), " n=50:", round(p_match(50), 4))
rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
teams = rng.integers(0, 365, size=(100_000, 23))
hits = sum(len(set(row)) < 23 for row in teams)
print("simulated n=23:", hits / 100_000)
exact n=23: 0.5073  n=50: 0.9704
simulated n=23: 0.50695

The simulated value is close but not identical, because it is random. With 100,000 teams the error is about ±0.003. That is the first lesson about simulation: it gives an estimate with noise.

KEY INSIGHT
The number of pairs grows like n²/2. With 23 people there are C(23, 2) = 253 pairs, and each pair is a chance for a match. The same idea explains hash collisions: a hash table or ID generator with N slots starts colliding after about √N items, not after N items.

Connection to the real job

Collision probability decides how long a random ID must be (order IDs, tracking numbers, UUIDs). Counting also appears in A/B test design (how many variant combinations), and as the "n choose k" inside the binomial distribution in Chapter 2.

Applications

  • Data Engineering: choosing hash sizes and partition keys; estimating duplicate-key risk in a pipeline.
  • Quality testing: pairwise test-case reduction when the full cross-product is too big.
  • Machine Learning: counting feature crosses; sampling a train/test split without replacement.
  • Analytics: counting possible segment combinations before a "slice and dice" analysis (more slices means more false findings, Chapter 5).
COMMON MISTAKE
Mixing up "order matters" and "order does not matter". Quick test: if swapping two chosen items gives a different result (first and second prize), use permutations. If not (a committee), use combinations. Always try a tiny case like n = 3, k = 2 and list it by hand.

Check yourself

  1. How many 4-digit PINs exist if digits may repeat? Answer: 10⁴ = 10,000.
  2. How many ways to pick a team of 2 from 6 people? Answer: C(6,2) = 15.
  3. Why is "at least one" usually easier through the complement? Answer: "None" is one product of simple terms, while "at least one" has many cases.

1.2 Conditional Probability and Independence

BASIC EXPLANATION
Conditional probability P(A|B) is the probability of event A given that event B has happened. Two events are independent if knowing one does not change the probability of the other.
INTUITION
You are in a hotel lobby with 100 people. 40 are engineers. Now someone says "everyone who speaks Hindi, raise a hand", and you look only at the raised hands. Inside that smaller group, the fraction of engineers may be different. That is conditional probability: the chance of A inside the smaller world where B is true. New information shrinks the world, and probabilities must be recalculated inside it.

Words you need

WordPlain meaningExample
P(A \B)Probability of A, given B happened
Joint P(A and B)Both happenSlow AND uses database
PartitionGroups that do not overlap and cover everythingDepot 1, 2 or 3
IndependentKnowing one tells you nothing about the otherTwo fair coin flips

The rules

  • Definition: P(A | B) = P(A and B) / P(B), for P(B) > 0. In words: of all the B cases, what fraction also has A.
  • Multiplication rule: P(A and B) = P(A | B) · P(B).
  • Law of total probability: if B₁, B₂, ... partition the world, P(A) = Σ P(A | Bᵢ) · P(Bᵢ). It is a weighted average of the within-group probabilities.
  • Independence: P(A and B) = P(A) · P(B), equivalently P(A | B) = P(A).
  • Conditional independence: A and B may be dependent overall, but independent once we know C. The Naive Bayes classifier assumes this for all features.

Problem 1.2.1 A shop ships from three depots. Depot 1 handles 50 percent of orders and 2 percent of them are returned. Depot 2 handles 30 percent and 3 percent are returned. Depot 3 handles 20 percent and 5 percent are returned. What is the overall return rate?

FormulaP(fail) = Σ P(fail | Sᵢ) · P(Sᵢ) (law of total probability)
Plug in0.02·0.5 + 0.03·0.3 + 0.05·0.2
Compute0.010 + 0.009 + 0.010 = 0.029
AnswerThe overall return rate is 2.9 percent, which means about 29 returns per 1,000 orders.
CheckThe answer must lie between the smallest (2 percent) and the largest (5 percent) rate, because it is a weighted average. It does. A simple average (3.3 percent) would be wrong because it ignores traffic weights.
Why it matters"Combined rate = weighted average" is the rule behind every dashboard roll-up.

Problem 1.2.2 (two children) A family has two children. (a) You learn that at least one is a girl. What is the probability that both are girls? (b) You learn instead that the older child is a girl. What is the probability that both are girls?

FormulaP(both | info) = P(both and info) / P(info). Because outcomes are equally likely, count outcomes.
Plug inThe four equally likely outcomes (older first) are GG, GB, BG, BB.
Compute(a) "at least one girl" leaves GG, GB, BG, so P = 1/3. (b) "older is a girl" leaves GG, GB, so P = 1/2.
Answer(a) 1/3, (b) 1/2. The way the information arrives changes the answer.
CheckWe simulate a million families below.
Why it mattersBe careful about exactly what a condition says. Data filters such as "at least one error in the session" and "the first order was late" select different worlds.

Predict: roughly 0.33 and 0.50? Run it.

import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(2)
older = rng.integers(0, 2, 1_000_000)   # 1 = girl
younger = rng.integers(0, 2, 1_000_000)
atleast = (older == 1) | (younger == 1)
both = (older == 1) & (younger == 1)
print("P(both | at least one girl):", round(both[atleast].mean(), 4))
print("P(both | older is girl):   ", round(both[older == 1].mean(), 4))
P(both | at least one girl): 0.3344
P(both | older is girl):    0.5009

The line both[atleast].mean() keeps only families where the condition holds, then takes the fraction with both girls. This is exactly "shrink the world, then count".

The picture

A probability tree: each branch carries a probability, and you multiply along a path.

            0.5 -- S1 -- fail 0.02 -> 0.5 x 0.02 = 0.010

Read it left to right. The first split chooses a depot. The second split (not drawn for "pass") chooses fail or pass. Add up the fail endings to get the total.

Connection to the real job

Every funnel metric is a conditional probability. In reliability work, P(late delivery | peak hour) differs from P(late delivery). In ML, a classifier output is an estimate of P(class | features).

Applications

  • Data Analytics: funnel conversion, churn given a segment.
  • Data Science: Naive Bayes spam filters (conditional independence of words).
  • Reliability / SRE: fault trees, failure rates per component.
  • Healthcare analytics: probability of disease given a symptom.
COMMON MISTAKE
Treating P(A | B) as if it equals P(B | A). "Most marathon runners are healthy" does not mean "most healthy people run marathons". This is the prosecutor's fallacy, and the next section shows its cost.

Check yourself

  1. P(A)=0.4, P(B)=0.5, independent. What is P(A and B)? Answer: 0.2.
  2. P(A and B)=0.1, P(B)=0.25. What is P(A | B)? Answer: 0.4.
  3. Can two events with non-zero probability be independent and mutually exclusive at once? Answer: No. If they cannot happen together, knowing one occurred tells you the other did not, so they are dependent.

1.3 Bayes' Theorem and the Base-Rate Trap

BASIC EXPLANATION
Bayes' theorem updates the probability of a hypothesis after seeing evidence: P(A|B) = P(B|A) P(A) / P(B). The base rate is the prior probability of the hypothesis, and ignoring it leads to the base-rate fallacy.
INTUITION
A smoke alarm rings for 99 percent of real fires. It also rings when you make toast. If real fires are very rare and toast is common, then most rings are toast. To judge a ring you must ask two questions: how good is the alarm, and how common is a real fire in the first place? The second question is the base rate. Ignoring it is called base-rate neglect, and it is the most expensive probability mistake in data work. Bayes' theorem is the machine that combines both questions into one correct answer.

Words you need

WordPlain meaningExample
Prior P(H)Belief before seeing evidence (the base rate)0.5 percent of minutes have a real incident
Likelihood P(E \H)How likely the evidence is if H is true
Posterior P(H \E)Belief after seeing the evidence
SensitivityP(positive \condition present) (also recall)
SpecificityP(negative \condition absent)
False positive rate1 − specificity0.02
Oddsp / (1 − p)0.2 means 1 to 4

The theorem

P(H | E) = P(E | H) · P(H) / P(E), where P(E) = P(E | H)·P(H) + P(E | not H)·P(not H).

The odds version is easiest for repeated updates: posterior odds = prior odds × likelihood ratio, where the likelihood ratio is sensitivity / false positive rate.

Problem 1.3.1 An anomaly detector flags 99 percent of real incidents (sensitivity 0.99) and wrongly flags 2 percent of normal minutes (false positive rate 0.02). Real incidents occur in 0.5 percent of minutes. An alert fires. What is the probability that it is a real incident?

FormulaP(H | E) = P(E | H)·P(H) / [P(E | H)·P(H) + P(E | not H)·P(not H)]
Plug in(0.99 · 0.005) / (0.99 · 0.005 + 0.02 · 0.995)
Computetop = 0.00495. Bottom = 0.00495 + 0.0199 = 0.02485. Ratio = 0.00495 / 0.02485 = 0.1992.
AnswerOnly about 19.9 percent of alerts are real incidents. About 4 of every 5 alerts are false alarms, although the detector looks excellent on paper.
CheckNatural frequencies. Take 100,000 minutes. 500 are incidents and the detector fires on 495 of them. 99,500 are normal and the detector fires on 1,990 of them. Real alerts = 495 out of 2,485 total alerts = 19.9 percent. Same answer.
Why it mattersIf the on-call team learns that 80 percent of pages are noise, they stop trusting the system. Detector quality means nothing without the base rate.

Predict: what happens to the posterior if incidents become 10 times rarer (prior 0.0005)? Higher or lower than 0.199?

def posterior(prior, sens, fpr):
    pe = sens * prior + fpr * (1 - prior)
    return sens * prior / pe
print("posterior:", round(posterior(0.005, 0.99, 0.02), 4))
for prior in [0.0005, 0.005, 0.05, 0.2, 0.5]:
    print(f"prior={prior:<7} posterior={posterior(prior, 0.99, 0.02):.4f}")
posterior: 0.1992
prior=0.0005  posterior=0.0242
prior=0.005   posterior=0.1992
prior=0.05    posterior=0.7226
prior=0.2     posterior=0.9252
prior=0.5     posterior=0.9802

The function computes the denominator pe (the total chance of an alert) first, then the share of it that comes from real incidents. The table shows the key lesson: with the same detector, precision runs from 2.4 percent to 98 percent depending only on the prior.

Problem 1.3.2 (two independent alerts) Two independent monitors both fire. Each has sensitivity 0.99 and false positive rate 0.02. With prior 0.005, what is the posterior after both alert?

Formulaposterior odds = prior odds × LR₁ × LR₂ (independent evidence multiplies)
Plug inprior odds = 0.005 / 0.995 = 0.005025. LR = 0.99 / 0.02 = 49.5.
Computeodds = 0.005025 × 49.5 × 49.5 = 0.005025 × 2450.25 = 12.31. Probability = odds / (1 + odds) = 12.31 / 13.31 = 0.925.
AnswerAbout 92.5 percent. A second independent signal moves belief from 20 percent to over 90 percent.
CheckUpdating one at a time must give the same: after the first alert odds = 0.005025 × 49.5 = 0.2487 (probability 0.199, as before). Multiply by 49.5 again: 12.31. Same.
Why it mattersThis is why alert rules like "two of three checks fail" cut noise so strongly. The word independent is critical: if both checkers look at the same faulty scanner, the second one adds almost nothing.
prior_odds = 0.005 / 0.995
lr = 0.99 / 0.02
odds = prior_odds * lr**2
print("posterior after two alerts:", round(odds / (1 + odds), 4))
posterior after two alerts: 0.9249

Problem 1.3.3 (Monty Hall) You pick door 1 of 3. The host, who knows where the car is, opens door 3 (a goat). Should you switch to door 2?

FormulaP(car behind door k) before any door opens = 1/3 each.
Plug inYour door has 1/3. The other two doors together have 2/3.
ComputeThe host never opens the car door and never opens yours. Opening door 3 does not change the chance that your door is right (still 1/3), so the whole 2/3 now sits on door 2.
AnswerSwitching wins with probability 2/3. Staying wins with 1/3.
CheckIn the simulation a switcher wins exactly when the first pick was wrong, because the host then removes the only other goat.
Why it mattersThe host's action is evidence, and its meaning depends on his rule. Always ask "how was this evidence produced?" before updating.
import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(3)
n = 200_000
car = rng.integers(0, 3, n)
pick = np.zeros(n, dtype=int)            # always pick door 0
stay = (car == pick).mean()
switch = (car != pick).mean()
print("stay:", round(stay, 3), " switch:", round(switch, 3))
stay: 0.334  switch: 0.666

The picture: natural frequencies

Read it as a funnel from top to bottom.

100,000 minutes
  500 real incidents        -> 495 alerts (99%)
  99,500 normal minutes     -> 1,990 alerts (2%)
Total alerts = 2,485;  real = 495  -> 495 / 2,485 = 19.9%

The 1,990 false alerts come from the huge normal group, even though only 2 percent of it fires. Small rate times huge group still gives a big number.

Connection to the real job

Precision of a classifier is exactly P(positive class | predicted positive), and it depends on prevalence. The naive Bayes classifier multiplies likelihoods under conditional independence. Fraud teams with a 0.1 percent fraud rate face this trap every day.

Applications

  • Data Analytics: judging whether a dashboard anomaly flag is trustworthy.
  • Machine Learning: precision, recall and prevalence (Math PDF, confusion matrix); Naive Bayes.
  • MLOps / SRE: alert tuning, drift detectors with false alarms.
  • GenAI: content-safety filters where the unsafe class is rare.
COMMON MISTAKE
Quoting only sensitivity ("our model catches 99 percent of fraud") without the false positive rate and base rate. Always ask: what is the prevalence, and what is the precision?
INTERVIEW TIP
When given a Bayes question, say "I will use natural frequencies" and draw 100,000 cases. Interviewers like it because it shows you understand instead of memorising.

Check yourself

  1. Prior 1 percent, sensitivity 90 percent, false positive rate 9 percent. Rough posterior? Answer: 0.009 / (0.009 + 0.0891) is about 9.2 percent.
  2. What does the likelihood ratio 49.5 mean? Answer: A positive result is 49.5 times more likely under "incident" than under "normal".
  3. If the base rate rises, does precision rise or fall, with sensitivity and false positive rate fixed? Answer: It rises.

1.4 Random Variables, Expectation and Variance

BASIC EXPLANATION
A random variable is a numerical outcome of a random process. Its expectation is the probability-weighted average value, and its variance measures how far the values typically spread from the mean.
INTUITION
A random variable is a number produced by a random process, like the delivery time of the next order. You cannot predict one value, but you can describe the pattern. The expectation is the balance point (the long-run average) and the variance says how widely the values scatter. Expectation has a superpower called linearity: the average of a sum is the sum of the averages, always, with no conditions. It turns hard-looking counting questions into one-line answers.

Words you need (see Math PDF Pillar 3.2 for fuller definitions)

WordPlain meaningExample
Random variable XA number from a random processDelivery time of the next order
Expectation E[X]Probability-weighted average250 minutes
Variance Var(X)Average squared distance from E[X]70,500 min²
Standard deviation√Var, same unit as X265 minutes
CovarianceDo two variables move together?Two services slow at the same time
Indicator variable1 if something happens, else 01 if call i fails

The rules

  • E[X] = Σ x·P(X = x); E[aX + b] = a·E[X] + b; E[X + Y] = E[X] + E[Y] always.
  • Var(X) = E[X²] − (E[X])²; Var(aX + b) = a²·Var(X).
  • Var(X + Y) = Var(X) + Var(Y) + 2·Cov(X, Y); the covariance term is zero for independent variables.
  • Cov(X, Y) = E[XY] − E[X]·E[Y] and Corr(X, Y) = Cov / (σₓ · σᵧ).

Problem 1.4.1 A shop offers 3 delivery options. Delivery takes 100 minutes with probability 0.6, 300 minutes with probability 0.3 and 1000 minutes with probability 0.1. Find the mean, the variance and the standard deviation.

FormulaE[X] = Σ x·p; Var = E[X²] − E[X]²
Plug inE[X] = 100·0.6 + 300·0.3 + 1000·0.1; E[X²] = 100²·0.6 + 300²·0.3 + 1000²·0.1
ComputeE[X] = 60 + 90 + 100 = 250. E[X²] = 6,000 + 27,000 + 100,000 = 133,000. Var = 133,000 − 250² = 133,000 − 62,500 = 70,500. SD = √70,500 = 265.5.
AnswerThe mean is 250 minutes and the standard deviation is about 265.5 minutes. The SD is larger than the mean: the 10 percent slow tail dominates the spread.
CheckThe mean (250) lies between the smallest (100) and the largest (1000) value, and the probabilities add to 0.6 + 0.3 + 0.1 = 1. The code below confirms.
Why it mattersA mean of 250 minutes hides that 1 order in 10 takes 1000 minutes. This is why percentiles matter in delivery reports.

Problem 1.4.2 (linearity of expectation) A parcel of 20 independent items is packed. Each item is damaged with probability 0.03. (a) Find the expected number of failed calls and its variance. (b) Ten people drop their hats in a box and each takes one hat back at random. What is the expected number of people who get their own hat?

FormulaWrite the count as a sum of indicators: Total = I₁ + ... + Iₙ with E[Iᵢ] = P(event i).
Plug in(a) E[Iᵢ] = 0.03 for i = 1..20. (b) Person i gets own hat with probability 1/10.
Compute(a) E = 20 × 0.03 = 0.6. Calls are independent, so Var = 20 × 0.03 × 0.97 = 0.582. (b) E = 10 × (1/10) = 1. The hat events are dependent, but linearity does not need independence.
AnswerExpect 0.6 failed calls per page view (variance 0.582). In the hat puzzle the expected number of lucky people is exactly 1, for any number of people.
CheckSimulation below: mean near 0.6, variance near 0.582, hats near 1.
Why it mattersOver 1,000,000 page views you should expect about 600,000 failed calls. Also, P(at least one failure on a page) = 1 − 0.97²⁰ = 0.456, so a small 3 percent per-call failure rate makes almost half of all page loads show at least one failure. Small per-call rates multiply across many calls.
import numpy as np
v = np.array([100, 300, 1000]); p = np.array([0.6, 0.3, 0.1])
m = (v * p).sum(); var = (v**2 * p).sum() - m**2
print("mean", m, "var", var, "sd", round(var**0.5, 2))
rng = np.random.default_rng(4)
fails = rng.binomial(1, 0.03, size=(500_000, 20)).sum(axis=1)
print("fails mean/var:", round(fails.mean(), 3), round(fails.var(), 3))
print("P(at least one failure):", round(1 - 0.97**20, 4), " simulated:", round((fails > 0).mean(), 4))
n_people = 10
matches = [(rng.permutation(n_people) == np.arange(n_people)).sum() for _ in range(100_000)]
print("expected own hats:", round(np.mean(matches), 3))

Problem 1.4.3 (correlated sum) Two legs of a delivery (warehouse to hub, hub to door) have time variances 400 and 900 (min²) and covariance 300. What is the standard deviation of their total time? Compare with the case of independence.

FormulaVar(X+Y) = Var(X) + Var(Y) + 2·Cov(X, Y)
Plug in400 + 900 + 2 × 300
ComputeVar = 1,900, so SD = √1,900 = 43.6 minutes. If independent: Var = 1,300, SD = 36.1 minutes.
AnswerThe total has SD 43.6 minutes, which is about 21 percent more than the independent value of 36.1 minutes.
CheckThe correlation is 300 / (20 · 30) = 0.5. The rule is only valid if the covariance matrix is possible: |Cov| ≤ σₓσᵧ = 600. Yes.
Why it mattersPositive correlation (both slow when the weather is bad) makes the total more variable, so the tail of delivery times is worse than a model with independence predicts.

The picture

Independent:  spread of X+Y = sqrt(400+900)     = 36.1
Correlated:   spread of X+Y = sqrt(400+900+600) = 43.6

Think of two people walking together. If they step randomly, their combined position wobbles a little. If they step in unison, it wobbles more.

Connection to the real job

Indicator variables plus linearity give expected counts for error budgets. The variance-of-sum rule explains why a multi-leg delivery's time is more variable than a naive calculation, and why diversification works in finance.

Applications

  • Operations: service-promise error budgets, staffing plans with expected counts.
  • Finance analytics: portfolio variance.
  • Machine Learning: variance of averaged models (bagging) depends on their correlation.
  • Data Engineering: expected duplicates or failed records in a batch.
COMMON MISTAKE
Adding standard deviations. Variances add (for independent variables), standard deviations do not. For independent SD = 3 and SD = 4, the SD of the sum is 5, not 7.

Check yourself

  1. E[X] = 5. What is E[3X − 2]? Answer: 13.
  2. Var(X) = 4. What is Var(3X − 2)? Answer: 36.
  3. Does E[X + Y] = E[X] + E[Y] need independence? Answer: No. Only the variance rule needs it (or a covariance term).

1.5 Descriptive Statistics and Correlation Done Properly

BASIC EXPLANATION
Descriptive statistics summarise data with measures such as the mean, median, standard deviation and quantiles. Correlation measures the strength and direction of a linear relationship between two variables.
INTUITION
Before any model, you summarise the data with a few numbers. But each number is like one photo of a building: it hides the other sides. The mean hides the tail, and a correlation hides the shape. Think of delivery times: an average of 200 minutes can hide a 99th percentile of 5 hours. A summary is good only if you know what it hides.

Words you need

WordPlain meaningExample
NominalLabels, no orderBrowser name

RatioEqual gaps and a true zeroDelivery time, weight

IQRQ3 − Q1, the middle 50 percentRobust spread
Skewness0 = symmetric, positive = long right tailDelivery time
Excess kurtosis0 for a normal, large for heavy tailsBad-sensor spikes
MonotonicAlways rising (or always falling)y = x³ for x > 0

The data type decides what you may compute: average ratio data, take the median of ordinal data, and only count nominal data.

Correlation family

  • Pearson r: strength of a straight-line relationship. Sensitive to outliers.
  • Spearman ρ: Pearson computed on the ranks (1st, 2nd, 3rd smallest). Detects any monotonic relationship.
  • Kendall τ: counts concordant pairs (both go up together) minus discordant pairs, divided by all pairs. Robust, good with ties and small samples.

Problem 1.5.2 Compare Pearson, Spearman and Kendall for (a) y = x³ with x = 1..20 and (b) the straight line y = 2x with one wild outlier replacing the last value by −200.

FormulaSpearman = Pearson of ranks. For (a) the ranks of y equal the ranks of x.

CheckIn (b), 19 of 20 points lie on a perfect rising line, so a sensible measure must be clearly positive. Only the rank-based ones are.

Connection to the real job

EDA (exploratory data analysis) starts here. Report median and percentiles for delivery times, never only the mean. Use Spearman for feature screening when relations are curved. Skewed targets often need a log transform before regression (Chapter 6).

Applications

  • Data Analytics: delivery-time and on-time-rate reports, KPI distributions.
  • Data Science: feature screening, outlier detection.
  • Data Engineering: data-quality checks (a kurtosis spike reveals a faulty sensor).
  • ML: choosing between mean and median imputation.
COMMON MISTAKE
Concluding "no relationship" from r near 0. A perfect U-shape y = x² on a symmetric range has Pearson r of 0 yet y is fully determined by x. Always plot the data.

Check yourself

  2. Which correlation gives exactly 1 for y = exp(x)? Answer: Spearman and Kendall (not Pearson).
  3. Is a 1-5 satisfaction score ratio data? Answer: No, it is ordinal.

1.6 Simpson's Paradox and Why Correlation Is Not Causation

BASIC EXPLANATION
Simpson's paradox is when a trend that appears in several groups reverses or disappears when the groups are combined. It shows that correlation does not imply causation, because a hidden third variable can create or reverse an association.
INTUITION
Suppose Depot B looks faster than Depot A in your overall results. You are about to ship B. Then you notice that B was mostly tested on a quiet network and A mostly on a busy one. In each network condition, A is actually better. The overall comparison was unfair because the two builds faced different conditions. A trend that holds in every subgroup can flip when the groups are merged. This is Simpson's paradox.

Words you need

WordPlain meaningExample
ConfounderA hidden variable linked to both the "treatment" and the outcomeTraffic level
StratifyCompare inside each group separatelyCompare A and B only under heavy traffic
AdjustInclude the confounder in a modelRegression (Chapter 6)
RandomiseAssign treatments by coin flip so confounders balance outA/B test (Chapter 12)

Problem 1.6.1 Two depots were compared on a light day and a busy day. The numbers are parcels delivered within the promised time.

Depot A81 / 87 pass (93.1%)192 / 263 pass (73.0%)
Depot B234 / 270 pass (86.7%)55 / 80 pass (68.8%)
  • Formula pass rate = passed / total; combined rate = (sum of passed) / (sum of total)
  • Plug in A: (81 + 192) / (87 + 263). B: (234 + 55) / (270 + 80).
  • Compute A: 273 / 350 = 78.0%. B: 289 / 350 = 82.6%. Inside light traffic A wins (93.1 vs 86.7). Inside heavy traffic A wins again (73.0 vs 68.8).
  • Answer A is better in both conditions, but B looks better in the combined table, because 270 of B's 350 tests ran under easy light traffic while 263 of A's 350 ran under hard heavy traffic.
  • Check Compute A's weights: 87/350 = 25 percent light, 75 percent heavy. B's weights: 77 percent light, 23 percent heavy. Different mixes explain the flip. Verify with code below.
  • Why it matters The fair comparison is within the traffic level (or after adjusting for it). Looking only at the dashboard total would ship the worse build.

This is the daily danger in observational analysis: dashboards compare groups that differ in many ways. The fixes are stratification (compare within groups), regression that includes the confounder (Chapter 6) and randomised experiments (Chapter 12).

Applications

  • Operations: comparing depots, regions, or suppliers under unequal volumes.
  • Data Analytics: conversion rates by device, campaign or country.
  • Data Science: feature importance when features are correlated with a hidden driver.
  • Healthcare and policy: treatment comparisons where sicker patients get stronger treatments.
COMMON MISTAKE
Believing that "more data" fixes this. The paradox comes from structure, not sample size. You need a causal story (what influences what) to decide whether to aggregate or to split.

Check yourself

  3. Name two fixes. Answer: Stratify, adjust in a regression, or randomise.

Chapter recap

  • Many probability questions reduce to counting; the complement trick makes "at least one" easy.
  • The birthday problem shows collisions appear after about √N items.
  • Conditional probability is "probability inside a smaller world"; P(A | B) is not P(B | A).
  • Bayes' theorem needs the prior (base rate); a great detector can still give mostly false alarms when the event is rare.
  • Natural frequencies (100,000 cases) make Bayes easy to explain and check.
  • Expectation is linear even without independence; variance adds only with independence or with a covariance term.
  • Report median, MAD, IQR and percentiles for skewed data like delivery times; mean and SD are fragile.
  • Pearson measures straight-line links; Spearman and Kendall measure monotonic links; always plot.
  • Simpson's paradox can reverse a trend; confounders must be handled by design or adjustment.

Problem 1.7.4 P(A) = 0.3, P(B) = 0.6, P(A or B) = 0.72. Are A and B independent?

Answer: P(A and B) = 0.3 + 0.6 − 0.72 = 0.18 = 0.3 × 0.6. Yes, independent.

Problem 1.7.5 A disease affects 1 in 1,000 people. A test has 95 percent sensitivity and a 5 percent false positive rate. What is the posterior after a positive test?

Answer: 0.95·0.001 / (0.95·0.001 + 0.05·0.999) = 0.00095 / 0.05090 = 1.87 percent.

Problem 1.7.6 Find the bug: "Three depots have return rates 1%, 4% and 10%. The average is 5%, so the combined error rate is 5%."

Answer: The depots handle different volumes. The combined rate must be weighted by order counts (law of total probability).

Problem 1.7.7 X has mean 10 and SD 2; Y has mean 4 and SD 1; their correlation is 0.5. Find the SD of X − Y.

Answer: Var = 4 + 1 − 2·(0.5·2·1) = 5 − 2 = 3. SD = √3 = 1.73.

Problem 1.7.8 A coin is flipped 10 times. What is the expected number of positions where heads is followed by heads?

Answer: There are 9 adjacent pairs, each HH with probability 1/4. By linearity, 9/4 = 2.25.

Problem 1.7.9 (interview) "A fraud test is 90 percent accurate and it says 'fraud' for a transaction. Is the transaction 90 percent likely to be fraud?" Explain.

Answer: No. Accuracy is not the posterior. You need the base rate. If fraud is 0.1 percent and the test has 90 percent sensitivity and a 10 percent false positive rate, the posterior is 0.0009 / 0.1008 = 0.89 percent.

Problem 1.7.10 (interview) In a team of 10 people, what is the probability that at least two share a birth month (12 equally likely months)?

Answer: P(all different) = (12·11·10·...·3) / 12¹⁰ = 239,500,800 / 61,917,364,224 = 0.00387. So the probability of a match is 1 − 0.00387 = 99.6 percent.
