A product manager runs 40 A/B tests this quarter. Three come back "significant at 5 percent" and the team celebrates. A data scientist who knows this chapter asks three questions: how big are the effects, how many tests were run, and how many of those three would we expect by luck alone? (Answer to the last: about 2.) This chapter teaches you to measure the size of an effect, to correct for looking many times, and to recognise the ways data tricks honest people: peeking at results, slicing into subgroups, picking winners, and regression to the mean. These skills separate analysts whose findings hold up from those whose findings disappear when repeated. You will build each correction by hand so you understand what it does, then check it against a library.

The running example continues from Chapter 4: delivery-time comparisons between two depots and A/B tests on a web shop.

5.1 Effect Sizes: How Big Is the Difference?

BASIC EXPLANATION
An effect size measures the magnitude of a difference or relationship, such as Cohen's d or the correlation. It shows how large an effect is, which a p-value alone does not.
INTUITION
A p-value is like a smoke detector: it tells you whether there is something there, not how big the fire is. With enough data, even a 0.5 minute difference becomes "highly significant". An effect size is the measuring tape: it tells you how much the groups differ, in units you can judge. Always report both: the p-value says "is it real?", the effect size says "does it matter?".

Words you need

WordPlain meaningExample

The formulas

  • d = (x̄₁ − x̄₂) / s_p, pooled SD s_p = √[((n₁−1)s₁² + (n₂−1)s₂²) / (n₁ + n₂ − 2)].
  • Hedges' g = d × (1 − 3 / (4(n₁ + n₂) − 9)).
  • SE of d ≈ √[(n₁+n₂)/(n₁n₂) + d²/(2(n₁+n₂))], so an approximate 95 percent CI is d ± 1.96 SE.
  • Probability of superiority for normal data = Φ(d/√2), where Φ is the normal CDF.
  • Odds ratio CI: compute on the log scale, ln OR ± 1.96 √(1/a + 1/b + 1/c + 1/d) using the four counts of the 2×2 table, then exponentiate.
  • The guide "0.2 small, 0.5 medium, 0.8 large" is a rough convention. In your field the right yardstick is the business or engineering consequence.

Problem 5.1.1 (Cohen's d with a confidence interval) Use the Depot A/Depot B summary from Chapter 4: A has n = 40, mean 250, SD 60; B has n = 45, mean 225, SD 50. Compute d, Hedges' g, a 95 percent interval for d, and the probability of superiority.

Formulas_p = √[((n₁−1)s₁² + (n₂−1)s₂²)/(n₁+n₂−2)]; d = difference / s_p
Plug ins_p = √[(39 × 3600 + 44 × 2500) / 83] = √[(140,400 + 110,000) / 83]
Computes_p = √3,017.1 = 54.93. d = 25 / 54.93 = 0.455. Correction factor 1 − 3/(4 × 85 − 9) = 1 − 3/331 = 0.991, so g = 0.451. SE(d) = √(85/1,800 + 0.455²/170) = √(0.04722 + 0.00122) = 0.220, so the interval is 0.455 ± 0.431 = (0.024, 0.886). Probability of superiority = Φ(0.455/1.414) = Φ(0.322) = 0.626.
AnswerThe effect is medium (d = 0.45), but uncertain: it could be anywhere from nearly nothing (0.02) to large (0.89). A random delivery from B beats a random delivery from A in about 63 percent of pairs.
CheckThe p-value from Chapter 4 (0.042) is just below 0.05, and the interval for d just excludes zero. Both tell the same story, as they must.
Why it matters"Significant" here hides a wide range of possible effects. A stakeholder needs to see "somewhere between trivial and large".
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

se = np.sqrt((n1 + n2) / (n1 * n2) + d**2 / (2 * (n1 + n2)))

print("95%% CI for d: (%.3f, %.3f)" % (d - 1.96 * se, d + 1.96 * se))

Problem 5.1.2 (statistically significant but tiny) A giant experiment has 2,000,000 orders per arm. Mean delivery time differs by 0.5 minutes (SD 100 minutes). Compute the z statistic, the p-value and d. Is this worth a change of process?

Formulaz = difference / √(2σ²/n); d = difference / σ
Plug inSE = 100 × √(2 / 2,000,000) = 100 × 0.001 = 0.1
Computez = 0.5 / 0.1 = 5.0. Two-sided p = 5.7 × 10⁻⁷. d = 0.5 / 100 = 0.005.
AnswerThe result is extremely significant (z = 5, p ≈ 6 × 10⁻⁷) yet d = 0.005 is about 40 times smaller than the "small" benchmark 0.2. A half-minute gain on a 200-minute delivery is invisible to customers.
CheckDoubling the data raises z by √2 but leaves d unchanged. That is the key difference between significance and size.
Why it mattersIn big-data settings everything is significant. Always decide beforehand what effect is large enough to matter (the MDE from Chapter 4).

Problem 5.1.3 (risk difference, relative risk, odds ratio) Variant A: 500 of 10,000 convert. Variant B: 600 of 10,000 convert. Compute the risk difference, the relative risk, the odds ratio and a 95 percent interval for the odds ratio.

FormulaRD = p_B − p_A; RR = p_B / p_A; OR = [p_B/(1−p_B)] / [p_A/(1−p_A)]
Plug inp_A = 0.05, p_B = 0.06. Odds_A = 0.05/0.95 = 0.05263; odds_B = 0.06/0.94 = 0.06383.
ComputeRD = 0.01; RR = 1.20; OR = 0.06383 / 0.05263 = 1.2128. SE(ln OR) = √(1/600 + 1/9,400 + 1/500 + 1/9,500) = √(0.001667 + 0.000106 + 0.002 + 0.000105) = 0.0623. Interval: exp(ln 1.2128 ± 1.96 × 0.0623) = (1.073, 1.370).
AnswerB converts 1 percentage point more (a 20 percent relative lift). The odds ratio is 1.21, with 95 percent interval 1.07 to 1.37, which excludes 1.
CheckWhen the event is rare (5 percent), the OR (1.21) is close to the RR (1.20). For common events the OR exaggerates the RR.

a, b, c, dd = 600, 9400, 500, 9500     # B convert, B not, A convert, A not

Connection to the real job

Every experiment readout should have: the effect size in business units (minutes, percentage points, revenue per customer), its confidence interval and the p-value. Regression coefficients in Chapter 6 are effect sizes too (minutes per extra 100 orders waiting). In machine learning, "model A is 0.3 percent more accurate" needs an interval to be meaningful.

Applications

  • Data Analytics: experiment readouts, KPI change reports.
  • Data Science: feature importance, uplift modelling.
  • Machine Learning: differences in AUC or F1 with intervals.
  • Operations: deciding whether a change in delivery time is large enough to justify changing a process.
COMMON MISTAKE
Reporting only "p < 0.05". The reader cannot tell if the gain is 0.5 minutes or 50 minutes. Also, do not apply "d = 0.2 is small" blindly. A d of 0.05 on revenue for millions of users may be worth a lot, while d = 0.8 on a vanity metric is worthless.

Check yourself

  1. Means 105 and 100, pooled SD 10. What is Cohen's d? Answer: 0.5.
  2. When does the odds ratio differ a lot from the relative risk? Answer: When the event is common.
  3. Does a larger sample change Cohen's d? Answer: Not on average; it only makes the estimate of d more precise.

5.2 Multiple Testing: Bonferroni, Holm and Benjamini-Hochberg

BASIC EXPLANATION
Multiple testing is running many hypothesis tests at once, which raises the chance of at least one false positive. Bonferroni and Holm control the family-wise error rate, and Benjamini-Hochberg controls the false discovery rate.
INTUITION
If you buy one lottery ticket, winning is unlikely. If you buy 20 tickets, a win somewhere is likely. Every test at α = 0.05 is a ticket in the "false discovery lottery". The more tests you run, the more likely at least one is a false alarm. Corrections for multiple testing make each ticket harder to win so that the group of tests stays honest. Two goals are common: control the chance of any false alarm (strict, called FWER) or control the share of false alarms among your discoveries (looser, called FDR).

Words you need

WordPlain meaningExample
Family of testsThe set of tests that you interpret together40 A/B metrics
FWER (family-wise error rate)P(at least one false positive in the family)Without correction, 1 − 0.95⁴⁰ = 87 percent for 40 tests
FDR (false discovery rate)Expected share of false positives among the discoveriesAt most 5 percent of the flagged metrics
Adjusted p-valueThe p-value you compare to α after correction
q-valueAdjusted p-value for FDR

The three methods (sort the p-values p₍₁₎ ≤ p₍₂₎ ≤ ... ≤ p₍ₘ₎)

  • Bonferroni: reject if p ≤ α / m. Simple, controls FWER, but loses power when m is large.
  • Holm (step-down): compare the smallest p to α/m, the next to α/(m−1), and so on, stopping at the first failure. Controls FWER and is never worse than Bonferroni, so always prefer it to plain Bonferroni.
  • Benjamini-Hochberg (BH): find the largest k with p₍ₖ₎ ≤ (k / m) · q. Reject hypotheses 1..k. It controls FDR at q (for independent or positively dependent tests), and finds many more discoveries than Bonferroni.
  • When to use which: confirmatory tests with serious consequences (clinical, safety): Bonferroni or Holm. Exploratory screening of many features, genes or metrics: BH.

Problem 5.2.1 (by hand, five tests) Five p-values: 0.001, 0.008, 0.020, 0.041, 0.300. With α = 0.05, which are rejected under Bonferroni, Holm and BH?

FormulaBonferroni: p ≤ 0.05/5 = 0.01. Holm thresholds: 0.05/5, 0.05/4, 0.05/3, 0.05/2, 0.05/1. BH thresholds: (k/5) × 0.05.
Plug inHolm thresholds 0.0100, 0.0125, 0.0167, 0.0250, 0.0500. BH thresholds 0.010, 0.020, 0.030, 0.040, 0.050.
ComputeBonferroni: 0.001 and 0.008 pass (≤ 0.01); 0.020 fails. So 2 rejections. Holm: 0.001 ≤ 0.0100 yes; 0.008 ≤ 0.0125 yes; 0.020 ≤ 0.0167 no, stop. So 2 rejections. BH: 0.001 ≤ 0.010 yes; 0.008 ≤ 0.020 yes; 0.020 ≤ 0.030 yes; 0.041 ≤ 0.040 no; 0.300 ≤ 0.050 no. The largest passing k is 3, so 3 rejections.
AnswerBonferroni and Holm reject the first two hypotheses; BH rejects the first three. Adjusted p-values: Bonferroni 0.005, 0.040, 0.100, 0.205, 1.0; Holm 0.005, 0.032, 0.060, 0.082, 0.300; BH 0.005, 0.020, 0.033, 0.051, 0.300.
CheckOur hand-built functions are compared with scipy.stats.false_discovery_control (the BH adjustment) in the code.
Why it mattersThe third test (p = 0.020) is a "discovery" under FDR control but not under FWER control. Which rule you pick depends on the cost of a false alarm.
def bonferroni(p):
    p = np.asarray(p); return np.minimum(p * len(p), 1)
def holm(p):
    p = np.asarray(p); m = len(p); o = np.argsort(p)
    run = 0; out = np.empty(m)
    for rank, i in enumerate(o):
        run = max(run, (m - rank) * p[i]); out[i] = min(run, 1)
    return out
def bh(p):
    p = np.asarray(p); m = len(p); o = np.argsort(p)
    adj = p[o] * m / (np.arange(m) + 1)
    adj = np.minimum.accumulate(adj[::-1])[::-1]
    out = np.empty(m); out[o] = np.minimum(adj, 1); return out
p = [0.001, 0.008, 0.020, 0.041, 0.300]
print("Bonferroni:", np.round(bonferroni(p), 3))
print("Holm      :", np.round(holm(p), 3))
print("BH        :", np.round(bh(p), 4))
print("scipy BH  :", np.round(stats.false_discovery_control(p), 4))
print("rejected at 0.05 -> Bonf", (bonferroni(p) <= .05).sum(), " Holm", (holm(p) <= .05).sum(), " BH", (bh(p) <= .05).sum())
Bonferroni: [0.005 0.04  0.1   0.205 1.   ]
Holm      : [0.005 0.032 0.06  0.082 0.3  ]

Problem 5.2.2 (simulation: 1,000 metrics, 100 real effects) Suppose 1,000 metrics are tested with a two-sample t-test (n = 40 per group). For 100 of them there is a real effect of d = 0.8; for 900 there is none. Compare four approaches: no correction, Bonferroni, Holm and BH (q = 0.05). Count true discoveries, false discoveries and the realised false discovery proportion.

Formulafalse discovery proportion = false discoveries / all discoveries
Plug inm = 1,000, true effects 100, α = q = 0.05
ComputeThe code simulates the data, runs 1,000 t-tests and applies each rule.
AnswerWithout correction there are 150 discoveries, 94 true and 56 false, so 37 percent of the discoveries are false alarms. Bonferroni and Holm give the same result: 29 discoveries, none false, but 71 of the 100 real effects are missed (power 0.29). BH finds 79 discoveries, 76 true and 3 false (false discovery proportion 3.8 percent, below the 5 percent target) and detects 76 percent of the real effects.
CheckUncorrected false alarms should be near 900 × 0.05 = 45; we got 56, a bit high by chance (the SD of that count is about 6.5). Also the uncorrected rule detects 94 percent of real effects, which is the power of a t-test with d = 0.8 and n = 40.
Why it matters"Correct or not" is a trade-off between false alarms and misses. FDR control is the practical choice when you scan many things and will follow up on the shortlist.
rng = np.random.default_rng(40)
m, k, n = 1000, 100, 40
true = np.zeros(m, bool); true[:k] = True
a = rng.normal(0, 1, (m, n)); b = rng.normal(0, 1, (m, n)) + np.where(true, 0.8, 0.0)[:, None]
pv = stats.ttest_ind(a, b, axis=1).pvalue
rules = {"no correction": pv <= 0.05, "Bonferroni": bonferroni(pv) <= 0.05, "Holm": holm(pv) <= 0.05, "BH (q=0.05)": bh(pv) <= 0.05}

Connection to the real job

A/B test platforms track dozens of metrics per test; anomaly detectors scan thousands of series; feature selection tests thousands of columns. Without correction the shortlist is full of noise. BH is the standard way to produce a shortlist with a known error budget.

Applications

  • Data Analytics: many-metric A/B test readouts, segment scans.
  • Data Science: screening features, genes or keywords.
  • Machine Learning: comparing many models or hyperparameters on the same test set.
  • Operations: flagging changes across hundreds of delivery routes or branches after a process change.
COMMON MISTAKE
Applying a correction only to the tests you "remember". The family is all tests that could have been reported, including the ones you ran and dropped. Also, don't use Bonferroni with 10,000 tests when a shortlist for follow-up is what you need; use BH.

  3. Which method controls the share of false alarms among discoveries? Answer: Benjamini-Hochberg (FDR).

5.3 p-Hacking: Peeking, Slicing and Forking Paths

BASIC EXPLANATION
P-hacking is analysing data in many ways, such as peeking, slicing into subgroups or trying different choices, until a significant result appears. It inflates false positives.
INTUITION
Imagine flipping a coin until you see 5 heads in a row and then claiming the coin is biased. Of course you will find it if you keep going. p-hacking means (often innocently) doing things that raise the chance of a "significant" result: stopping the test when the p-value dips below 0.05, trying many subgroups, trying many metrics, many outlier rules, many models. Each choice is a lottery ticket. The final result looks like one test but is really the best of many.

Problem 5.3.1 (peeking at an A/B test) An A/A test (no real difference) has two arms with conversion 5 percent. You look at the data after every 1,000 visitors per arm, up to 10 looks, and stop at the first look where p < 0.05. What share of A/A tests will you wrongly declare a winner? Compare with a single look at the end.

FormulaAt each look compute the two-proportion z-test on all data so far; "declare" if any look has p < 0.05.
Plug in10 looks, α = 0.05, 5,000 simulated A/A experiments
ComputeThe code uses cumulative counts, which is exactly what a dashboard does.
AnswerA single look at the end has a false-positive rate near 5 percent (we get 5.2). Peeking 10 times raises it to 19.6 percent, nearly 4 times the promised rate. Using Bonferroni across the 10 looks (0.005 each) brings it back down to 2.4 percent, which is safe but a little conservative.
CheckA single look must give about 5 percent, which validates the simulation.
Why it mattersPeeking at a dashboard and stopping when it looks good is the most common source of false wins in A/B testing.
rng = np.random.default_rng(41)
S, looks, step, p0 = 5000, 10, 1000, 0.05
xa = np.cumsum(rng.binomial(step, p0, (S, looks)), axis=1)
xb = np.cumsum(rng.binomial(step, p0, (S, looks)), axis=1)
nn = step * np.arange(1, looks + 1)
pp = (xa + xb) / (2 * nn)
z = (xb - xa) / nn / np.sqrt(np.maximum(pp * (1 - pp) * 2 / nn, 1e-12))
pvals = 2 * stats.norm.sf(np.abs(z))
print("single look at the end        : false positive rate = %.3f" % (pvals[:, -1] < .05).mean())
print("peek at all 10 looks, stop on p<.05: false positive rate = %.3f" % (pvals < .05).any(axis=1).mean())
print("Bonferroni over 10 looks (0.005 each): %.3f" % (pvals < .005).any(axis=1).mean())

Problem 5.3.2 (fishing in subgroups) An A/A test is sliced into 20 subgroups (country, device and so on). What is the probability that at least one subgroup shows p < 0.05 by luck? Simulate it, and show the corrected result.

FormulaP(at least one) = 1 − (1 − 0.05)²⁰
Plug in1 − 0.95²⁰ = 1 − 0.3585
Compute0.6415 by theory. Simulate 5,000 experiments of 20 independent null tests.
AnswerAbout 64 percent of null experiments contain at least one "significant" subgroup (0.6424 simulated, 0.6415 theory), and the average number of such subgroups is 1.01. With Holm correction, the false-alarm rate returns to 4.6 percent.
CheckThe simulated fraction should be near 0.64.
Why it matters"The new design works for users in Brazil on Android" is a typical headline from a slice scan. Unless you planned that slice in advance, treat it as a hypothesis to test on new data.
S, G = 5000, 20

  • Research: reproducibility.
COMMON MISTAKE
"We only looked at the data to check progress." Looking is fine; deciding based on the look is the problem. Decide the stopping rule before the test starts.

Check yourself

  1. 20 subgroups, all null. Expected number "significant" at 5 percent? Answer: 1.
  2. Why does peeking inflate false positives? Answer: Each look is another chance to cross the threshold by luck.
  3. Name a fix for peeking. Answer: Fix the sample size in advance, or use a sequential method such as alpha-spending.

5.4 More Ways Data Fools You: Winner's Curse, Regression to the Mean, and Low Prior Odds

BASIC EXPLANATION
The winner's curse is the tendency of the best-looking result to overstate its true effect, and regression to the mean is the tendency of extreme values to be followed by more average ones. With low prior odds of a real effect, many significant findings are false positives.
INTUITION
If you pick the tallest person from a big crowd, they are probably tall and lucky (they happened to be measured on a good day). Next time they will likely be a bit shorter. Selecting extreme results from noisy measurements always selects some luck, and luck does not repeat. This is the winner's curse (when you pick the best of many) and regression to the mean (when you pick the worst or best and measure again). A third trap: if most hypotheses you test are false to begin with, many "significant" results are false even at α = 0.05.

The formula for the third trap

PPV = (power × prior) / (power × prior + α × (1 − prior))

This is just Bayes' theorem from Chapter 1 with "significant" as the positive test.

Problem 5.4.1 (winner's curse) You test 200 landing-page variants. True effects on conversion (in percentage points) are Normal(mean 0, SD 0.3). Each test measures its effect with noise SD 0.5. You ship the top 5 by measured lift. What is the average measured lift of the top 5, and what is their average true lift?

Formulaobserved = true + noise. Selecting the top observed values selects both large true effects and large positive noise.
Plug in200 variants, true SD 0.3, noise SD 0.5
ComputeSimulate 2,000 repeated "tournaments" and average the top-5 results.
AnswerThe top 5 show a measured lift of about 1.34 points, but their true lift is only about 0.35 points: about three-quarters of the apparent gain is luck. The same measured numbers will not be seen again when you re-test.
CheckThe theory for the expected true effect of a selected unit is the "shrinkage" factor σ²_true / (σ²_true + σ²_noise) = 0.09 / 0.34 = 0.26 times the measured value, so 1.34 × 0.26 = 0.35. The simulation agrees.
Why it mattersWhen you pick the best of many variants, the forecast of the gain must be shrunk, or confirmed in a fresh test. Bayesian shrinkage (Chapter 9, 11) formalises this.
rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
obs_top, true_top = [], []
for _ in range(2000):
    true = rng.normal(0, 0.3, 200); obs = true + rng.normal(0, 0.5, 200)
    top = np.argsort(obs)[-5:]
    obs_top.append(obs[top].mean()); true_top.append(true[top].mean())
print("top-5 measured lift: %.2f   top-5 true lift: %.2f   shrink factor %.2f" % (np.mean(obs_top), np.mean(true_top), 0.09 / 0.34))
top-5 measured lift: 1.34   top-5 true lift: 0.35   shrink factor 0.26

Problem 5.4.2 (regression to the mean) 500 delivery branches have a "true" average delivery time of 200 minutes (with branch-specific differences, SD 20) and each day's measurement has noise SD 30. On day 1 you flag the 25 worst branches and "fix" them by doing nothing at all. On day 2 you measure again. What happens to their average?

FormulaDay measurement = branch's true level + daily noise. Flagging the worst picks high noise.
Plug inBranch SD 20, noise SD 30, top 5 percent
ComputeSimulate and compare day 1 and day 2 averages for the flagged branches.
AnswerThe flagged branches average 272 minutes on day 1 and drop to 225 minutes on day 2 with no change at all. A team that "retrained the staff" at those branches would believe it fixed 47 minutes.
CheckExpected day-2 value = 200 + (20² / (20² + 30²)) × (day-1 mean − 200) = 200 + 0.308 × 72.3 = 222.2 (the simulation gave 224.9).
Why it mattersAny "fix the worst offenders" programme, and any "treat the sickest patients" study, will look successful without a control group. This is why randomised comparison (Chapter 12) matters.
rng = np.random.default_rng(43)
true = rng.normal(200, 20, 500)
d1 = true + rng.normal(0, 30, 500); d2 = true + rng.normal(0, 30, 500)
worst = np.argsort(d1)[-25:]
print("flagged branches: day 1 mean %.1f -> day 2 mean %.1f (nothing was changed)" % (d1[worst].mean(), d2[worst].mean()))
print("theory for day 2: %.1f" % (200 + 20**2 / (20**2 + 30**2) * (d1[worst].mean() - 200)))

Problem 5.4.3 (why most findings can be false) A team tests ideas of which only 10 percent really work. Their tests have power 0.8 and α = 0.05. (a) What share of "significant" results is real? (b) If the tests are underpowered (power 0.3), what share is real? (c) If only 2 percent of ideas work and power is 0.8?

FormulaPPV = (power × prior) / (power × prior + α × (1 − prior))
Plug in(a) 0.8 × 0.1 = 0.08; false = 0.05 × 0.9 = 0.045.
Compute(a) 0.08 / (0.08 + 0.045) = 0.64. (b) 0.03 / (0.03 + 0.045) = 0.40. (c) 0.016 / (0.016 + 0.049) = 0.246.
Answer(a) 64 percent, (b) 40 percent, (c) only 24.6 percent of "wins" are real.
CheckVerify (a) with natural frequencies: of 1,000 ideas, 100 work and 80 are detected; of 900 duds, 45 are falsely detected; 80 / (80 + 45) = 64 percent.
Why it mattersIf your organisation tries many long-shot ideas, a large share of the "wins" are noise. Underpowered tests make it worse. This is the same base-rate trap as the alert example in Chapter 1.
def ppv(power, prior, alpha=0.05):
    return power * prior / (power * prior + alpha * (1 - prior))
for pw, pr in [(0.8, 0.1), (0.3, 0.1), (0.8, 0.02), (0.8, 0.5)]:
    print("power=%.1f prior=%.2f -> PPV=%.3f" % (pw, pr, ppv(pw, pr)))
power=0.8 prior=0.10 -> PPV=0.640
power=0.3 prior=0.10 -> PPV=0.400
power=0.8 prior=0.02 -> PPV=0.246

Connection to the real job

Winner's curse explains why launched features rarely deliver the lifts seen in experiments ("the lift decays"). Regression to the mean explains the success of many "rescue the worst" programmes and the failure of many "reward the best" ones. PPV explains why replication matters: confirm surprising findings with a fresh test before betting on them.

Applications

  • Data Analytics / Product: forecasting the impact of winning variants; holdout groups.
  • Machine Learning: the best of 500 hyperparameter runs overstates the true score; use an untouched test set.
  • Operations: "fix the slowest routes" without a control looks like a miracle.
  • Education and health: programmes aimed at the worst performers.
COMMON MISTAKE
Reporting the lift of the best variant as the expected lift after launch. Shrink it, or re-test the winner. Also: concluding a treatment works because the worst cases improved afterwards, with no control group.

Check yourself

  1. Why does the best of many noisy measurements look better than it is? Answer: It was selected partly for good luck (positive noise).
  2. What do you need to rule out regression to the mean? Answer: A control group (or randomisation).
  3. If power is low, does the share of true findings among significant ones rise or fall? Answer: It falls.

5.5 An Honest Reporting Checklist

BASIC EXPLANATION
An honest reporting checklist lists what to state with a result, such as the number of tests run, effect sizes, intervals, assumptions and data exclusions. It lets readers judge how reliable a finding is.
INTUITION
Think of a lab notebook: each step is written before and after the experiment, including the failures. A good analysis report is the same: another person can see what you tried, not just what worked.

Problem 5.5.1 (write the paragraph) From this chapter's numbers, write a short honest summary of the Depot A vs Depot B comparison (Chapter 4 and 5.1): Welch t = 2.07, p = 0.042, difference 25 minutes with a 95 percent interval of 1 to 49 minutes, d = 0.45 (0.02 to 0.89), pre-agreed MDE 20 minutes, only this one metric tested.

FormulaA good summary has: question, design, estimate with interval, effect size, caveats, decision rule.
Plug inUse the numbers above.
Compute(writing task)
Answer"We compared Depot B with Depot A on mean delivery time (one pre-planned metric, 40 and 45 deliveries). B was faster by 25 minutes (95 percent CI 1 to 49 minutes; Welch t = 2.07, p = 0.042; Cohen's d = 0.45, CI 0.02 to 0.89). The estimate is above our 20-minute threshold, but the interval is wide and includes gains below it, so we recommend a larger confirmation run (about 400 deliveries per depot) before concluding that the gain is practically important."
CheckEvery number is traceable to the earlier problems; the decision rule links to the MDE (20 minutes).
Why it mattersThis format protects you in a review, because it states what is known and what is not.

The checklist to copy:

  1. State the question, the primary metric and the MDE before collecting data.
  2. Fix the sample size or the stopping rule in advance.
  3. Report all tests that were run, and correct for them (Holm or BH).
  4. Report effect sizes with confidence intervals, not p-values alone.
  5. Label unplanned findings as "exploratory" and confirm them on fresh data.
  6. Say what the data cannot tell you.

Chapter recap

  • Effect size tells how much; the p-value tells whether it is likely luck. Report both, with an interval.
  • Cohen's d = difference / pooled SD; odds ratios and relative risks differ when the event is common.
  • With enough data, tiny effects become significant; decide the practical threshold beforehand.
  • Running m tests at α inflates false alarms: P(any) = 1 − (1 − α)ᵐ.
  • Bonferroni and Holm control the chance of any false alarm (Holm is never worse); BH controls the share of false discoveries and is the exploration default.
  • Peeking at p-values and fishing in subgroups can raise a nominal 5 percent false-positive rate to 20 percent or 64 percent.
  • Winner's curse and regression to the mean make the best and worst results look more extreme than they are; use controls and re-tests.
  • When few ideas are truly good, many "significant" results are false (PPV).
  • Plan, pre-register, correct, report intervals, and confirm surprises on new data.

Practice set

Problem 5.6.1 Means 80 and 70, pooled SD 20. What is d, and is it small, medium or large?

Answer: d = 10 / 20 = 0.5, medium.

Problem 5.6.2 You run 12 independent tests at α = 0.05. Chance of at least one false positive?

Answer: 1 − 0.95¹² = 1 − 0.5404 = 0.46.

Problem 5.6.3 What is the Bonferroni threshold for 25 tests at α = 0.05?

Answer: 0.05 / 25 = 0.002.

Problem 5.6.4 Four p-values: 0.003, 0.012, 0.030, 0.200. Which are rejected by BH at q = 0.05?

Answer: Thresholds (k/4) × 0.05 = 0.0125, 0.025, 0.0375, 0.05. 0.003 ≤ 0.0125 yes; 0.012 ≤ 0.025 yes; 0.030 ≤ 0.0375 yes; 0.200 ≤ 0.05 no. The first three are rejected. Bonferroni (0.0125) would reject only 0.003.

Problem 5.6.5 What is the output? print(round(0.8*0.1 / (0.8*0.1 + 0.05*0.9), 3))

Answer: 0.08 / 0.125 = 0.64.

Problem 5.6.6 Find the bug: "We watched the test for two weeks, and on day 9 the p-value was 0.03, so we stopped and shipped."

Answer: Optional stopping inflates false positives (up to about 20 percent with 10 looks). The stopping rule must be fixed before the test, or a sequential method used.

Problem 5.6.7 2×2 counts: exposed 30 events of 100; unexposed 10 events of 100. Compute RR and OR.

Answer: RR = 0.30 / 0.10 = 3.0. OR = (30/70) / (10/90) = 0.4286 / 0.1111 = 3.857. The OR is larger because the event is not rare.

Problem 5.6.8 The 10 branches with the longest waits are given to a team that "optimises" them. The next week their average wait drops 15 percent. List two reasons to doubt the team's effect.

Answer: Regression to the mean (the worst branches were partly unlucky) and no control group (other changes or seasonality could also explain it). Compare with similar branches that were left untouched.

Problem 5.6.9 (interview) "You ran 50 metrics in an A/B test and 3 are significant at 0.05. What do you do?"

Answer: Expected false positives are 50 × 0.05 = 2.5, so 3 hits is what noise alone would give. Apply BH or Holm, ask which metrics were pre-specified as primary, look at effect sizes and intervals, and confirm any promising metric in a follow-up test.

Problem 5.6.10 (interview) "Explain the difference between FWER and FDR and when you would use each."

Answer: FWER is the probability of at least one false positive; use it (Bonferroni, Holm) when any false positive is costly, as in a confirmatory safety decision. FDR is the expected proportion of false positives among the discoveries; use it (Benjamini-Hochberg) for screening many hypotheses when you will validate the shortlist later.
