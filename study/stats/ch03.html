An online shop checks 400 parcels and finds the average delivery time is 480 minutes. Your manager asks: "So what is the real average for all customers?" You cannot know it exactly, because you only measured a small part of the world. Statistics gives you a clear way to answer: "Probably between 450 and 510 minutes, and here is how I know." In this chapter you learn how samples are chosen (and how bad choices poison everything), how the noise of an average shrinks as the sample grows (the Central Limit Theorem), how to turn one sample into an honest range (confidence intervals), how to estimate parameters (maximum likelihood and method of moments), and how to get intervals for any statistic, such as p95, using the bootstrap. Every concept is checked by simulation, so you will see the theory work.

Our running example is the delivery time of an online shop: the full population has about 100,000 parcels per week, and we look at samples of 25 to 1,000.

3.1 Sampling Methods and Sampling Bias

BASIC EXPLANATION
A sample is a subset of a population used to learn about the whole. Sampling methods such as simple random, stratified and cluster sampling decide how it is chosen, and sampling bias is a systematic error from a sample that does not represent the population.
INTUITION
To taste a big pot of soup you stir it first, then taste one spoon. Stirring makes every part of the pot equally likely to be in the spoon. If you only taste the top, where the salt has not mixed, you get a wrong idea no matter how many top-spoonfuls you taste. Statistics calls "stirring" random sampling, and "tasting the top" sampling bias. More data does not fix bias. Only a better way of choosing the data does.

Problem 3.1.1 A shop has 100,000 customers: 70 percent mobile (average spend 20) and 30 percent desktop (average spend 60). The true mean spend is 0.7 × 20 + 0.3 × 60 = 32. (a) Draw a simple random sample of 200 customers, many times, and see how the estimate behaves. (b) Draw a stratified sample (140 mobile, 60 desktop). (c) Draw a biased sample where desktop users are 4 times more likely to respond. Compare the average and the spread of the three estimators over 2,000 repeats.

FormulaStratified estimate = Σ (stratum weight × stratum sample mean). The biased estimate is just the plain sample mean of the people who responded.
Plug inweights 0.7 and 0.3; response probability proportional to 1 for mobile and 4 for desktop, so the expected desktop share among responders is 0.3×4 / (0.7×1 + 0.3×4) = 1.2 / 1.9 = 0.632.
ComputeBiased expected mean = 0.368 × 20 + 0.632 × 60 = 7.37 + 37.9 = 45.3, far above 32. The code repeats each method 2,000 times.
AnswerSimple random and stratified samples both centre on 32, and stratified has about half the spread (0.74 against 1.47). The biased sample centres on about 45.2, which is wrong, and gathering more data would not help.
CheckThe theoretical biased mean 45.3 matches the simulation. The spread of a random sample of 200 is about √(var / 200) where the population SD is 21.1 (we compute it in the code), so 21.1 / √200 = 1.49. The simulation gives 1.47.
Why it mattersA survey of "customers who answered" or a tracker that only logs parcels that arrived (so lost parcels vanish) gives confident wrong answers. Always ask: how did this data get to me?

Predict: which estimator has the smallest spread, and which one is centred on the wrong value?

import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(20)
N = 100_000
seg = rng.random(N) < 0.7                       # True = mobile
spend = np.where(seg, rng.normal(20, 8, N), rng.normal(60, 15, N))
print("true mean:", round(spend.mean(), 3), " population sd:", round(spend.std(), 2))
mob, desk = spend[seg], spend[~seg]
srs, strat, biased = [], [], []
p_resp = np.where(seg, 1.0, 4.0); p_resp = p_resp / p_resp.sum()
for _ in range(2000):
    srs.append(rng.choice(spend, 200, replace=False).mean())
    strat.append(0.7 * rng.choice(mob, 140, replace=False).mean() + 0.3 * rng.choice(desk, 60, replace=False).mean())
    biased.append(spend[rng.choice(N, 200, replace=False, p=p_resp)].mean())
for name, a in [("simple random", srs), ("stratified", strat), ("biased (desktop x4)", biased)]:

Problem 3.1.2 (cluster sampling and its cost) A courier company has 100 depots, each with 50 drivers. Drivers inside one depot have similar average delivery times because they share the same roads and dispatcher. You can measure 500 drivers. Compare (a) a random sample of 500 drivers across all depots with (b) a cluster sample of 10 whole depots.

FormulaVariance of the estimate rises with the similarity of items inside a cluster (the intra-cluster correlation).
Plug inEach depot has its own mean delivery time (minutes) drawn from Normal(50, 10); each driver varies around the depot mean with SD 5.
ComputeRepeat both methods 2,000 times and compare the SD of the estimated overall mean.
AnswerThe cluster sample is much noisier (SD 2.67 versus 0.42 for random sampling, about 6 times larger), although both use 500 drivers.
CheckTheory for 10 clusters: SD ≈ 10 / √10 = 3.2 (between-depot noise dominates); it is a little smaller in the simulation (2.67) because we sample 10 of only 100 depots, so some of the variation is removed. For 500 random drivers: √((10² + 5²) / 500) = 0.5, and the simulation gives 0.42 for the same reason.
Why it mattersIf your data come in groups (pupils in classes, orders in deliveries, patients in hospitals), the effective sample size is closer to the number of groups than to the number of rows. This will matter again for mixed models in Chapter 11.
rng = np.random.default_rng(21)
depot_means = rng.normal(50, 10, 100)
drivers = depot_means[:, None] + rng.normal(0, 5, (100, 50))
flat = drivers.ravel()
a, b = [], []
for _ in range(2000):
    a.append(rng.choice(flat, 500, replace=False).mean())
    b.append(drivers[rng.choice(100, 10, replace=False)].mean())

Connection to the real job

In analytics you rarely control collection, so you must detect sampling bias: survivorship (only successful deliveries logged), non-response, and time bias (measuring only on Monday morning). Even the measuring tool can bias results: a stopwatch that is only started when the parcel leaves the depot misses the slow waiting time before dispatch, so the slow cases are under-counted.

Applications

  • Data Analytics: survey analysis, A/B test assignment, data auditing by stratum.
  • Data Science / ML: stratified train/test splits (stratify=y) so rare classes appear in both parts.
  • Data Engineering: sampling big tables for quality checks.
  • MLOps: monitoring on a sample of traffic; make sure the sample is random.
COMMON MISTAKE
Thinking "my sample is large, so it is trustworthy". A million biased rows are worse than 200 random ones, because the large number gives false confidence. Check how the sample was collected before checking how big it is.

Check yourself

  1. Which method fixes "small groups get missed"? Answer: Stratified sampling.
  2. Why can 10 clusters of 50 drivers be less informative than 500 random drivers? Answer: Drivers in one cluster are alike, so they add little new information.
  3. Does more data reduce bias? Answer: No, it only reduces random noise.

3.2 Standard Error and the Central Limit Theorem

BASIC EXPLANATION
The standard error is the standard deviation of a statistic, such as the sample mean, across repeated samples. The central limit theorem states that sample means are approximately normally distributed for large samples, whatever the shape of the data.
INTUITION
Weigh yourself on a wobbly bathroom scale five times and average the readings. The average wobbles much less than a single reading. If you average 100 readings it wobbles even less. The wobble of an average is called the standard error. The wonder is the Central Limit Theorem (CLT): no matter how weird the original data look (skewed delivery times, 0/1 conversions), the average of many values has a bell-shaped distribution around the true mean. That is why the normal curve is everywhere in statistics.

Words you need

WordPlain meaningExample
Sampling distributionThe distribution of a statistic over many repeated samplesThe distribution of x̄ for n = 36
Standard error (SE)The standard deviation of a statisticSE of the mean = σ/√n
CLTFor large n, x̄ is approximately Normal(μ, σ²/n)
z-score(value − mean) / SDz = 2 means 2 SDs above

The rules

  • SE(x̄) = σ / √n, estimated by s / √n where s is the sample SD.
  • Quadrupling the sample size halves the SE (the square-root law). Going from n = 100 to n = 10,000 gives only 10 times less noise.
  • For a proportion: SE(p̂) = √(p(1 − p) / n).
  • The CLT needs a finite variance. The more skewed the data, the larger n must be. Heavy tails (like the Cauchy) break it.
  • SD versus SE: SD describes how spread out individual values are. SE describes how precisely you know the mean. Do not mix them.

Problem 3.2.1 Delivery time (minutes) in a population is log-normal with mean 227.0 min and SD 121.0 min (heavily right-skewed). You take samples of size n and compute the mean. (a) What is the SE for n = 36 and n = 400? (b) Simulate 20,000 sample means for each n and check the SD of the means. (c) How skewed are the sample means?

FormulaSE = σ / √n
Plug inn = 36: 121.0 / 6; n = 400: 121.0 / 20
Compute121.0 / 6 = 20.2 min; 121.0 / 20 = 6.05 min.
AnswerThe simulated SDs of the means are 20.24 and 6.08 min, as theory says. The individual delivery times have skewness 1.77, the means for n = 36 have 0.28, and the means for n = 400 have skewness 0.10, so they are almost symmetric.
CheckThe simulated mean of the sample means must be near 227.0 (unbiasedness). The simulated values are 227.2 and 227.0.

Applications

3.3 Point Estimation: Maximum Likelihood and Method of Moments

BASIC EXPLANATION
A point estimate is a single value computed from data to estimate a parameter. Maximum likelihood chooses the parameter value that makes the observed data most probable, and the method of moments matches sample moments to theoretical ones.
INTUITION
You see 50 gaps between failures and you believe they come from an exponential distribution, but you do not know the rate. You must pick one number for the rate. Two clever strategies exist. Method of moments: choose the rate so that the model's average equals your data's average. Maximum likelihood (MLE): choose the rate that makes the data you actually saw most probable. The Math PDF (Pillar 3.2) gave you the MLE idea. Here we practise it and compare the two.

Words you need

WordPlain meaningExample
EstimatorA recipe that turns data into a guess for a parameterx̄ for the mean
BiasAverage error of an estimator over many samplesSample variance with n in the denominator is biased low
Likelihood L(θ)Probability of the observed data as a function of the parameter θ
Log-likelihoodln L(θ); sums instead of products, easier to maximise
Fisher informationHow sharply the log-likelihood curves at its top; more curve means less uncertaintySE(θ̂) ≈ 1/√(I(θ))

The recipes

  • Method of moments (MoM): set sample moments equal to theoretical moments (sample mean = E[X], sample variance = Var(X)) and solve for the parameters.
  • MLE: maximise Σ ln f(xᵢ; θ). Take the derivative, set it to zero.
  • Examples: Exponential rate λ̂ = 1/x̄. Poisson λ̂ = x̄. Normal: μ̂ = x̄, σ̂² = Σ(xᵢ − x̄)²/n (biased; divide by n−1 for unbiased).
  • For large samples MLEs are approximately unbiased, normal and have the smallest possible variance (they are "efficient"). That is why they are the default.

Problem 3.3.1 (exponential MLE by hand) Five gaps between failures (in hours) are 2, 5, 1, 4, 3. (a) Find the MLE of the failure rate λ. (b) Find its approximate standard error. (c) Give the log-likelihood at λ = 0.2 and at the MLE to show the MLE is higher.

FormulaL(λ) = ∏ λ e^(−λxᵢ), so ln L = n ln λ − λ Σxᵢ. Setting the derivative n/λ − Σxᵢ = 0 gives λ̂ = n / Σxᵢ. Fisher information I(λ) = n / λ², so SE = λ̂ / √n.
Plug inn = 5, Σx = 15
Computeλ̂ = 5 / 15 = 0.3333 per hour. SE = 0.3333 / √5 = 0.1491. ln L(0.2) = 5 ln 0.2 − 0.2 × 15 = −8.047 − 3 = −11.047. ln L(0.3333) = 5 ln 0.3333 − 0.3333 × 15 = −5.493 − 5 = −10.493.
AnswerThe MLE is one failure every 3 hours (rate 0.333 per hour), with standard error about 0.149. The log-likelihood at the MLE (−10.49) beats that at 0.2 (−11.05).
CheckThe MLE equals 1 / (mean gap) = 1 / 3 = 0.333, as the formula says. A numerical optimiser gives the same value (code below).
Why it mattersWith only 5 observations the SE (0.149) is almost half the estimate: the data say very little. The relative error is 1/√n = 45 percent. You need about 100 failures for 10 percent.
from scipy import optimize
x = np.array([2, 5, 1, 4, 3.0])
ll = lambda lam: len(x) * np.log(lam) - lam * x.sum()
res = optimize.minimize_scalar(lambda l: -ll(l), bounds=(0.01, 5), method="bounded")
print("MLE closed form:", round(len(x) / x.sum(), 4), " numeric:", round(res.x, 4))
print("SE:", round((len(x) / x.sum()) / np.sqrt(len(x)), 4))
print("loglik at 0.2 :", round(ll(0.2), 3), " at MLE:", round(ll(len(x) / x.sum()), 3))

Problem 3.3.2 (method of moments versus MLE for Gamma) Service time at a bank counter is Gamma distributed with shape 3 and scale 50 seconds (mean 150). From a sample of 300, estimate shape and scale by MoM and MLE. Repeat for 1,000 samples and compare the spread (variance) of the two estimators.

FormulaFor Gamma(k, θ): mean = kθ, variance = kθ². MoM: θ̂ = s² / x̄ and k̂ = x̄² / s².
Plug inExample: if x̄ = 150 and s² = 7,500 then θ̂ = 50 and k̂ = 22,500 / 7,500 = 3.
ComputeThe MLE has no closed form and is found numerically by stats.gamma.fit(data, floc=0).
AnswerBoth are close to the truth, but the MLE has a smaller spread across repeated samples, which is its advantage (efficiency).
CheckBoth estimators should average near the true shape 3 across 1,000 samples. Compare printed averages.
Why it mattersUse MoM for a quick start (and as the starting point for an optimiser); use MLE for final results.
rng = np.random.default_rng(23)

for _ in range(1000):

print("true shape 3.0")

Connection to the real job

Every model you train by minimising a loss is doing MLE in disguise: squared error is the MLE for normal noise; log-loss (cross-entropy) is the MLE for Bernoulli labels. Knowing this lets you pick a loss that matches the data.

Applications

  • Data Science: fitting distributions to delivery times, claim size or time-to-event.
  • Machine Learning: logistic regression, Poisson regression, neural network losses.
  • Reliability engineering: failure-rate estimation from few failures.
  • Finance: volatility estimation.
COMMON MISTAKE
Reporting a rate estimated from very few events without its uncertainty. "MTBF is 3 hours" from 5 failures could easily be 1.5 or 8 hours.

3.4 Confidence Intervals

BASIC EXPLANATION
A confidence interval is a range of values computed from a sample that is built to contain the true parameter in a stated share (such as 95%) of repeated samples. It shows the precision of an estimate.
INTUITION
A single number like "480 minutes" sounds precise but is a guess. A confidence interval (CI) gives a range, like "480 ± 41 minutes". The meaning of "95 percent" is subtle and often misunderstood. Imagine an archer who hits within a 1-metre circle 95 times out of 100. Before shooting you can say "my arrow will land in the circle with 95 percent chance". The CI procedure is that archer: if you repeat the whole study many times, 95 percent of the intervals you build will contain the true value. For your one interval, the truth is either in it or not; you just do not know which.

Words you need

WordPlain meaningExample
Confidence levelHow often the method captures the truth in repeated use95 percent
Margin of errorHalf the width of the interval±41 minutes
CoverageThe actual fraction of intervals that contain the truthShould be about 95 percent
Wald intervalp̂ ± z√(p̂(1−p̂)/n), simple but poor for small n or rare events
Wilson intervalA better interval for proportions

The rules

  • Mean (unknown SD): x̄ ± t* · s/√n, where t* is the t quantile with n−1 df (Chapter 2). For n > 100, t* is almost 1.96.
  • Proportion: Wald p̂ ± 1.96 √(p̂(1−p̂)/n); Wilson and exact (Clopper-Pearson) intervals are safer for small n or p near 0 or 1.
  • Rule of three: if you see 0 failures in n trials, the 95 percent upper limit on the failure rate is about 3/n.
  • Width: halving the width needs four times the data.
  • A wide interval is information: it says "we do not know yet".

3.5 The Bootstrap

BASIC EXPLANATION
The bootstrap estimates the variability of a statistic by resampling the data with replacement many times. It is used to compute standard errors and confidence intervals without strong assumptions.
INTUITION
You want an error bar for the 95th percentile of delivery time, but no neat formula exists. The bootstrap says: your sample is your best picture of the population, so treat the sample as the population. Draw many new samples from your sample, with replacement (like pulling numbered balls from a bag and putting each back), compute your statistic each time, and look at how much it varies. You are "pulling yourself up by your own bootstraps". It works for medians, percentiles, ratios, correlations, and almost anything else.

Words you need

WordPlain meaningExample
ResampleA new sample of the same size, drawn from the data with replacement500 values drawn from 500
Bootstrap distributionThe statistic computed on each resample10,000 medians
Percentile intervalThe 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the bootstrap distributionA 95 percent CI

The recipe

  1. Take your sample of n values.
  2. Repeat B times (say 10,000): draw n values with replacement; compute the statistic.
  3. The standard deviation of the B results is the bootstrap SE. Their 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles give a 95 percent percentile interval.

The bootstrap works best for smooth statistics (mean, median, p90). It struggles for extremes (the maximum, the 99.99th percentile), for very small samples, and for dependent data (time series need a block bootstrap).

rng = np.random.default_rng(27)
def diff_ci(n):
    a = rng.lognormal(np.log(200), 0.5, n); b = rng.lognormal(np.log(190), 0.5, n)
    ia = rng.integers(0, n, (5000, n)); ib = rng.integers(0, n, (5000, n))
    d = np.median(b[ib], axis=1) - np.median(a[ia], axis=1)
    return np.median(b) - np.median(a), np.percentile(d, [2.5, 97.5])

Connection to the real job

Bootstrap is the go-to when no formula exists: p95 delivery time, differences in medians, AUC, ratios of two means, and complicated business metrics. It is also the tool behind "confidence band on a model's accuracy" and behind bagging and random forests (each tree sees a bootstrap sample).

Applications

  • Operations: CI on percentiles, detecting whether one depot really became slower.
  • Data Science: intervals for AUC, F1, or any custom metric.
  • Machine Learning: bagging and random forests.
  • Finance: risk measures such as Value at Risk.
COMMON MISTAKE
Bootstrapping dependent data (a time series of daily delivery times) as if every row were independent. The resamples destroy the time structure and the interval becomes too narrow. Use a block bootstrap, or analyse per-test summaries instead.

Check yourself

  1. Why do we resample with replacement? Answer: Without it every resample would be identical to the original sample.
  2. Name one statistic where the bootstrap fails. Answer: The maximum of a sample (or extreme quantiles like the 99.99th percentile).
  3. Bootstrap SE of the mean is close to what formula? Answer: s / √n.

Chapter recap

  • A sample can only be trusted if the way it was chosen is fair; bias is not cured by size.
  • Stratified sampling reduces noise when groups differ; cluster sampling is cheap but noisy when items inside a cluster are alike.
  • The standard error of a mean is σ/√n; quadruple the data to halve the noise.
  • The CLT says averages become normal even if the data are skewed; proportions need n·p ≥ 10.
  • Estimators: method of moments matches averages; MLE maximises the likelihood and is efficient for large n.
  • Sample variance divides by n−1 to remove bias.
  • A 95 percent CI describes the procedure, not a single interval; use t for the mean and Wilson or exact for proportions.
  • Zero failures in n trials still allows a failure rate up to about 3/n.
  • The bootstrap gives an SE and CI for almost any statistic, including p95; it fails for extremes and for dependent data unless adapted.

Problem 3.6.5 By what factor must n grow to reduce the margin of error from ±6 to ±2?

Answer: The margin scales as 1/√n, so we need 3 times smaller, which is 9 times more data.

Problem 3.6.6 Poisson counts: 2, 0, 3, 1, 4, 2. What is the MLE of λ and its SE?

Answer: Mean = 12/6 = 2. SE = √(λ̂ / n) = √(2/6) = 0.577.

Problem 3.6.7 Find the bug: "The mean delivery time is 300 min ± 5 min (SD of 5), so 95 percent of parcels are between 290 and 310 min."

Answer: The ±5 is the SE of the mean, not the SD of parcels. With n = 400 the parcel SD would be 5 × 20 = 100 min. Individual parcels range far more widely.

Problem 3.6.8 A survey of 1,000 people who visited the help page says 90 percent are unhappy with the product. Why is this unreliable?

Answer: Sampling bias (self-selection): people visiting a help page are those who have a problem. Size does not repair it.

Problem 3.6.9 (interview) "Explain a 95 percent confidence interval to a manager in two sentences."

Answer: "If we repeated this measurement many times and built an interval each time, about 95 of 100 intervals would contain the true value. For this study the plausible range for the average is 438 to 521 minutes, so we cannot yet say it meets a 500-minute target."

Problem 3.6.10 (interview) "You must report the 99th percentile of delivery time from 2,000 parcels. How do you quantify its uncertainty and why is it hard?"

Answer: Use a bootstrap percentile interval. It is hard because the 99th percentile depends on only about 20 data points in the tail, so it is noisy, and the bootstrap itself is less reliable for such extreme quantiles. Report the interval and consider collecting more data or fitting a tail model.
