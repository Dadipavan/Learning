A statistics interview rarely asks you to recite a formula. It asks you to explain an idea in plain words, do a quick calculation on a whiteboard, and spot what is wrong in a business story. This chapter gathers all three. First you get an answer method that works for any concept question. Then a bank of model answers with real numbers, then ten pitfalls, each demonstrated with a simulation you can run so you truly believe it. The earlier chapters taught the ideas; here you practise saying them clearly and recognising the traps. The simulations use one script with a fixed seed (numpy, scipy), and every number below came from it or from a hand check.

14.1 How to Answer a Statistics Question

BASIC EXPLANATION
Answering a statistics question well means stating the concept, giving the formula or definition, working an example and noting assumptions. This section gives a structure for doing that clearly in an interview.
INTUITION
An interviewer is not checking whether you have memorised a definition. They are checking whether you could be trusted with a real decision. A good answer is like a good bug report: short, with an example, and with the limits stated.

The four-step method

  1. Say it in one plain sentence (no jargon).
  2. Give a tiny example with numbers.
  3. Say why it matters (what decision it changes).
  4. State the limit (when it fails, or the common misreading).

Problem 14.1.1 "What is a p-value?" Answer with the four steps.

Step 1"If there were truly no effect, the p-value is how often we would see a result at least this extreme just by luck."
Step 2"Example: a new page gives 11.0% conversion against 10.0%, p = 0.03. If the page did nothing, about 3 in 100 experiments would show a gap this big or bigger."
Step 3"It tells us whether the gap is easy to explain by noise."
Step 4"It is not the probability the page works, it does not measure effect size, and with huge samples tiny effects get tiny p-values."
AnswerThat four-part answer covers definition, example, use and misreading, in under 30 seconds.
CheckA weak answer is "the probability the null hypothesis is true". That is the single most common wrong answer.
Why it mattersThe same structure works for confidence intervals, power, regularization and anything else.

Problem 14.1.2 An interviewer says: "Our 95% CI for the mean waiting time is 210 to 250 minutes. So there is a 95% chance the true mean is in that range, right?" What do you say?

Step 1Politely say: "Almost, but the wording matters."
Step 2The true mean is one fixed number. The method captures it 95% of the time over repeated samples.
Step 3Quick proof: simulate 5,000 samples of 25 from a Normal(50, 10) population and count how often the interval contains 50. The result (below) is 0.9518.
Answer"If we repeated the study many times, about 95% of the intervals built this way would contain the true mean. For this one interval, the truth is either in it or not." Many practitioners accept the casual wording; say so and move on if the interviewer prefers it. A Bayesian credible interval does allow the "95% probability" statement (Chapter 9).
CheckCoverage 0.9518 is close to 0.95.
Why it mattersIt shows you know what is random (the interval) and what is fixed (the parameter).

Applications: hiring loops for Data Science, Analytics, ML Engineering and Quant roles; internal reviews where you must explain a result to non-specialists.

COMMON MISTAKE
Answering with a formula and nothing else. Formulas show memory; examples and limits show understanding.

Check yourself

  1. What are the four steps? Answer: plain sentence, example, why it matters, limit.
  2. Why say the limit? Answer: it shows you know when the idea fails.
  3. Is "p = 0.03 means a 3% chance the effect is zero" correct? Answer: no.

14.2 The Concept Question Bank

BASIC EXPLANATION
The concept question bank is a set of common statistics questions, such as on p-values, confidence intervals, bias and variance, with model answers.
INTUITION
Interview questions repeat. If you can answer these fifteen smoothly, you can handle most of the rest by combining them.

Each answer is short, because you must say it in about 30 seconds. Chapter numbers show where to revise.

#QuestionStrong short answerChapter
1Mean vs median?Mean is pulled by outliers; use median for skewed data like hospital waiting times. Mean 204.5 min vs median 148.1 min in our lognormal data.1
2Why n−1 in the sample variance?Dividing by n underestimates the spread, because deviations are measured from the sample mean, not the true mean. Average of 100,000 samples of size 5: 0.800 with n, 1.002 with n−1 (true 1).3
3State the Central Limit Theorem.Averages of many independent values are close to Normal, whatever the shape of the data, so standard error = σ/√n.3
4Type I vs Type II error?Type I: false alarm (reject a true null), rate α. Type II: miss (keep a false null), rate β. Power = 1 − β.4
5What determines power?Effect size, sample size, noise, and α. Halving the effect needs about 4 times the sample (3,841 to 14,751 per group in Chapter 12).4, 12
6Confidence interval vs p-value?The p-value says only "surprising or not". The interval shows the plausible size of the effect. Report the interval.3, 4
7Statistical vs practical significance?With 2 million users per arm, 10.0% vs 10.1% gives z = 3.33 and p = 0.00088, yet 0.1 point may be worthless. Ask about effect size and cost.5
8Multiple testing?Run 20 tests at 5% and 64% of the time at least one is "significant" by luck. Fix with Bonferroni, Holm or Benjamini-Hochberg.5
9Correlation vs causation?A third factor can drive both (confounding). Randomize, or adjust for measured confounders.1, 12
10Bias-variance trade-off?Simple models underfit (bias), flexible ones chase noise (variance). Regularization and cross-validation balance them.7
11When is R² misleading?It never falls when you add features. Use adjusted R², AIC/BIC, or test-set error.6, 7
12Logistic regression coefficient?exp(β) is the odds ratio. Not a probability change.8
13Frequentist vs Bayesian?Frequentist: probability is long-run frequency; Bayesian: probability is degree of belief, updated by data using a prior.9
14Why check stationarity before ARIMA?Statistics like mean and variance must be stable over time for the model's patterns to carry forward.10
15Censoring?The event has not happened yet when we stop watching; delete the rows and you bias results. Use survival methods.11

Where each strong answer gets its numbers: rows 1, 2, 7 and 8 come from the simulations in 14.4.

Problem 14.2.1 Using the table, answer row 5 as in an interview: "Why can't we detect a very small lift quickly?"

Step 1Plain sentence: "A small effect is hard to separate from noise, so we need many users."
Step 2Example from Chapter 12: lifting 10% to 12% needs about 3,841 users per group; lifting 10% to 11% needs about 14,751, nearly 4 times more, because the needed sample grows with 1/(effect)².
Step 3Why it matters: it tells the business how long the test must run.
Step 4Limit: the formula assumes fixed sample size; peeking breaks it.
AnswerThat reply is complete in four sentences.
Check(0.02/0.01)² = 4, matching the 3,841 → 14,751 growth within rounding (the formula is not exactly 1/effect²).
Why it mattersQuoting a computed number from memory sounds credible.

Applications: screening interviews, take-home explanations, and writing documentation for stakeholders.

COMMON MISTAKE
Giving every answer at the same length. If the interviewer wants depth they will ask; start short, then go deeper on request.

Check yourself

  1. Why is "odds ratio" not "probability ratio"? Answer: odds = p/(1−p); the two differ unless p is small.
  2. Which test statistic handles unequal variances in a two-sample t-test? Answer: Welch's t-test.
  3. What is the name of the correction that controls false discovery rate? Answer: Benjamini-Hochberg.

14.3 Calculation Questions Done Out Loud

BASIC EXPLANATION
Calculation questions ask for a number worked out step by step, such as a probability or test statistic. Doing it out loud means stating the method, the formula and each step.
INTUITION
Whiteboard questions are small. They test whether you set up the problem correctly. Say the setup before the arithmetic.

Problem 14.3.1 (Base rate) A fraud detector flags 90% of real fraud and wrongly flags 5% of genuine transactions. Fraud is 1% of transactions. A transaction is flagged. What is the chance it is fraud?

FormulaP(fraud | flag) = P(flag | fraud) P(fraud) / P(flag)
Plug inUse 10,000 transactions: 100 fraud, 9,900 genuine.
ComputeFlagged fraud: 0.9 × 100 = 90. Flagged genuine: 0.05 × 9,900 = 495. Total flagged 585. 90 / 585 = 0.1538.
AnswerAbout 15%. Only about 1 flagged transaction in 6.5 is real fraud.
CheckBayes formula: 0.009 / (0.009 + 0.0495) = 0.1538.
Why it mattersThis is the base-rate trap of Chapter 1; fraud teams must handle the 85% false alarms.

Problem 14.3.2 (Multiple testing) Eight p-values: 0.001, 0.008, 0.012, 0.04, 0.045, 0.20, 0.60, 0.90. Which are significant at 5% under Bonferroni, Holm and Benjamini-Hochberg?

Step 1Bonferroni threshold: 0.05 / 8 = 0.00625. Only 0.001 passes: 1 discovery.
Step 2Holm tests the smallest at 0.05/8 = 0.00625 (0.001 passes), then the next at 0.05/7 = 0.00714. 0.008 > 0.00714, so stop: 1 discovery.
Step 3BH compares the i-th smallest p with 0.05 × i / 8: thresholds 0.00625, 0.0125, 0.01875, 0.025, 0.03125. The largest i where p ≤ threshold is i = 3 (0.012 ≤ 0.01875). Next, 0.04 > 0.025 and 0.045 > 0.03125. So reject the first 3.
AnswerBonferroni 1, Holm 1, Benjamini-Hochberg 3.
CheckBH is the least strict because it controls the expected share of false discoveries, not the chance of any false discovery.
Why it mattersSay which error you control; it is the answer the interviewer wants.

Problem 14.3.3 (Interval) A sample of 25 hospital waiting times has mean 230 minutes and standard deviation 50 minutes. Give a 95% confidence interval for the mean.

Formulamean ± t × s/√n, with t = 2.064 for 24 degrees of freedom.
Plug ins/√n = 50/5 = 10.
Compute2.064 × 10 = 20.64. Interval 230 ± 20.64 = (209.4, 250.6).
Answer(209.4, 250.6) minutes.
Checkscipy.stats.t.ppf(.975, 24) is 2.0639. Using z = 1.96 would give ±19.6, a slightly too-narrow interval for n = 25.
Why it mattersUse t for small samples.

Applications: Data Analytics (quick calculations in review meetings), Data Science (sizing tests), Finance (risk and false positives in alerts).

COMMON MISTAKE
Skipping the setup and going straight to arithmetic. Say "I will use 10,000 imaginary transactions" or "I need a t value because n is small"; a correct setup earns marks even if the arithmetic slips.

Check yourself

  1. What does BH control? Answer: the false discovery rate.
  2. In 14.3.1, if fraud were 10% instead of 1%, would precision rise or fall? Answer: rise (to 0.09/(0.09+0.045) = 67%).
  3. Why t and not z? Answer: the standard deviation is estimated from a small sample.

14.4 Ten Pitfalls, Each With a Simulation

BASIC EXPLANATION
A statistical pitfall is a common mistake that leads to wrong conclusions, such as peeking, multiple testing or confounding. Each one can be shown with a short simulation.
INTUITION
Most statistical disasters are not wrong formulas; they are a correct formula applied to data that was selected, peeked at or confounded. Here you see each trap happen on your own screen.

We run one script (seed 14). The code for each trap is short; the real output follows the code.

import numpy as np
from scipy import stats
rng=np.random.default_rng(14)
# Trap 1: 20 independent tests, no real effect anywhere
hits=0
for _ in range(2000):
    p=[stats.ttest_ind(rng.normal(size=30),rng.normal(size=30)).pvalue for _ in range(20)]
    hits+=min(p)<0.05
print("theory",round(1-0.95**20,4),"simulated",hits/2000)
# Trap 2: peek at an A/A test 10 times (every 100 users)
peek=final=0
for _ in range(2000):
    a=rng.normal(size=1000);b=rng.normal(size=1000)
    ps=[stats.ttest_ind(a[:n],b[:n]).pvalue for n in range(100,1001,100)]
    peek+=min(ps)<0.05;final+=ps[-1]<0.05
print("peek 10 times",peek/2000,"look once",final/2000)
theory 0.6415 simulated 0.6265
peek 10 times 0.201 look once 0.054

Trap 1: p-hacking and multiple looks. With 20 tests and no real effect, about 64% of runs find "something" (theory 0.6415, simulated 0.6265). Cause: each test has a 5% false-alarm chance. Fix: pre-register the primary metric, correct for multiple tests (Chapter 5).

Trap 2: peeking. Looking 10 times at a test with no true difference gave a false "winner" in 20.1% of runs, against 5.4% for one look at the end. Cause: each look is another chance. Fix: fixed sample size, or a sequential method designed for repeated looks (Chapter 12).

# Trap 3: regression to the mean
n=100000
skill=rng.normal(100,10,n);s1=skill+rng.normal(0,10,n);s2=skill+rng.normal(0,10,n)
top=s1>np.percentile(s1,90)
print("top decile test1",round(s1[top].mean(),1),"test2",round(s2[top].mean(),1),"corr",round(np.corrcoef(s1,s2)[0,1],3))
# Trap 4: selection creates a fake relationship
x=rng.normal(size=100000);y=rng.normal(size=100000);sel=(x+y)>1.5
print("corr all",round(np.corrcoef(x,y)[0,1],3),"selected",round(np.corrcoef(x[sel],y[sel])[0,1],3))
top decile test1 124.8 test2 112.5 corr 0.499
corr all -0.003 selected -0.686

Trap 3: regression to the mean. The top 10% on a first noisy test scored 124.8; on a second test of the same people, their average was 112.5 with no intervention at all. Test scores are skill plus luck, and the lucky ones are not lucky twice (correlation between tests 0.499). Danger: "we trained the worst servers and they improved". They would have anyway. Fix: use a control group.

Trap 4: selection bias (survivorship, collider). x and y are independent (correlation −0.003). Keep only rows where x + y > 1.5 (think "only startups that survived" or "only hired candidates") and they become strongly negatively correlated (−0.686). Fix: ask who is missing from the dataset.

# Trap 5: huge n makes tiny effects "significant"
n=2_000_000;pa,pb=0.1000,0.1010
se=np.sqrt(pa*(1-pa)/n+pb*(1-pb)/n);z=(pb-pa)/se
print("z",round(z,3),"p",round(2*stats.norm.sf(z),5))
# Trap 6: overfitting with many features
Xr=rng.normal(size=(50,40));yr=rng.normal(size=50)
Xb=np.c_[np.ones(50),Xr];b=np.linalg.lstsq(Xb,yr,rcond=None)[0];r=yr-Xb@b
print("train R2",round(1-r@r/((yr-yr.mean())**2).sum(),3))
Xt=rng.normal(size=(1000,40));yt=rng.normal(size=1000);rt=yt-np.c_[np.ones(1000),Xt]@b
print("test R2",round(1-rt@rt/((yt-yt.mean())**2).sum(),3))
z 3.326 p 0.00088
train R2 0.754
test R2 -2.565

Trap 5: significance is not importance. A 0.1 point lift on 2 million users per arm has p = 0.00088 but may not repay the engineering cost. Report the effect and the interval.

Trap 6: overfitting. 40 pure-noise features, 50 rows: the fit explains 75.4% of the variance (training R²). On 1,000 fresh rows, R² = −2.565, worse than predicting the mean (a negative R² on new data means exactly that). Fix: hold-out data, cross-validation, regularization (Chapter 7), and more rows than features.

# Trap 7: skewed data and the mean
wait=rng.lognormal(5,0.8,100000)
print("mean",round(wait.mean(),1),"median",round(np.median(wait),1),"p95",round(np.percentile(wait,95),1))
# Trap 8: variance with n vs n-1
v0=np.mean([np.var(rng.normal(0,1,5)) for _ in range(100000)]);v1=np.mean([np.var(rng.normal(0,1,5),ddof=1) for _ in range(100000)])
print("ddof0",round(v0,3),"ddof1",round(v1,3))
# Trap 9: CI coverage
from scipy import stats
hit=0
for _ in range(10000):
    d=rng.normal(10,2,30); half=stats.t.ppf(0.975,29)*d.std(ddof=1)/np.sqrt(30)
    hit+=abs(d.mean()-10)<=half
print("coverage",hit/10000)
# Trap 10: p-values when nothing is going on
pv=np.array([stats.ttest_ind(rng.normal(0,1,30),rng.normal(0,1,30)).pvalue for _ in range(10000)])
print("share p<.05",round((pv<.05).mean(),4),"share p<.5",round((pv<.5).mean(),4))
mean 204.5 median 148.1 p95 554.0
ddof0 0.8 ddof1 1.002
coverage 0.9485
share p<.05 0.0512 share p<.5 0.5073

Trap 7: averaging skewed data. The mean waiting time is 204.5 minutes but the typical (median) patient waits 148.1 minutes and 1 in 20 waits more than 554 minutes. Report percentiles as well as the mean. Trap 8: np.var divides by n by default. For samples of 5 it averages 0.8 instead of the true 1; ddof=1 fixes it (pandas .var() already uses ddof=1, numpy does not). Trap 9: reading the interval. Coverage 0.9485 means the method works about 95% of the time; it does not say one particular interval has a 95% chance. Trap 10: p-values under no effect are uniform. 5.1% of null p-values are below 0.05 and 50.7% are below 0.5, as theory says (any p-value is equally likely when nothing is going on). A p-value of 0.04 is therefore not "almost impressive".

COMMON MISTAKE
Believing a trap only hits others. Teams hit these every week. Keep this list next to your analysis checklist.

Check yourself

  1. Why did the top decile fall from 124.8 to 112.5? Answer: part of their first score was luck, which does not repeat.
  2. Does 75% training R² on pure noise mean the model works? Answer: no; test R² was negative.
  3. np.var default divisor? Answer: n (ddof=0).

14.5 Scenario Questions

BASIC EXPLANATION
Scenario questions describe a realistic situation, such as an A/B test or a model result, and ask what to do or conclude. They test the ability to apply statistical ideas to practical decisions.
INTUITION
The final round is usually a business story with a flaw. The task is to ask the questions that expose it.

Problem 14.5.1 "We emailed a coupon to our best customers. Their spending rose 12% next month. The coupon works!" What do you ask?

Step 1Who got the coupon? "Best customers" were chosen by past spending, so regression to the mean may push them down, not up, but seasonality could push them up. Choose neither story; ask for a control.
Step 2Ask for a randomized holdout: of the 10,000 best customers, hide the coupon from a random 1,000. Compare the two groups.
Step 3Ask about the metric (spend or profit after the discount cost) and about the period (a holiday?).
Answer"Without a randomized holdout we cannot separate the coupon from the season or from customers who would have bought anyway. I would hold out a random 10% and compare profit, with an interval."
CheckChapter 12: random assignment balances confounders.
Why it mattersAsking for a counterfactual is the strongest answer in a causal scenario.

Problem 14.5.2 "Model accuracy is 99%. Ship it?" The positive class is 1% of the data.

Step 1A model that always predicts "negative" scores 99% accuracy on this data.
Step 2Ask for precision, recall, a confusion matrix and the cost of each error type.
Answer"Accuracy is misleading with 1% positives. Show precision and recall, and compare against the always-negative baseline before deciding."
Check0.99 × 1 + 0.01 × 0 = 0.99 for the dummy model.
Why it mattersAlways compare with a trivial baseline.

Problem 14.5.3 "Two teams report conversion: Team A 4.8% (480 of 10,000), Team B 5.3% (530 of 10,000). B wins." Do a quick check.

Step 1Difference 0.5 points. Pooled rate = 1,010 / 20,000 = 0.0505.
Step 2SE = √(0.0505 × 0.9495 × (1/10,000 + 1/10,000)) = √(0.04795 × 0.0002) = √(9.59e-6) = 0.003097.
Step 3z = 0.005 / 0.003097 = 1.614; two-sided p = 0.106.
Answerp ≈ 0.106; not significant at 5%. The gap could be noise, and confirming a 0.5 point lift would need about 3 times more data.
CheckSample size for 4.8% → 5.3% at 80% power is about 30,100 per group by the Chapter 12 formula.
Why it mattersA quick z calculation turns an opinion into a number.
REAL JOB TIP
Practise these aloud, with a timer. Explaining in simple English is a skill, and it is the same skill you use daily with non-technical colleagues.

Check yourself

  1. What is the first question for any "X caused Y" claim? Answer: what is the control, or counterfactual?
  2. What baseline should you compare accuracy with? Answer: always predicting the majority class.
  3. When a gap is "not significant", does it mean there is no effect? Answer: no; it may mean too little data.

Practice set

Problem 14.6.1 10 independent tests at 5% with no real effects. Chance of at least one false positive?

Answer: 1 − 0.95¹⁰ = 1 − 0.5987 = 0.4013, about 40%.

Problem 14.6.2 What does np.var([1,2,3,4,5]) print, and np.var([1,2,3,4,5], ddof=1)?

Answer: 2.0 (sum of squared deviations 10, divided by 5) and 2.5 (divided by 4).

Problem 14.6.3 95% CI for a mean: 210 to 250 minutes. Interpret correctly.

Answer: If the study were repeated many times, about 95% of intervals built this way would contain the true mean. It is a statement about the method.

Problem 14.6.4 A test shows p = 0.049 on the primary metric, but the analyst tried 12 metrics. Concern?

Answer: With 12 metrics, the chance of at least one p < 0.05 by luck is 1 − 0.95¹² = 0.46. Correct for multiple testing or pre-register one primary metric.

Problem 14.6.5 Find the bug: "We kept only customers who stayed more than 6 months and found that discount size has no relation with churn."

Answer: Selecting on survival (stayed 6 months) removes the churners and can hide or create relationships (survivorship and collider bias). Use survival analysis on all customers.

Problem 14.6.6 Test A/A on a new platform 100 times. How many "significant" results at 5% should you expect, and when do you worry?

Answer: About 5. Worry if it is far above (for example 20), which signals a broken randomization or analysis.

Problem 14.6.7 The 10 worst-performing schools were given a new teaching plan and improved 30%. Conclusion?

Answer: Unreliable. Extreme performers drift back toward average (regression to the mean). Compare with a randomly chosen control group of similarly poor schools.

Problem 14.6.8 (Interview) A classifier has 95% accuracy. How do you decide whether it is good?

Answer: Compare with the majority-class baseline, look at the confusion matrix, precision and recall at the business threshold, and check on data from a later time period.

Problem 14.6.9 (Interview) Explain Type I and Type II errors using a smoke alarm.

Answer: Type I: the alarm rings with no fire (false alarm, rate α). Type II: a fire and no ring (miss, rate β). Lowering α raises β unless you collect more evidence (more sensors, more data).

Problem 14.6.10 (Interview) Explain why the sample standard deviation underestimates if we divide by n.

Answer: Deviations are measured from the sample mean, which sits closer to the data than the true mean, so squared deviations come out too small by a factor (n−1)/n. Our simulation: 0.8 for n = 5, as predicted by 4/5.
