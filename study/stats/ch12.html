Experimental Design and Causal Basics

How to tell "this change caused that result" from "these two things just moved together".

Your team ships a new caching feature and conversion goes up 14 percentage points among users who turned it on. The manager says, "Great, roll it out to everyone." Should you? This chapter shows you why the honest answer is often "we do not know yet", and what to do about it. You will learn how randomization removes bias, how confounding creates fake effects (you met the idea as Simpson's paradox in Chapter 1), how to size and sanity-check an experiment, and how difference-in-differences gives a fair estimate when you cannot randomize. Testing mechanics (p-values, power, peeking, multiple testing) are in Chapters 4 and 5, so we point to them instead of repeating them. Every number below was computed with numpy and scipy (fixed seeds).

12.1 Correlation, Causation and Potential Outcomes

INTUITION
You want to know if a new route to office saves time. You try the new route on Monday (rainy) and the old route on Tuesday (sunny). The new route took longer. Did the route cause it, or the rain? You can never live both versions of the same Monday. That is the whole problem of causal inference: for each person we see only one of two worlds.

Problem 12.1.1 Four users have these potential outcomes (monthly purchases). We can never see both columns in real life, but here we pretend we can.

(a) What is the true ATE? (b) Suppose only u1 and u3, the keenest users, switch the feature on. What does the naive comparison say?

  • Step 1 ATE = (2 + 0 + 4 + 0) / 4 = 6 / 4 = 1.5 purchases.
  • Step 2 Treated users (u1, u3) show their Y(1): 5 and 6, average 5.5. Untreated (u2, u4) show their Y(0): 4 and 3, average 3.5.
  • Step 3 Naive difference = 5.5 − 3.5 = 2.0.
  • Answer The truth is 1.5, but the naive comparison says 2.0. The extra 0.5 is selection bias: the people who chose the feature were the people who gain most from it.
  • Check The treated would have bought (3 + 2) / 2 = 2.5 without the feature, versus 3.5 for the untreated. They were different kinds of users from the start.
  • Why it matters If users choose the treatment themselves, the groups differ before the treatment even starts. Any difference afterwards mixes the feature effect with that starting difference.
KEY INSIGHT
Randomization fixes this by breaking the link between "who gets treated" and everything else. If a coin decides, the treated and untreated groups are alike on average in everything, including things you never measured.

Applications: A/B tests (Data Science, Product Analytics), marketing uplift, policy and pricing studies (Economics), clinical trials (Healthcare), ML model comparisons in production (MLOps).

COMMON MISTAKE
Comparing "users who used feature X" with "users who did not" and calling the gap the effect of X. Users who chose X differ from the rest in motivation, size and history.

Check yourself

  1. Why can't we see the individual effect Y(1) − Y(0)? Answer: each person is observed in only one world.
  2. What does randomization do to confounders? Answer: it balances them on average between groups, even unmeasured ones.
  3. In Problem 12.1.1, if all four users were treated by a coin flip, what would the average difference approach? Answer: the ATE, 1.5, over many repeats.

12.2 Confounding in Action: a Simulation

INTUITION
Hospitals have more deaths than hotels. Does hospital cause death? No: sick people go to hospitals. "Sickness" sits behind both. In your product, power users both adopt features more and buy more.

We simulate 100,000 users. 30% are power users. Power users adopt the feature with probability 0.7, regular users with 0.2. Power users convert at base rate 0.30, regular users at 0.05. The feature adds exactly +0.02 to everyone's conversion chance. So the truth is known: 0.02.

Predict first: will the naive gap be close to 0.02?

import numpy as np
rng=np.random.default_rng(12)
N=100000
power=rng.random(N)<0.3
adopt=rng.random(N)<np.where(power,0.7,0.2)
base=np.where(power,0.30,0.05)
conv=rng.random(N)<(base+0.02*adopt)
print("share power",power.mean(),"adopt rate",adopt.mean())
print("naive diff",conv[adopt].mean()-conv[~adopt].mean(), conv[adopt].mean(),conv[~adopt].mean())
adj=0
for s in (True,False):
    m=power==s
    a=conv[m&adopt].mean();b=conv[m&~adopt].mean()
    print("power" if s else "regular", m.sum(), a,b,a-b)
    adj=adj+(a-b)*m.mean()
print("adjusted",adj)

Problem 12.2.1 Explain the output and compute the adjusted effect.

Step 1Naive: adopters convert at 21.8%, non-adopters at 8.2%, gap 13.6 percentage points. That is almost 7 times the true 2 points.
Step 2Why? Among adopters, power users are 60.3% of the group (they adopt 70% of the time); among non-adopters only 13.7% are power users. Adopters are mostly the high-converting kind.
Step 3Compare like with like. Inside power users the gap is 0.0223. Inside regular users it is 0.0213.
Step 4Weight by the share of each type (0.30 and 0.70): 0.0223 × 0.2995 + 0.0213 × 0.7005 = 0.0216.
AnswerAfter stratifying by user type, the effect is 2.2 points, close to the true 2.0. The naive 13.6 was mostly confounding.
CheckBoth strata gaps are near 0.02, which matches how we built the data.
Why it mattersStratifying (also called adjusting, or "controlling for") works only for confounders you have measured. If "power user" were not in the data, you could not fix it this way.

  • Answer With a coin, the power-user share is 30.1% in treatment and 29.7% in control, so the groups are balanced. The estimate 0.0182 is near 0.02. The difference from 0.02 is random noise (we only have about 50,000 per group; the standard error of a difference of two proportions near 0.14 with that size is about 0.002).

Observational:  power user ---> adopts feature ---> converts
                     \_______________________________/^
                       (power user also converts more: the back door)
Randomized:     coin ---> feature ---> converts      (the back door is cut)

How to read it: an arrow means "influences". In the observational picture there are two routes from "adopts" to "converts": the direct one we want, and a back-door route through "power user". A coin has no arrows into it except from nothing, so the back door is closed. This kind of picture is a causal diagram (DAG), and the back door idea is the heart of causal reasoning.

Applications: Data Science (why dashboards of "feature users vs non-users" mislead), Machine Learning (training on historical data where past decisions depended on the features, e.g. credit approved only for good customers), MLOps (offline vs online metric gaps), Healthcare (observational drug studies).

COMMON MISTAKE
Adding every available column as a "control". Controlling for a collider (something caused by both treatment and outcome) or a mediator (something on the path from treatment to outcome) can create bias or hide the real effect. Pick controls using a causal story, not by habit.

Check yourself

  1. In 12.2, why was the naive gap so large? Answer: adopters were mostly power users, who convert more anyway.
  2. Does stratification fix unmeasured confounders? Answer: no.
  3. What does the coin do in the randomized version? Answer: it cuts the link between user type and treatment.

12.3 Designing a Good Experiment

INTUITION
An experiment is a fair race. You need runners drawn the same way, a start line that is the same for everyone, and enough runners to separate a small difference from luck. Design means deciding these before the race.

Steps of a design

  1. Question and metric: one primary metric (for example, conversion), decided in advance. Guard-rail metrics (refund rate, complaints) must not get worse.
  2. Unit of randomization: user, session, or something bigger like a store. If the same user can see both versions, results mix.
  3. Randomization: use a random number, or a stable hash of the user ID, so a user always sees the same version.
  4. Sample size: choose from the smallest effect you care about (the minimum detectable effect), the significance level and the power (Chapter 4).
  5. Run for full cycles (whole weeks, because weekdays differ), and do not stop early when the p-value dips below 0.05 (Chapter 5 explained peeking).
  6. Sanity checks: balance of groups, and the sample ratio check below.

Sample size for two proportions

For two groups with rates p₁ and p₂, significance α, power 1 − β:

n per group = ( z_(α/2) × √(2 p̄ (1 − p̄)) + z_β × √(p₁(1−p₁) + p₂(1−p₂)) )² / (p₂ − p₁)², where p̄ = (p₁ + p₂)/2.

Problem 12.3.1 Baseline conversion is 10%. You want to detect a lift to 12% with α = 0.05 (two-sided) and power 80%. How many users per group?

Formulathe formula above, with z_(α/2) = 1.960 and z_β = 0.8416.
Plug inp̄ = 0.11; 2 × 0.11 × 0.89 = 0.1958; √0.1958 = 0.4425. And p₁(1−p₁) + p₂(1−p₂) = 0.09 + 0.1056 = 0.1956; √0.1956 = 0.4423.
Compute1.960 × 0.4425 = 0.8673; 0.8416 × 0.4423 = 0.3722; sum = 1.2395; squared = 1.5364. The gap squared is 0.02² = 0.0004. So n = 1.5364 / 0.0004 = 3,841.
AnswerAbout 3,841 users per group, or about 7,682 in total.
CheckCode gives 3840.85, which rounds up to 3,841. Halving the effect (10% to 11%) needs about 14,751 per group, roughly 4 times more: small effects are expensive.
Why it mattersIf you only have 2,000 users per week, this test needs about 4 weeks. Knowing this before launch avoids "inconclusive" tests.
from scipy.stats import norm
p1,p2=0.10,0.12
za,zb=norm.ppf(0.975),norm.ppf(0.80)
pb=(p1+p2)/2
n=((za*np.sqrt(2*pb*(1-pb))+zb*np.sqrt(p1*(1-p1)+p2*(1-p2)))**2)/(p2-p1)**2

The sample ratio mismatch (SRM) check

You planned a 50/50 split of 100,000 users. You got 50,600 and 49,400. Is that a harmless wobble? Use a chi-square goodness-of-fit test (Chapter 4): χ² = Σ (observed − expected)² / expected.

Problem 12.3.2 Test 50,600 vs 49,400 against an expected 50,000 each.

Formulaχ² = Σ (O − E)² / E, with 1 degree of freedom.
Plug in(50,600 − 50,000)² / 50,000 + (49,400 − 50,000)² / 50,000
Compute360,000 / 50,000 + 360,000 / 50,000 = 7.2 + 7.2 = 14.4.
Answerp = 0.000148. A split this lopsided almost never happens by chance, so something is broken: a bug in assignment, bots, a redirect that loses users in one arm. Do not read the results; fix the pipeline first.
CheckFor 50,100 vs 49,900 the statistic is 0.4 and p = 0.527, a normal wobble.
Why it mattersSRM is the cheapest and most powerful alarm in experimentation. Teams use p < 0.001 as the trigger because they run it on every experiment.
REAL JOB TIP
Run an A/A test (both groups see the same thing) before trusting a new experiment platform. About 5% of A/A tests should show p < 0.05, no more. If you see 20%, the platform is broken.

Blocking and stratified randomization

If you know a strong factor (country, device), randomize inside each block, so both arms get the same mix. It reduces noise. It is the experiment-design cousin of stratified sampling (Chapter 3). When you cannot randomize individuals (for example, a pricing change per city) you randomize clusters and analyze at the cluster level.

COMMON MISTAKE
Stopping the test the day the p-value first dips below 0.05, or testing 10 metrics and reporting the one that "won". Both inflate false positives (Chapter 5). Decide metric, sample size and stop date before launch.

Check yourself

  1. Which three numbers decide the sample size? Answer: minimum effect, significance level, power (plus baseline variance).
  2. 10,300 vs 9,700 in a 50/50 test: SRM? Answer: χ² = 300²/10,000 × 2 = 18, p = 0.00002. Yes, SRM.
  3. Why run whole weeks? Answer: user behavior changes across weekdays; partial weeks bias the result.

12.4 Difference-in-Differences

INTUITION
You cannot randomize, because the new payment flow launched in one region only. Comparing "that region after launch" with "other regions after launch" ignores that the regions were different anyway. Comparing "that region before and after" ignores things that changed everywhere (a holiday, better weather). Difference-in-differences (DiD) uses both: it measures how much the treated region changed, subtracts how much the untreated region changed, and calls what remains the effect.

Setting: average delivery time in minutes; a new dispatch process launched in one region.

GroupBeforeAfterChange
Treated region (new flow)420330−90
Control region (old flow)400370−30

Problem 12.4.1 Compute the DiD effect and compare with the naive answers.

FormulaDiD = (treated after − treated before) − (control after − control before)
Plug in(330 − 420) − (370 − 400)
Compute(−90) − (−30) = −60.
AnswerThe new flow reduced delivery time by about 60 minutes. The "before and after" answer (−90) credits the new flow with the 30-minute drop that happened everywhere. The "after only" answer (330 vs 370 = −40) ignores that the treated region started 20 minutes slower.
CheckWithout the new flow, the treated region would have fallen by the same 30 minutes as control, from 420 to 390. The actual 330 is 60 below that.
Why it mattersDiD removes two kinds of bias: fixed differences between groups, and shared trends over time.

The same number from regression

Regression with an interaction gives DiD as a coefficient: y = b₀ + b₁·treated + b₂·post + b₃·(treated × post). Here b₃ is DiD. We simulate 500 users per cell with the four cell means above plus noise (SD 40):

reg=np.repeat([0,1],2*n);post=np.tile(np.repeat([0,1],n),2)
mu=np.array([[400,370],[420,330]])          # rows: control, treated; columns: before, after
y=mu[reg,post]+rng.normal(0,40,len(reg))
X=np.c_[np.ones(len(y)),reg,post,reg*post]
b=np.linalg.lstsq(X,y,rcond=None)[0]
res=y-X@b; se=np.sqrt(res.var(ddof=4)*np.diag(np.linalg.inv(X.T@X)))
print("coef",b)
print("se",se,"ci",b[3]-1.96*se[3],b[3]+1.96*se[3])

delivery (min)  before          after

Read the picture: each row is one region, left to right is time. The middle arrow shows where the treated region would have gone if it had only followed the control's drop. The gap between that imagined 390 and the real 330 is the effect.

The key assumption: parallel trends. Without the treatment, both groups would have changed by the same amount. You cannot prove this, but you can check that the two groups moved in parallel in several periods before the launch. If they did not, DiD is unreliable.

Applications: policy and pricing changes by region (Economics), feature launches without randomization (Product Analytics), marketing campaigns in some cities, process changes at some depots (Operations), minimum-wage studies.

COMMON MISTAKE
Using DiD when the groups were already moving in different directions, or when something else happened only to the treated group at the same time (a competitor opened). Plot the pre-period trends first.

Check yourself

  1. Treated 50 to 40, control 48 to 45. DiD? Answer: (−10) − (−3) = −7.
  2. What is the main assumption? Answer: parallel trends.
  3. Why not just compare the groups after launch? Answer: they differed before the launch.

12.5 Other Causal Tools, and What You Do Not Need Yet

Randomization is the gold standard. When it is impossible, know the names:

MethodIdea in one lineNeeds
Stratification / regression adjustmentCompare like with like on measured confoundersAll confounders measured
Matching, propensity scoresPair treated with similar untreated usersThe same
Difference-in-differencesChange vs changeParallel trends
Regression discontinuityCompare people just above and just below a cutoff (score 700 gets a loan)A sharp cutoff
Instrumental variablesUse a nudge that affects treatment but not outcome directlyA valid instrument (hard)

Ignore the last two until a project needs them. Also be honest about limits: every observational method rests on assumptions that data cannot fully prove. An interview-ready sentence: "I trust randomized evidence most; for observational data I state the assumptions and check what I can."

PROFESSOR'S NOTE
Causal inference is a huge field. This chapter gives you the vocabulary to ask the right question: "What is the counterfactual, and what could make the groups differ besides the treatment?"

Chapter recap

  • We never see both potential outcomes for one person, so causal effects are estimated by comparing groups.
  • Self-selected treatment creates selection bias; our simulation showed a naive gap of 13.6 points against a true 2.
  • Randomization balances measured and unmeasured confounders; stratification fixes only measured ones.
  • Decide the metric, sample size and stop rule before launch; whole weeks.
  • Check sample ratio mismatch with a chi-square test; run A/A tests.
  • Difference-in-differences = (treated change) − (control change); it needs parallel trends.
  • Pick controls using a causal story; do not control for colliders or mediators.

Practice set

Problem 12.6.1 Treatment group average 5.5, control 3.5, true ATE 1.5. What is the selection bias?

Answer: 2.0 − 1.5 = 0.5.

Problem 12.6.2 In a 50/50 split with 20,000 users you get 10,300 and 9,700. Compute χ² and decide.

Answer: (300² / 10,000) × 2 = 18, p = 0.000022. SRM: do not trust the results.

Problem 12.6.3 Baseline conversion 20%, target 25%, α = 0.05, power 80%. Users per group?

Answer: About 1,094 per group (1,093.7 from the formula, rounded up).

Problem 12.6.4 Treated: 50 before, 40 after. Control: 48 before, 45 after. Find DiD.

Answer: (40 − 50) − (45 − 48) = −10 + 3 = −7.

Problem 12.6.5 What is the output? print(1-0.95**20)

Answer: 0.6415 (the chance of at least one false positive in 20 independent tests at 5%; see Chapter 5).

Problem 12.6.6 Find the bug: "Users who opened the new dashboard retained 20 points better, so the dashboard improves retention."

Answer: Users who chose to open it are more engaged to begin with (self-selection). Randomize who is shown it, or at least adjust for prior engagement.

Problem 12.6.7 A DiD estimate is −60 minutes but treated and control delivery times were already diverging for 6 months before launch. What do you conclude?

Answer: Parallel trends fails, so the estimate mixes the old divergence and the effect. Model the trend, pick a better control, or do not trust it.

Problem 12.6.8 (Interview) Why does randomization let us ignore confounders we never measured?

Answer: A coin assigns treatment independently of every user trait, so on average each trait, measured or not, has the same distribution in both groups. Remaining differences are random and are accounted for by the standard error.

Problem 12.6.9 (Interview) Product wants to stop an A/B test after 3 days because p = 0.03. Your reply?

Answer: The planned sample size was not reached, and checking repeatedly inflates false positives (Chapter 5). Continue to the planned end, or use a sequential method designed for peeking. Also confirm no SRM and that full weeks are covered.

Problem 12.6.10 (Scenario) A new fraud rule launched in India only. How do you estimate its effect?

Answer: Use DiD with similar regions as control. Check parallel trends before launch, make sure nothing else changed only in India, and report an interval.
