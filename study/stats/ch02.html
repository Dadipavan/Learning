Every dataset you will ever analyse was produced by some random process, and a distribution is the shape that describes that process. If you pick the right shape, you can answer questions like "how many orders will be returned in the next hour?", "how long until the next outage?" and "what is the chance that conversion is above 5 percent?" without more data. If you pick the wrong shape, your delivery-promise numbers, your confidence intervals and your forecasts are all wrong. The Math PDF (Pillar 3.2) introduced the common distributions. In this chapter you go deeper: the story behind each one, how to tell them apart, how they connect to each other, and how to simulate them in Python so you can test your intuition. By the end you will be able to look at a column of numbers and say which two or three distributions are sensible candidates.

Our running example is an online shop: orders arrive, some are returned, each takes some time to deliver, and visitors buy or not. Each section picks one part of that story.

2.1 Discrete Distributions: Counting Successes and Events

INTUITION
A discrete variable counts things: failures, arrivals, clicks. Think of a cashier at a counter. How many customers arrive in one minute? How many of the next 20 customers pay by card? How many customers must you serve until the first one complains? Each question has its own distribution, and the question you ask decides which one you need.

Problem 2.1.1 A parcel of 20 independent items is packed and each item is damaged with probability 0.03. What is P(exactly 2 fail), P(at most 1 fails) and P(at least 3 fail)?

FormulaP(X = k) = C(20, k) · 0.03ᵏ · 0.97²⁰⁻ᵏ
Plug inP(X = 2) = C(20, 2) · 0.03² · 0.97¹⁸ = 190 · 0.0009 · 0.5780
Compute190 × 0.0009 = 0.171; 0.171 × 0.5780 = 0.0988. P(X ≤ 1) = P(0) + P(1) = 0.5438 + 0.3364 = 0.8802. P(X ≥ 3) = 1 − P(X ≤ 2) = 1 − (0.8802 + 0.0988) = 0.0210.
AnswerAbout 9.9 percent for exactly 2 failures, 88.0 percent for at most 1 failure, and 2.1 percent for 3 or more.
CheckThe code below gives the same numbers, and the three probabilities for "0 or 1", "2" and "3 or more" add to 1.
Why it mattersEven if each call is 97 percent reliable, about 1 page load in 48 will have three or more failed calls. Capacity and retry designs depend on such tail counts.
from scipy import stats
b = stats.binom(20, 0.03)
print("P(X=2)  =", round(b.pmf(2), 4))
print("P(X<=1) =", round(b.cdf(1), 4))
print("P(X>=3) =", round(b.sf(2), 4), "  (sf = survival function = 1 - cdf)")
print("check sum:", round(b.cdf(1) + b.pmf(2) + b.sf(2), 6))
P(X=2)  = 0.0988
P(X<=1) = 0.8802
P(X>=3) = 0.021   (sf = survival function = 1 - cdf)
check sum: 1.0

Problem 2.1.2 (Poisson arrivals) A service receives on average 4 orders per minute, arrivals being independent. What is P(0 orders in a minute), P(more than 8 in a minute), and how many orders per minute should you staff for so that you are overwhelmed less than 1 percent of the time?

FormulaP(X = k) = e⁻⁴ 4ᵏ / k!
Plug inP(0) = e⁻⁴ = 0.0183
ComputeP(X > 8) = 1 − P(X ≤ 8). We use the CDF in code. For the capacity, we need the smallest c with P(X > c) < 0.01, which is the 99th percentile of Poisson(4).
AnswerP(0) = 1.8 percent, P(X > 8) = 2.1 percent, and the 99th percentile is 9 orders per minute. A capacity of 9 orders per minute is exceeded only 0.8 percent of the time, so it meets the 1 percent target.
CheckSimulate 1,000,000 minutes. The fraction of minutes with more than 9 orders must be below 1 percent (we get 0.8 percent), and the fraction above 8 must be near 2.1 percent.
Why it mattersPoisson tells you that "average 4 per second" does not mean "never above 4". Burst capacity is always larger than the average. This is the same fact that makes a queue grow even when average load is below capacity.
import numpy as np
p = stats.poisson(4)
print("P(0)   =", round(p.pmf(0), 4))
print("P(>8)  =", round(p.sf(8), 4))
print("99th percentile:", p.ppf(0.99), "  P(>9) =", round(p.sf(9), 4))
sim = np.random.default_rng(10).poisson(4, 1_000_000)
print("simulated P(>8):", round((sim > 8).mean(), 4), " P(>9):", round((sim > 9).mean(), 4))
P(0)   = 0.0183
P(>8)  = 0.0214
99th percentile: 9.0   P(>9) = 0.0081
simulated P(>8): 0.0214  P(>9): 0.0082

Problem 2.1.3 (Poisson as limit of Binomial; mean vs variance) A batch has 10,000 records and each is corrupt with probability 0.0005. Compare Binomial(10000, 0.0005) with Poisson(5) for P(X = 8). Then simulate counts to see the mean and variance of each.

FormulaPoisson(λ) approximates Binomial(n, p) when n is large, p is small and λ = np.
Plug inλ = 10,000 × 0.0005 = 5.
ComputeCompare pmf(8) for both and the sample variance.
AnswerBoth give P(X = 8) of about 0.0653, and both have mean 5 and variance about 5.
CheckBinomial variance = np(1−p) = 4.9975, which is almost 5 because 1−p is almost 1.
Why it mattersWhen you only know "average 5 bad records per batch", Poisson is the natural model, and you do not need n or p.
rng = np.random.default_rng(11)
bin_pmf, poi_pmf = stats.binom(10000, 0.0005).pmf(8), stats.poisson(5).pmf(8)
print("binom pmf(8):", round(bin_pmf, 5), " poisson pmf(8):", round(poi_pmf, 5))
xb = rng.binomial(10000, 0.0005, 200_000); xp = rng.poisson(5, 200_000)
print("binom   mean/var:", round(xb.mean(), 3), round(xb.var(), 3))
print("poisson mean/var:", round(xp.mean(), 3), round(xp.var(), 3))

Connection to the real job

Binomial and Poisson are the building blocks of A/B testing (conversions are Binomial), log analysis (error counts per minute are Poisson-like) and queueing models for capacity planning. Multinomial is the model behind text count features and softmax outputs in classification.

Applications

  • Data Analytics: conversion counts (Binomial), support tickets per day (Poisson).
  • Operations: order arrivals, returns per batch, number of attempts until a delivery succeeds (Geometric).
  • Machine Learning: Naive Bayes for counts (Multinomial), Poisson regression (Chapter 8).
  • GenAI: next-token counts and sampling from a categorical distribution.
COMMON MISTAKE
Using Poisson when counts are clumpy. If the variance is far above the mean (for example, errors come in bursts), Poisson will make you under-estimate risk. Compare sample mean and sample variance first.

  2. Binomial(10, 0.5): what is the mean? Answer: 5.
  3. You count "number of trials until the first success". Which distribution? Answer: Geometric.

2.2 Continuous Distributions for Time, Size and Noise

INTUITION
A continuous variable is a measurement on a ruler: time, bytes, money. A single exact value has probability zero, so we talk about density (how crowded the values are near a point) and about intervals. Imagine pouring sand on a line: the height of the pile at a point is the density, and the area under the pile between two points is the probability.

Words you need

Quantile / percentileThe value below which a given fraction lies99th-percentile delivery time
Scale and shapeParameters that stretch the curve and bend its tailWeibull(k, λ)
MemorylessThe future wait does not depend on how long you already waitedExponential

The exponential and Poisson distributions are two views of the same process: if events arrive as a Poisson process with rate λ per second, then the gaps between events are Exponential with mean 1/λ seconds.

Problem 2.2.1 The gaps between customer orders are Exponential with mean 0.25 minutes (rate 4 per minute). What is P(gap > 1 minute), the median gap, and given that you have already waited 1 minute, what is the chance you wait more than 1 more minute (memoryless check)?

FormulaP(T > t) = e^(−λt); median = ln 2 / λ
Plug inλ = 4: P(T > 1) = e^(−4); median = 0.6931 / 4
Computee^(−4) = 0.0183. Median = 0.1733 minutes. Memoryless: P(T > 2 | T > 1) = e^(−8) / e^(−4) = e^(−4) = 0.0183.
Answer1.8 percent of gaps exceed 1 minute, the median gap is 0.173 minutes (smaller than the mean 0.25 minutes because of the right tail), and the chance of waiting another minute is still 1.8 percent no matter how long you have waited.
CheckThe Poisson rate 4 per minute gave P(0 arrivals in 1 minute) = e⁻⁴ = 0.0183 in 2.1.2. "No arrival in one minute" and "gap longer than one minute" are the same event. They match.
Why it mattersSimulation tools that generate random customer arrivals (a queue at a bank, calls to a help line) use exactly this kind of random gap. Memorylessness is also why a "bathtub" failure model needs Weibull instead.
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats
ex = stats.expon(scale=0.25)           # scale = mean = 1/rate
print("P(gap>1)       :", round(ex.sf(1), 4))
print("median gap     :", round(ex.median(), 4))
print("P(T>2 | T>1)   :", round(ex.sf(2) / ex.sf(1), 4))
gaps = np.random.default_rng(12).exponential(0.25, 1_000_000)
print("sim mean/median:", round(gaps.mean(), 4), round(np.median(gaps), 4))

Problem 2.2.2 (fit a distribution to delivery times) We have 2,000 delivery times (minutes). Is Normal or Log-normal a better description? Fit both by maximum likelihood (MLE, the Math PDF Pillar 3.2 idea of choosing parameters that make the data most likely) and compare the log-likelihood and the 99th percentile prediction against the data.

FormulaFor the log-normal, MLE is simple: μ̂ = mean of ln(x), σ̂ = SD of ln(x) (divide by n). For the normal: x̄ and the SD of x.
Plug inTake the logs of the data, compute their mean and SD. Compute the sum of log-densities for each model.
ComputeSee the output. A higher total log-likelihood means a better fit (same number of parameters, 2, so the comparison is fair).
AnswerThe log-normal has a far higher log-likelihood (about −12,045 against −12,481). The normal even puts 3.4 percent of its probability on negative delivery times and under-estimates the 99th percentile (516 minutes against the data's 670; the log-normal says 639).
CheckThe empirical 99th percentile from the data should be close to the log-normal 99th percentile and far from the normal one.
Why it mattersIf you used a Normal model with mean ± 3 SD to set a delivery promise, you would set the 99th-percentile target about 150 minutes too low and promise too much.
rng = np.random.default_rng(13)

print("normal    p99:", round(stats.norm(m, s).ppf(0.99), 1))

Problem 2.2.3 (Weibull and hardware failure) A disk has Weibull lifetime with shape k = 1.5 and scale λ = 4 years. Find the probability of surviving 3 years, the median lifetime, and the hazard (failure rate) at 1 year and at 5 years.

FormulaSurvival S(t) = exp(−(t/λ)ᵏ); hazard h(t) = (k/λ)(t/λ)ᵏ⁻¹; median = λ(ln 2)^(1/k)
Plug inS(3) = exp(−(3/4)^1.5); h(1) = (1.5/4)(1/4)^0.5; h(5) = (1.5/4)(5/4)^0.5
Compute(3/4)^1.5 = 0.6495, S(3) = e^(−0.6495) = 0.5222. Median = 4 × 0.6931^(0.6667) = 4 × 0.7832 = 3.13 years. h(1) = 0.375 × 0.5 = 0.1875 per year; h(5) = 0.375 × 1.1180 = 0.4193 per year.
Answer52.2 percent of disks survive 3 years (0.5223), half are dead by 3.13 years, and the failure rate more than doubles from 0.19 to 0.42 per year, which is the signature of wear-out (k > 1).
CheckWith k = 1 the hazard is a constant 1/λ (exponential). Here k = 1.5 > 1 so the hazard must rise with age. It does.
Why it mattersWeibull is the standard starting model in reliability and in the survival analysis of Chapter 11.
w = stats.weibull_min(c=1.5, scale=4)
print("S(3) =", round(w.sf(3), 4), " median =", round(w.median(), 3))
h = lambda t: w.pdf(t) / w.sf(t)
print("hazard at 1 yr:", round(h(1), 4), " at 5 yr:", round(h(5), 4))
S(3) = 0.5223  median = 3.133
hazard at 1 yr: 0.1875  at 5 yr: 0.4193

Connection to the real job

Choosing between log-normal, Gamma and Weibull for a positive skewed variable is a daily modelling task. Wrong-shape assumptions lead to wrong delivery promises, wrong anomaly thresholds and wrong simulated demand.

Applications

  • Operations: delivery-time models, gaps between orders, failure and lifetime models.
  • Data Science: choosing the response distribution for a GLM (Gamma for positive skewed targets).
  • Finance / Insurance: log-normal claims and prices, Weibull for time to default.
  • Simulation: generating realistic synthetic load and test data.

2.3 The Distributions That Come From the Normal: Chi-square, t and F

INTUITION
You will meet three distributions in almost every statistical test: chi-square, t and F. Do not memorise them as separate objects. They are all built from normal variables. Imagine building with the same Lego bricks (standard normal numbers): stack their squares and you get chi-square, divide a normal by a chi-square-based spread and you get t, divide two chi-squares and you get F. Tests use them because they describe how noisy an estimate is when you must estimate the spread from the data.

Words you need

WordPlain meaningExample
Standard normal ZNormal with mean 0 and SD 1Z-score
Degrees of freedom (df)How many independent pieces of information are availablen − 1 for a sample variance
Critical valueThe cut-off beyond which a test statistic is "rare"1.96 for z at 5 percent two-sided

The recipes

  • Chi-square(k) = Z₁² + Z₂² + ... + Z_k². Mean k, variance 2k.
  • Student t(k) = Z / √(χ²_k / k). Looks like the normal but with heavier tails. When k is large it becomes normal.
  • F(d₁, d₂) = (χ²_{d₁} / d₁) / (χ²_{d₂} / d₂). It is a ratio of two variance estimates, used in ANOVA and in the F-test of regression.

Problem 2.3.1 Show by simulation that Z/√(χ²/k) with k = 5 behaves like a t distribution with 5 degrees of freedom, by comparing the 97.5th percentile and the tail probability beyond 2.

FormulaT = Z / √(V / k), where V is chi-square(k) built from k squared normals
Plug ink = 5. Simulate Z and V = sum of 5 squared normals, 500,000 times.
ComputeTake the 97.5th percentile of T and P(T > 2). Compare with stats.t(5).
AnswerThe simulated percentile is close to the exact 2.571 and P(T > 2) is close to 0.051. For the normal, the same numbers are 1.960 and 0.023.
CheckThe exact t(5) percentile (2.571) is larger than the normal's (1.960), confirming heavier tails.
Why it mattersWhen you estimate the SD from only 6 data points, you must use the wider t cut-offs, otherwise your intervals are too narrow and you will be over-confident.
rng = np.random.default_rng(14)
N, k = 500_000, 5
Z = rng.standard_normal(N)
V = (rng.standard_normal((N, k)) ** 2).sum(axis=1)
T = Z / np.sqrt(V / k)
print("sim  97.5% :", round(np.percentile(T, 97.5), 3), " P(T>2):", round((T > 2).mean(), 4))
print("t(5) exact :", round(stats.t(5).ppf(0.975), 3), " P(T>2):", round(stats.t(5).sf(2), 4))
print("normal     :", round(stats.norm.ppf(0.975), 3), " P(Z>2):", round(stats.norm.sf(2), 4))

Connection to the real job

A t-test (Chapter 4) compares a mean difference to a t distribution. ANOVA and regression F-tests use F. Chi-square is used for tests on counts (Chapter 4) and in deviance tests for GLMs (Chapter 8). You rarely compute these by hand, but you must understand why the library picks them.

Applications

  • Quality engineering: control charts and variance tests.

2.4 Beta and Dirichlet: Distributions Over Probabilities

INTUITION
Suppose 10 visitors saw a new page and 3 converted. What is the true conversion rate? It is not exactly 0.3; it is uncertain. The Beta distribution is a curve over the possible values of a rate between 0 and 1. A tall narrow curve means "we are sure", a wide flat curve means "we know very little". The Dirichlet distribution is the same idea for several rates at once that must sum to 1, like the shares of clicks on 4 buttons. These two are the backbone of Bayesian A/B testing (Chapter 9).

The definitions

  • Beta(a, b) lives on (0, 1). Mean = a / (a + b). Variance = ab / ((a+b)²(a+b+1)). Think of a as "successes + 1" and b as "failures + 1" when you start from a flat view.
  • Dirichlet(α₁, ..., α_m) lives on vectors (p₁, ..., p_m) with sum 1. Mean of pᵢ = αᵢ / Σα. A draw from it is a random probability vector.
  • Beta(1, 1) is the flat Uniform(0, 1). Beta is the Dirichlet with two categories.

2.5 Joint, Marginal and Conditional Distributions; the Multivariate Normal; Transforming Variables

INTUITION
So far we looked at one number at a time. Real data has many columns that move together: order size and delivery time, height and weight. The joint distribution is the full table of how the variables go together. The marginal distribution is what you see if you ignore the other variable (add up the row or column totals). The conditional distribution is what you see after fixing the other variable. A multivariate normal is the bell curve in many dimensions: an ellipse of points described by a mean vector and a covariance matrix.

Key facts

  • Independent variables: joint = product of marginals.
  • For a bivariate normal with means μ, SDs σₓ, σᵧ and correlation ρ: E[Y | X = x] = μᵧ + ρ (σᵧ/σₓ)(x − μₓ) and Var(Y | X = x) = σᵧ² (1 − ρ²). Knowing X reduces the spread of Y by the factor (1 − ρ²).
  • Any linear combination of normal variables is normal.
  • If Y = e^X and X is normal, Y is log-normal. If Z is standard normal, Z² is chi-square(1).
  • MGF fact: the MGF of a sum of independent variables is the product of their MGFs. For a Bernoulli(p), M(t) = 1 − p + p·eᵗ. So the sum of n of them has M(t) = (1 − p + p·eᵗ)ⁿ, which is the Binomial MGF. This is a short proof that sums of independent Bernoullis are Binomial.

2.6 Law of Large Numbers and Markov Chains

INTUITION
If you flip a coin many times, the share of heads settles near 0.5. That is the Law of Large Numbers (LLN): sample averages converge to the true mean as the sample grows. A Markov chain is a process that jumps between states where tomorrow depends only on today (not on the longer past), like a user moving between pages. Run it for a long time and the share of time in each state settles to a stationary distribution. Both ideas say "randomness calms down in the long run", and both are the basis for simulation and for MCMC (Chapter 9).

Definitions

  • LLN: if X₁, X₂, ... are independent with mean μ, then the average X̄ₙ → μ as n → ∞. It says nothing about speed, and it fails for some heavy-tailed laws (Cauchy has no mean).
  • Markov property: P(next state | all past) = P(next state | current state). A chain is described by a transition matrix P where row i lists the probabilities of moving from state i to each state.
  • Stationary distribution π: a probability vector with π P = π. If you start from π you stay at π.
  • Poisson process: events occur independently at rate λ; counts in a window are Poisson, gaps are exponential (see 2.2).

Problem 2.6.1 A website has three page states: Home, Search, Buy. From Home, 60 percent stay Home, 30 percent go to Search, 10 percent go to Buy. From Search: 20 percent to Home, 50 percent stay, 30 percent to Buy. Buy is final-ish: from Buy, 50 percent go Home, 0 to Search, 50 percent stay. Find the long-run share of time in each state.

FormulaSolve π P = π with π₁ + π₂ + π₃ = 1.
Plug inP = [[0.6, 0.3, 0.1], [0.2, 0.5, 0.3], [0.5, 0.0, 0.5]]
ComputeThe stationary vector is the eigenvector of Pᵀ for eigenvalue 1, scaled to sum to 1. We compute it with NumPy and also by multiplying a start vector by P many times.
AnswerIn the long run the chain spends about 46.3 percent of its time in Home, 27.8 percent in Search and 25.9 percent in Buy.
CheckApplying P once more to π must give π back. The code prints the difference.
Why it mattersThis is how PageRank works (a random surfer on the web), and how MCMC samplers are designed (build a chain whose stationary distribution is the one you want).
P = np.array([[0.6, 0.3, 0.1], [0.2, 0.5, 0.3], [0.5, 0.0, 0.5]])

pi = np.real(v[:, np.argmin(abs(w - 1))]); pi = pi / pi.sum()
print("stationary (eigen):", np.round(pi, 4))
x = np.array([1.0, 0.0, 0.0])

print("after 100 steps   :", np.round(x, 4))

Connection to the real job

Markov chains model user journeys, state machines of services, and are the engine inside MCMC samplers. The LLN justifies simulation: "run it 100,000 times and average" works because averages converge.

Applications

  • Data Analytics: customer journey and funnel models.
  • Data Science: PageRank, hidden Markov models, MCMC.
  • GenAI: text generation is a (very long-memory) sequence model; Markov chains are the simple ancestor.
  • Operations: queue and state models, Monte Carlo simulation of demand.
COMMON MISTAKE
Believing that a short run "evens out". The LLN is about the long run. After 10 coin flips, 7 heads is quite normal. There is no force that "corrects" streaks (the gambler's fallacy).

Check yourself

  1. What does the Markov property say? Answer: The future depends on the present only, not the earlier past.
  2. Rows of a transition matrix add up to what? Answer: 1.
  3. Why does the LLN fail for the Cauchy distribution? Answer: It has no finite mean.

Chapter recap

  • Choose a distribution by the story: yes/no (Bernoulli), count of yes in n (Binomial), count of events in time (Poisson), wait for an event (Exponential), skewed positive size (log-normal), lifetime (Weibull).
  • Poisson has mean = variance; if variance is much bigger, the data are overdispersed.
  • Exponential gaps and Poisson counts describe the same process.
  • Delivery and waiting times are typically log-normal; fitting a normal gives negative values and a wrong 99th percentile.
  • Chi-square, t and F are built from normal variables; t has heavier tails and is used when the SD is estimated.
  • Beta and Dirichlet are distributions over probabilities; they express uncertainty about rates.
  • A bivariate normal's conditional mean is a straight line; the conditional variance shrinks by (1 − ρ²).
  • The LLN says averages converge (when a mean exists); Markov chains settle to a stationary distribution.
  • Always check a fitted distribution against the data (percentiles, histogram, log-likelihood).

Problem 2.7.1 X is Binomial(10, 0.5). What is P(X = 5)?

Answer: C(10,5) × 0.5¹⁰ = 252 / 1024 = 0.246.

Problem 2.7.2 The mean of a count variable is 6 and its sample variance is 6.2. Which model first?

Answer: Poisson, because mean and variance are almost equal.

Problem 2.7.3 What is the output? from scipy import stats; print(round(stats.poisson(3).pmf(0), 4))

Answer: e⁻³ = 0.0498.

Problem 2.7.4 Orders arrive as Poisson with rate 12 per minute. What is the mean gap between orders in seconds and P(gap > 10 s)?

Answer: Mean gap = 60/12 = 5 s. Rate per second = 0.2, so P(gap > 10) = e^(−0.2 × 10) = e⁻² = 0.135.

Problem 2.7.5 Find the bug: an analyst sets the late-delivery alert at mean + 3 × SD using a normal model on log-normal delivery times (mean 227, SD 119). What is wrong?

Answer: The threshold is 227 + 3 × 119 = 584 minutes. A normal model says only 0.13 percent of orders exceed it. For log-normal delivery times with median 200 minutes and sigma 0.5, the real exceedance is 1.6 percent, about 12 times more alerts than planned. Use percentiles of the data or a log-scale model.

Problem 2.7.6 Beta(2, 8) represents a click rate. Find the mean. After 10 more visits with 5 clicks, what is the new Beta and the new mean?

Answer: Mean = 2/10 = 0.2. New Beta = (2 + 5, 8 + 5) = Beta(7, 13), mean = 7/20 = 0.35.

Problem 2.7.7 Z is standard normal. What is the distribution of Z₁² + Z₂² + Z₃²? What is its mean and variance?

Answer: Chi-square with 3 df, mean 3, variance 6.

Problem 2.7.8 A Markov chain has P = [[0.9, 0.1], [0.5, 0.5]]. Find the stationary distribution.

Answer: Solve π₁ = 0.9π₁ + 0.5π₂, giving 0.1π₁ = 0.5π₂, so π₁ = 5π₂. With π₁ + π₂ = 1: π = (5/6, 1/6) = (0.833, 0.167).

Problem 2.7.9 (interview) "Delivery-time data has mean 200 minutes and median 150 minutes. What does this tell you about the distribution, and what would you report?"

Answer: Mean greater than median means a right-skewed distribution with a long tail (likely log-normal). Report the median and percentiles (p90, p95, p99), not only the mean.

Problem 2.7.10 (interview) "Why does the t distribution have heavier tails than the normal?"

Answer: The t statistic divides by an estimated SD, which is itself noisy. When the estimate is small by chance, the ratio becomes large. This extra uncertainty gives heavier tails, especially with few degrees of freedom.
