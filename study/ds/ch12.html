Your churn model says customer 4,017 has a 31% chance of leaving. So what? Do we send a 100 rupee offer, call them, or do nothing? Nobody pays a data scientist for the 31%. They pay for the decision, and a good decision needs three more ingredients: what each outcome costs, what limits we face (budget, call-centre minutes, stock), and how sure we are about our inputs. By the end of this chapter you will be able to choose a threshold from a cost matrix, allocate a budget with linear and integer programming, simulate a campaign to get a profit range, and explain bandits and feedback loops in plain words. 12.1 Cost Matrices, Expected Value and Choosing the Threshold

BASIC EXPLANATION
A cost matrix assigns a cost or benefit to each outcome of a decision, such as a true positive or false positive. The expected value of each action then guides the choice of the decision threshold that maximises profit or minimises cost.
INTUITION
A smoke alarm that rings at every toast is switched off by the family. One that stays silent in a real fire is worse. The right sensitivity is not "balanced"; it depends on what a false alarm costs against what a miss costs. Choosing a threshold is the same question for any model.

Words you need.

WordMeaningExample
ThresholdThe score above which we actSend an offer if churn probability ≥ 0.22
Cost matrixA 2×2 table of money for TP, FP, FN, TNTP = +350, FP = −100
Expected value (EV)Probability-weighted average outcomep × 450 − 100
Operating pointThe chosen threshold (or number of cases to act on)Top 273 customers
Break-even probabilityProbability where acting and not acting have equal EV22.2%

The retention-offer decision. We offer a discount that costs 100 rupees to send. If the customer was going to churn, the offer saves them 30% of the time, and a saved customer is worth 1,500 rupees of future profit. If they would have stayed, the offer is just a cost. (This simple story treats the offer as acting only on churners. Real offers also have effects on non-churners; see uplift below.)

Problem 12.1.1 Using the numbers above, find the expected gain of one offer to a customer with churn probability p, and the break-even p.

FormulaEV(send) = p × (0.30 × 1,500) − 100. EV(do nothing) = 0
Plug in0.30 × 1,500 = 450, so EV(send) = 450p − 100
ComputeSet 450p − 100 = 0, so p = 100 / 450 = 0.2222
AnswerSend the offer when churn probability is above 22.2%. Customer 4,017 at 31% has EV = 450 × 0.31 − 100 = +39.5 rupees, so yes, send.
CheckAt p = 0.2222, EV = 450 × 0.2222 − 100 = 0.0. At p = 0.5, EV = +125, which is positive, as expected.
Why it mattersThe threshold came from the business numbers, not from 0.5. If the offer cost doubles to 200, the break-even moves to 44% and fewer customers get it.

Problem 12.1.2 Now run it on data. We train logistic regression on 1,700 of the 2,562 active shop customers (features: days since last order, log of order count, pro plan, ads channel), and evaluate on the other 862.

from ds_common import *
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression
from sklearn.metrics import roc_auc_score

print(len(tr), len(te), "churn rate test", yt.mean().round(3), "AUC", round(roc_auc_score(yt, p), 3))

cost, save, val = 100, 0.3, 1500
def profit(send):
    return (send & (yt == 1)).sum() * save * val - send.sum() * cost
for t in [0.0, 0.1, cost/(save*val), 0.4, 0.6, 0.8]:
    s = p >= t; print(round(t, 3), "sent", s.sum(), "profit", round(profit(s)))

  • Answer Profit peaks near the break-even threshold: 53,250 rupees at 0.222, against 16,400 if we send to everyone and 0 if we send to no one. A sweep over 101 thresholds found the best at 0.23 with 53,450, almost the same.
  • Check At 0.222 the model reaches 179 of the 228 test churners and wastes 94 offers on stayers. The cost matrix is: TP = 0.30 × 1,500 − 100 = +350, FP = −100, FN and TN = 0. Then 179 × 350 − 94 × 100 = 62,650 − 9,400 = 53,250. It matches.
  • Why it matters An AUC of 0.878 sounds like a score; 53,250 rupees on 862 customers is a result. The first one cannot go in a budget meeting.

The picture. Draw profit on the y axis against threshold on the x axis. At threshold 0 you send to all: many wasted offers, profit 16,400. As the threshold rises you drop the customers whose EV is negative and profit climbs. Past 0.22 you start dropping customers whose EV is still positive, and profit falls slowly. The curve is a hill with a flat top: anything between 0.15 and 0.30 is almost as good. That flatness is useful; do not argue about the third decimal.

Fixed budget instead of a threshold. If marketing gives you a budget, take the top K by score (like precision at K in fraud, Chapter 11):

K = 100  profit 27,800  (spend 10,000)
K = 200  profit 46,150  (spend 20,000)
K = 400  profit 50,450  (spend 40,000)

At K = 273 you would match the threshold rule (about 53,250). The value of the last rupee spent falls: going from 200 to 400 offers doubles the spend and adds only 4,300 profit.

Calibration must hold. The expected-value formula multiplies a probability by a value. If the model says 0.84 and the real rate is 0.75, you over-pay. Quintiles on the test set:

Words that look similar and are not.

IdeaQuestion it answers
Churn probabilityWho will leave?
UpliftWho will leave less if we act? (needs an experiment or causal model, Chapter 8)
Value at riskHow much is it worth if we save them?

The EV above used a single "save rate" of 30% for all churners. If an A/B test shows the offer only works on some groups, the threshold should use uplift × value, and "sure things" and "lost causes" drop out. Use the cost matrix thinking; just swap in the right quantity.

Connection to the job. Every ML deployment has this step, from loan approval cut-offs to fraud queues. Writing the cost matrix with the business owner is often the most valuable meeting of the project, because it forces them to say what a mistake costs.

Applications

  • Data Science: threshold choice for churn, fraud, collections, lead scoring.
  • Data Analytics: ROI of campaigns by score decile.
  • MLOps: threshold is a config value; review it when costs change, not only when the model changes.
COMMON MISTAKE
Using 0.5 as the threshold, or "the threshold that maximises F1". Neither knows your costs. Also, do not forget costs that depend on action volume (agent time) or interact (offer fatigue).

Check yourself

  1. Offer costs 60, saves 20% of churners, a saved customer is worth 900. Break-even probability? Answer: 60 / (0.20 × 900) = 60 / 180 = 0.333.
  2. Why can profit be higher at threshold 0.222 than at 0.0, even though 0.0 reaches all 228 churners? Answer: It also sends 634 wasted offers, costing more than the extra churners are worth.
  3. A model is badly over-confident. Which part of the EV formula breaks? Answer: The probability p; the multiplication by value then overstates the gain.

12.2 Optimization: Allocating Scarce Resources

BASIC EXPLANATION
Optimisation allocates limited resources, such as budget or staff, to get the best result. It is formulated as an objective with constraints and solved with methods such as linear programming.
INTUITION
A shopkeeper has 100 rupees of ad budget and three places to spend it. Each place pays back differently and has a cap. She will put money into the best place until it is full, then the next best. That is a linear program: a goal (maximise profit), choices (how much per channel), and rules (budget, caps). Optimisation is just doing that carefully when there are hundreds of choices.

Words you need.

WordMeaning
Decision variableA number we choose (rupees per channel)
ObjectiveThe single number we maximise or minimise (profit, cost)
ConstraintA rule the answer must obey (total ≤ budget)
Linear program (LP)Objective and constraints are straight-line formulas; variables can be fractions
Integer program (MIP/MILP)Some variables must be whole numbers, often 0 or 1 (choose or not)
Shadow priceHow much the objective improves if a constraint loosens by 1 unit

Problem 12.2.1 Three channels return 3.0, 2.2 and 1.5 rupees of profit per rupee. Budget is 100 (thousand). Caps: channel 1 up to 50, channel 2 up to 40, channel 3 up to 60. Contract rule: at least 10 must go to channel 3. Maximise profit.

Step 1Spend the minimum on channel 3: 10. Remaining 90.
Step 2Fill the best channel first: channel 1 gets its cap, 50. Remaining 40.
Step 3Next best, channel 2: its cap is 40, and 40 remains. Channel 2 gets 40. Remaining 0.
ComputeProfit = 3.0 × 50 + 2.2 × 40 + 1.5 × 10 = 150 + 88 + 15 = 253
AnswerAllocate (50, 40, 10) for a profit of 253 thousand. The solver agrees:
from scipy.optimize import linprog
c = -np.array([3.0, 2.2, 1.5])                 # linprog minimises, so negate
res = linprog(c, A_ub=[[1, 1, 1]], b_ub=[100], bounds=[(0, 50), (0, 40), (10, 60)])
print("alloc", res.x.round(1), "profit", round(-res.fun, 1), "shadow price", round(-res.ineqlin.marginals[0], 3))
res2 = linprog(c, A_ub=[[1, 1, 1]], b_ub=[101], bounds=[(0, 50), (0, 40), (10, 60)])
print("budget + 1 ->", round(-res2.fun, 2))
alloc [50. 40. 10.] profit 253.0 shadow price 1.5
budget + 1 -> 254.5
  • Check The shadow price 1.5 says one extra thousand of budget adds 1.5 thousand profit. We confirm: 254.5 − 253 = 1.5. Why 1.5 and not 3.0? Channels 1 and 2 are full, so the extra rupee can only go to channel 3, which pays 1.5.
  • Why it matters The shadow price is the answer to "should I ask finance for more budget?". If a new channel can be bought below 1.5 return, ask. Also, the rule "at least 10 on channel 3" costs us: forced money pays 1.5, but we could earn 2.2 if channel 2 had room.

Integer choices: who gets a call? Now the decision is yes or no per customer. We have 12 at-risk customers with expected values (rupees) and call times (minutes), and 60 minutes of agent time. Choose a set that maximises total value. This is the 0/1 knapsack problem: a bag with limited capacity, items with value and weight.

from scipy.optimize import milp, LinearConstraint, Bounds
rng = np.random.default_rng(1); n = 12
value = np.round(rng.uniform(100, 900, n)); w = rng.integers(5, 30, n)
print("values ", value.astype(int).tolist()); print("minutes", w.tolist())
r = milp(-value, constraints=LinearConstraint(w[None, :], 0, 60), integrality=np.ones(n), bounds=Bounds(0, 1))
sel = np.where(r.x > 0.5)[0]
print("optimal", sel.tolist(), int(value[sel].sum()), int(w[sel].sum()))
order = np.argsort(-value / w); tot = wt = 0; gs = []          # greedy by value per minute
for i in order:
    if wt + w[i] <= 60: gs.append(int(i)); wt += w[i]; tot += value[i]
print("greedy ", sorted(gs), int(tot), wt)
values  [509, 860, 215, 859, 349, 439, 762, 427, 540, 122, 703, 531]
minutes [25, 13, 16, 24, 8, 12, 8, 16, 29, 8, 14, 15]
optimal [1, 4, 6, 10, 11] 3205 58
greedy  [1, 4, 5, 6, 10] 3113 55
  • Answer The best set (customers 1, 4, 6, 10, 11) is worth 3,205 rupees and uses 58 of the 60 minutes. The greedy rule "best value per minute first" gets 3,113, which is 2.9% lower.
  • Check 860 + 349 + 762 + 703 + 531 = 3,205 and 13 + 8 + 8 + 14 + 15 = 58. Both add up and 58 ≤ 60.
  • Why it matters Greedy is a good first answer and often fine. When the gap pays for the effort, use a solver. Never ship a heuristic without measuring its gap to a proper optimum, as we just did.

Assignment: matching people to tasks. Four agents, four tasks, and a cost (minutes) matrix. Each agent gets exactly one task. scipy.optimize.linear_sum_assignment solves it exactly.

from scipy.optimize import linear_sum_assignment
C = np.array([[9, 2, 7, 8], [6, 4, 3, 7], [5, 8, 1, 8], [7, 6, 9, 4]])
ri, ci = linear_sum_assignment(C); print(list(zip(ri.tolist(), ci.tolist())), int(C[ri, ci].sum()))
[(0, 1), (1, 0), (2, 2), (3, 3)] 13

Agent 0 takes task 1 (cost 2), agent 1 takes task 0 (6), agent 2 takes task 2 (1), agent 3 takes task 3 (4). Total 2 + 6 + 1 + 4 = 13. Note that agent 1's cheapest task is 2 (cost 3), but giving it away lets agent 2 take it at cost 1 and the total is lower. Local choices are not global optimum.

The picture. For two variables, imagine a polygon on a graph where each side is a constraint. Every allowed answer lies inside. The objective is a straight line sliding across; the last point it touches is the optimum, and it is always at a corner. That is why LP answers are "channel full or at zero", just like (50, 40, 10).

The wider OR toolbox (not run here, standard tools). Network flow and transportation problems (move goods from warehouses to stores at least cost). Routing (TSP, vehicle routing VRP) with Google OR-Tools. Scheduling with constraint programming. Modelling libraries: PuLP, Pyomo, cvxpy, and commercial solvers such as Gurobi (awareness). Knapsack-like problems stay hard as they grow, so large routing uses heuristics and metaheuristics (local search, simulated annealing, genetic algorithms) that give good, not guaranteed-optimal, answers. Queueing theory (Chapter 11.6) tells you the staff level at which waiting time explodes, an optimisation constraint: keep utilisation below the point where the line starts to explode.

Predict, then optimise. The values in the knapsack came from models (probability × value). Errors in predictions flow into the plan: the optimiser is an "error maximiser", it loves the customers whose value was over-estimated. Mitigate with calibrated inputs, caps, and the simulation in 12.3.

Connection to the job. Budget allocation in marketing, route and slot planning in logistics, shift rosters, ad bidding, portfolio selection, and hospital bed planning all use this machinery. "Predict then optimise" is a very hireable skill pair.

Applications

  • Marketing: budget split with caps and minimums.
  • Logistics: delivery assignment, routing, load planning.
  • Operations: shift scheduling, call-centre staffing.
  • Finance: portfolio and limit allocation.
  • Healthcare and call centres: choosing the number of staff or beds to meet a waiting-time target at minimum cost.
COMMON MISTAKE
Writing the objective without the real constraints (agent minutes, legal minimums, fairness limits), then being surprised that the "optimal" plan is unusable. Review every constraint with the owner and check that the solver result is feasible by hand.

Check yourself

  1. What is a shadow price of 0 for a constraint? Answer: The constraint is not binding; extra of that resource gives no gain.
  2. Why does an LP optimum sit at a corner? Answer: A straight-line objective over a polygon is maximised at an extreme point.
  3. Greedy by value per minute gave 3,113, optimal 3,205. Is greedy wrong? Answer: Not wrong, just 2.9% below the optimum; whether that matters depends on the stakes.

12.3 Simulation: Monte Carlo, What-If and Sensitivity

BASIC EXPLANATION
Simulation uses random sampling to study a system. Monte Carlo simulation estimates outcomes and risks, what-if analysis changes inputs to see effects, and sensitivity analysis shows which inputs matter most.
INTUITION
A weather service does not solve one exact equation for tomorrow; it runs the model many times with slightly different starting readings and reports how often it rains. Monte Carlo simulation does the same for business numbers. If you cannot solve a formula because inputs are uncertain, draw random inputs many times and look at the spread of outcomes.

Words you need.

WordMeaning
Monte CarloRepeat a calculation with random inputs thousands of times
Scenario / what-ifOne specific set of assumptions ("if the save rate is 20%")
Sensitivity analysisChange one input at a time and see how much the answer moves
RiskThe probability and size of bad outcomes (P(loss), 5th percentile)
Discrete-event simulationSimulate events (arrivals, services) in time order, as in a queue

Problem 12.3.1 Our campaign sends 273 offers at 100 each. We are not sure of three things: the true churner share among those sent (about 60%, Beta(60, 40)), the save rate (about 30%, Beta(3, 7)), and the value of a saved customer (about 1,400 rupees, lognormal with spread 0.3). Find the profit range.

Formulaprofit = 273 × churner_share × save_rate × value − 273 × 100
Step 1Draw 100,000 values for each input from its distribution
Step 2Compute profit for each draw
Step 3Summarise: mean, percentiles, and the chance of loss
rng = np.random.default_rng(42); N = 100000; n_send, cost = 273, 100
cs = rng.beta(60, 40, N); sv = rng.beta(3, 7, N); vl = rng.lognormal(np.log(1400), 0.3, N)
profit = n_send * cs * sv * vl - n_send * cost
print("mean profit", profit.mean().round(0), "P(loss)", (profit < 0).mean().round(3))
print("5%, 50%, 95%", np.percentile(profit, [5, 50, 95]).round(0))
mean profit 44614.0 P(loss) 0.104
5%, 50%, 95% [ -7266.  36817. 123503.]
  • Answer The average is 44,614 rupees, but there is a 10.4% chance of losing money, and the 90% range runs from −7,266 to +123,503. The median (36,817) is lower than the mean because a few lucky draws of value and save rate pull the mean up (a right-skewed result).
  • Check The inputs are independent, so the mean of the product is the product of the means. The mean of a lognormal with median 1,400 and spread 0.3 is 1,400 × e^(0.3²/2) = 1,400 × 1.046 = 1,464.5. Then 273 × 0.6 × 0.3 × 1,464.5 − 27,300 = 71,967 − 27,300 = 44,667, close to the simulated 44,614. If we plug in the medians instead we get 38,318 (the "base" line below), about 6,000 lower, because these inputs are skewed (mean above median). So a single plugged-in number depends on which "typical" value you pick. This is a small version of the flaw of averages.
  • Why it matters "Expected profit 45k" and "one chance in ten of losing" are very different messages from "profit 53k". Executives can decide how much risk they accept.

Sensitivity: which input matters? Fix the other two at their medians, move one input from its 10th to its 90th percentile, and record profit (a tornado analysis):

base (all medians)         38,318
churner share  10%..90%    31,334  to  45,022   (range 13,688)
save rate      10%..90%     2,455  to  84,972   (range 82,517)
saved value    10%..90%    17,370  to  69,264   (range 51,894)
  • Answer The save rate dominates: moving it across its plausible range swings profit by 82,517 rupees, six times more than the churner share. Check: 84,972 − 2,455 = 82,517.
  • Why it matters The save rate is an assumption (0.30). Run a small A/B test of the offer before the big rollout (Chapter 7) because that is where the money risk is. The model's AUC barely matters compared to it.

Discrete-event simulation: a queue at a bank counter. Chapter 11.6 gave the formula R = S / (1 − ρ) for the M/M/1 queue. Now simulate it with no formula at all. Each customer waits for the previous one: wait_i = max(0, wait_{i−1} + service_{i−1} − gap_i), a rule called Lindley's recursion. Time in the bank is wait plus service. We simulate 200,000 customers, a service rate of 10 customers per hour (mean 0.1 hour, 6 minutes), at three utilisations. All times below are in hours.

def mm1(lam, mu, n=200000, seed=0):
    r = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    ia = r.exponential(1/lam, n); sv = r.exponential(1/mu, n)
    w = np.zeros(n)
    for i in range(1, n): w[i] = max(0, w[i-1] + sv[i-1] - ia[i])
    resp = w + sv
    return resp.mean(), np.percentile(resp, 95)
for rho in [0.5, 0.8, 0.9]:
    m, p95 = mm1(rho * 10, 10)
    print(rho, "sim mean", round(m, 3), "theory", round(0.1/(1-rho), 3), "sim p95", round(p95, 3))
0.5 sim mean 0.202 theory 0.2 sim p95 0.609 theory p95 0.599
0.8 sim mean 0.505 theory 0.5 sim p95 1.498 theory p95 1.498
0.9 sim mean 1.019 theory 1.0 sim p95 3.005 theory p95 2.996
  • Answer Simulation agrees with theory within about 2%: at 90% utilisation the mean time in the bank is about 1.0 hour and the 95th percentile about 3.0 hours, three times the mean. The theory for the p95 of an M/M/1 queue is ln(20) × S / (1 − ρ), which gives 2.996 × 0.1 / 0.1 = 2.996 at ρ = 0.9.
  • Check The same method works for a system with no formula (two counters, customers who give up, a priority lane). That is the point of simulation. Libraries such as simpy help (not run here).
  • Why it matters A tail percentile is far worse than the mean: 1 customer in 20 waits about 3 hours while the average is 1. Simulation lets you test staffing and capacity plans before spending money.

Agent-based simulation (awareness): simulate many individual actors (customers, drivers) with simple rules and see group behaviour emerge, useful for marketplaces and traffic.

Connection to the job. Finance teams ask for P10/P50/P90, not a point forecast. Operations teams use simulation to test peak-season staffing. Data scientists use bootstrap and Monte Carlo to give intervals on any complicated metric.

Applications

  • Data Science: profit intervals for campaigns, risk quantification for portfolios.
  • Operations: queue and capacity what-ifs.
  • Supply chain: inventory simulations with random demand and lead times.
  • Hospitals and banks: testing staffing plans on simulated arrivals before changing the roster.
COMMON MISTAKE
Choosing input distributions that look fancy but are not based on data, then trusting the output range. Garbage distributions give confident garbage. State where each distribution came from (past campaigns, an experiment, expert judgment) and show sensitivity.

Check yourself

  1. Why can a single plugged-in profit number mislead, even if it is computed carefully? Answer: It hides the spread (here a 10% chance of loss), and it depends on whether you plug in means or medians of skewed inputs.
  2. In a tornado analysis, which input should you study first? Answer: The one with the widest swing (here the save rate).
  3. What is the benefit of simulating a queue instead of using S/(1−ρ)? Answer: It works for systems with no neat formula (customers who give up, several counters, priority lanes).

12.4 Decisions Under Uncertainty, Bandits and Feedback Loops

BASIC EXPLANATION
Decision-making under uncertainty chooses actions when outcomes are not known for certain. Bandits balance exploring and exploiting options, and feedback loops occur when model decisions change the data used to train the next model.
INTUITION
A restaurant owner tries three new dishes. Serving each equally for a month (an A/B test) teaches her the best dish but wastes sales on the bad ones. A smarter owner shifts more tables to the dish that looks best while still trying the others now and then. That is a bandit (named for slot machines, "one-armed bandits"). And once a dish is on the menu, customers' orders change what she sees next, which is a feedback loop.

A/B test as a decision tool. A test result is not "significant or not". It is an input to a decision with costs. A Bayesian view makes this direct. Variant A converted 40 of 1,000; variant B 55 of 1,000. Using a flat Beta(1, 1) prior (Chapter 4), the posteriors are Beta(41, 961) and Beta(56, 946). Draw 200,000 samples from each:

r = np.random.default_rng(0)
pa = r.beta(1 + 40, 1 + 960, 200000); pb = r.beta(1 + 55, 1 + 945, 200000)
print("P(B>A)", (pb > pa).mean().round(3))
print("expected loss choosing B", np.maximum(pa - pb, 0).mean().round(5),
      "choosing A", np.maximum(pb - pa, 0).mean().round(5))
P(B>A) 0.941
expected loss choosing B 0.00024
choosing A 0.01519
  • Answer There is a 94.1% chance B is truly better. If we choose B and are wrong, we lose on average only 0.00024 conversion rate points (0.024 percentage points); if we choose A and are wrong we lose 0.01519 (1.5 points). Choosing B has a much smaller expected loss.
  • Check 0.01519 / 0.00024 is about 63 times. A classical two-sided test gives z = 0.015 / 0.0095 = 1.58, so p is about 0.11 and "not significant". Yet the decision can still be clear when the cost of a wrong choice is this lopsided.
  • Why it matters Decision-based thinking uses expected loss. Pair it with a guardrail (never ship if a guardrail metric drops, Chapter 7).

Multi-armed bandits. With three variants whose true conversion rates are 4.0%, 4.8% and 4.5%, and 20,000 visitors, we compare three policies, averaging 20 runs:

  • Random: equal split forever.
  • A/B then pick: split equally for 6,000 visitors, then send all to the best-looking arm.
  • Thompson sampling: each visitor, draw a plausible rate from each arm's Beta posterior and show the arm with the highest draw. Good arms get more traffic automatically.
random  mean conversions 886.0  share of traffic on best arm 0.333
ab      mean conversions 926.7  share on best arm 0.700
ts      mean conversions 929.8  share on best arm 0.675
oracle (always the best arm)    960.0
  • Answer Both smart policies gain about 41 to 44 extra conversions per 20,000 visitors over random (926.7 and 929.8 against 886.0). Thompson sampling is only slightly ahead of A/B-then-pick (3 conversions).
  • Check Oracle 960 = 0.048 × 20,000. The gap to the oracle is the price of learning: 33 conversions for A/B-then-pick, 30 for Thompson, 74 for random.
  • Why it matters Be honest: in this setup, where the arms differ by less than one percentage point, the bandit's gain over a plain A/B is small. Bandits shine when you have many arms, a short-lived decision (a headline for a day), or a high cost per wasted visitor. They are weaker when you want a clean, unbiased estimate of effect size for a report, and with delayed rewards.

Feedback loops and selective labels. Your model changes the data you will see next.

  • Selective labels: a lender only sees repayment for approved applicants. We simulated 50,000 applicants where a risk score x drives the chance of default, and approved those with x < 0.
true bad rate, all applicants          0.169
observed bad rate, approved only       0.057
slope of bad-odds on x, all            1.225
slope of bad-odds on x, approved only  1.254
  • Read it The observed bad rate (5.7%) is a third of the real one (16.9%). Anyone who sizes the whole market from approved loans under-estimates risk by a factor of three. But the slope (how risk grows with x) is almost the same, 1.254 against 1.225, because we selected on a variable the model sees. Selection on what you observe is harmless to a correctly specified model; selection on something you do not observe (a loan officer's gut feeling) is what biases it. And a model has no information at all about the region it never approves.
  • Remedies: keep a small random "exploration" group (for example approve 5% of the lowest scores under a loss budget) and weight those cases by 1/0.05; use reject-inference methods (Chapter 11.2) with stated assumptions; log every decision and score so future analysis can adjust (not run here).
  • Other loops: a recommender shows popular items, so they get more clicks (position and popularity bias); a fraud model blocks patterns, so fraudsters change them (drift, Chapter 11.3); a churn campaign removes the churners we would learn from. Designs that help: holdout groups that never get the action, regular exploration, monitoring of input drift (PSI), and retraining on recent data.

Connection to the job. Pricing, ads and recommendations are run as bandits at scale. Credit, hiring and healthcare models have selective-label problems that every audit asks about. Interviewers love: "Your model's decisions change future training data. What goes wrong?"

Applications

  • Growth teams: bandits for headline and creative testing.
  • Lenders: exploration with a loss budget and reject inference.
  • Recommendation: exploration slots and position-bias correction.
  • Clinical: adaptive trials (with strong regulatory oversight).
COMMON MISTAKE
Using only the observed (approved, shown, treated) data to judge a model that decides who is approved, shown or treated. You are grading the model on the cases it already liked. Keep a random holdout to measure the truth.

Check yourself

  1. In the bandit run, why does the oracle (960) beat all policies? Answer: It knows the best arm at the start; others must pay to learn which arm is best.
  2. P(B>A) = 0.94, expected loss of choosing B is tiny. Should you ship B? Answer: Likely yes, if guardrail metrics are fine and a wrong choice is cheap; the decision depends on costs, not only on p < 0.05.
  3. The approved-only bad rate is 5.7% vs a true 16.9%. What does this mean for pricing the whole portfolio? Answer: You under-price risk; the unseen rejected population is much riskier.

Chapter recap

  • A prediction needs a cost matrix to become a decision. Break-even probability = cost of acting / gain if right (100 / 450 = 22.2%).
  • Threshold choice beat "send to all" by a wide margin (53,250 vs 16,400 rupees) with the same model. The profit curve has a flat top.
  • Fixed budget means top K by score. Calibration matters because the EV formula multiplies by a probability.
  • LP allocates budget to the best returns up to caps; the shadow price (1.5) tells you the value of one more unit.
  • Integer programs choose who to act on. Greedy was 2.9% below the optimal knapsack (3,113 vs 3,205). Assignment solves matching exactly.
  • Monte Carlo gives the profit range (mean 44,614, 10.4% chance of loss). Tornado analysis showed the save rate dominates. One plugged-in number hides the range (flaw of averages).
  • Simulating an M/M/1 queue reproduces theory (mean about 1.0 hour, p95 about 3.0 hours at 90% utilisation).
  • Bayesian decisions use expected loss. Bandits give modest gains in a 3-arm test (about 44 extra conversions per 20,000). Feedback loops and selective labels (5.7% observed vs 16.9% true) need exploration and holdouts.

Practice set

1. (Easy) Offer cost 50, success rate 25%, value 800. Break-even probability? Answer: 50 / (0.25 × 800) = 50 / 200 = 0.25.

2. (Easy) A cost matrix has TP = +350, FP = −100, FN = 0, TN = 0. With 179 TP and 94 FP, what is the profit? Answer: 179 × 350 − 94 × 100 = 62,650 − 9,400 = 53,250.

3. (Easy) In an LP with a budget constraint, adding 1 unit of budget raises profit by 1.5. What is that number called? Answer: The shadow price of the budget constraint.

4. (Medium) What is the output? linear_sum_assignment(np.array([[1, 5], [5, 1]])) gives which pairs and total cost? Answer: Pairs (0, 0) and (1, 1), cost 1 + 1 = 2.

5. (Medium) A bank counter has a service time of 0.2 hours. Find the mean time in the bank at 75% utilisation and at 95%. Answer: 0.2 / 0.25 = 0.8 hours; 0.2 / 0.05 = 4.0 hours. A 20 point utilisation rise multiplies the time by 5.

6. (Medium) Find the bug: "We set the threshold to 0.5 because it is the default, and report an F1 of 0.71." Answer: The threshold ignores costs. Compute the cost matrix, find the break-even or the profit-maximising threshold, and report profit, not F1.

7. (Medium) Items have (value, weight): A (60, 10), B (100, 20), C (120, 30). Capacity 50. Greedy by value/weight takes A, B, then C does not fit (10 + 20 + 30 = 60). What is the optimal set? Answer: B + C = 220 with weight 50. Greedy (A + B) gives only 160 (A + B weight 30, then C does not fit). Optimal 220 beats greedy 160, so the heuristic can be badly wrong.

8. (Hard) A Monte Carlo of profit gives mean 50,000 but P5 = −20,000. The board wants "at least 90% chance of no loss". Does the project pass? Answer: Not yet. P5 below zero means at least a 5% chance of loss, and it may be more than 10%; compute P(profit < 0) directly. If it exceeds 10%, run a pilot to narrow the uncertain input.

9. (Hard) A lender sees a 4% default rate on approved loans and plans to approve 3 times more applicants using the same model. What is the risk? Answer: The approved-only rate does not describe the new, riskier applicants (selective labels). Start with a controlled expansion plus exploration, and estimate the rate with reject inference with stated assumptions.

10. (Interview) "You have a churn model with AUC 0.88. How do you decide who gets the retention offer?" Answer: Check calibration; write the cost matrix with the owner (offer cost, success rate, value); compute break-even probability; sweep thresholds for profit; apply budget and capacity limits; test the save rate by A/B; consider uplift modelling; keep a holdout; monitor.

11. (Interview) "What is the difference between an A/B test and a bandit? When would you use each?" Answer: A/B: fixed split, clean unbiased effect estimate, good for decisions that need evidence and a report. Bandit: adapts traffic to the better arm while learning, good for many options, short-lived content, or a high cost of showing a bad option. In a 3-arm test with small differences the gain over A/B was only a few conversions in 20,000.
