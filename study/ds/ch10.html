Recommendation, Ranking and Search

How to decide what to show each user: baselines, similarity, matrix factorization, the two-stage design used in industry, the right metrics, cold start and search relevance.

Open any shopping, music or video app. Hundreds of thousands of items exist, the screen shows ten. Someone, or rather some system, chose those ten for you, and that choice drives a large part of the company's revenue. Building such a system is a mix of data science, engineering and product thinking. The hard parts are not the algorithms. They are the data (you only see what was shown), the evaluation (offline numbers often disagree with online results) and the scale (you cannot score 10 million items for every request). By the end of this chapter you will be able to build a working recommender from scratch, compare it with honest baselines using ranking metrics (Precision@K, Recall@K, NDCG, MRR), explain the two-stage "candidate then rank" architecture with numbers, handle new items (cold start), spot position bias, and score documents for a search query with BM25. The algorithms behind gradient boosting and neural nets live in the ML document (study/ml/); here we only use them.

Running example. A shopping app with 2,000 users and 600 items. We generated interactions ("user bought or clicked item") from hidden taste factors plus an item popularity effect, with a fixed seed (default_rng(10)). The result is 37,347 interactions: 18.7 per user, a density of 3.1%. Each interaction has a timestamp in [0, 1). We train on the first 80% of time (28,638 interactions) and test on the last 20% (8,709). The last 30 items launch only at t = 0.8, so they are brand new in the test period. All outputs below are real. Libraries implicit, LightFM, Surprise, faiss and lightgbm were not installed, so we wrote ALS and similarity in numpy, and used scikit-learn's HistGradientBoostingClassifier as the ranker.

10.1 Problem Types, Feedback Data and the Baselines

INTUITION
A good shop assistant does three things. They know what is popular. They know what people like you bought. And they notice what you looked at today. A recommender automates those same three ideas, but it only ever sees what customers did, not what they would have liked.

Problem types

TypeQuestionExample
Top-N recommendationWhich N items for this user?"Recommended for you" row
Rating predictionWhat star rating would the user give?Old Netflix prize style
Next-item / sequenceWhat comes next after these clicks?"Up next" video
Similar itemsItems like this one"Customers also viewed"
Search rankingOrder results for a queryProduct search
Feed rankingOrder a mixed feedSocial feed

Today most work is top-N and ranking, not rating prediction, because users rarely rate.

Feedback data

Explicit feedback means the user says what they like (a 5-star rating). It is clear but rare. Implicit feedback is behaviour: click, view, purchase, watch time. It is plentiful but noisy: a click is not a like, and "no interaction" does not mean dislike (the user may never have seen the item).

Three biases to know now:

  • Exposure bias: users can only interact with what the system showed. Items never shown look "disliked".
  • Popularity bias: popular items get more data and more exposure, so they get recommended even more.
  • Position bias: items shown at the top get more clicks just because they are at the top (Section 10.4).

In our data, the top 10% of items (60 items) hold 33.7% of training interactions.

The baselines

Problem 10.1.1 (tiny numbers) Three users bought: Asha {shoes, socks}, Ravi {shoes, bag}, Meena {shoes, socks, hat}. Recommend one item to a new user who just bought shoes, with (a) popularity and (b) item-item co-occurrence.

Step 1 (popularity)Counts: shoes 3, socks 2, bag 1, hat 1. Excluding items the user already has, the top is socks (2).
Step 2 (co-occurrence with shoes)Customers who bought shoes also bought: socks 2 times (Asha, Meena), bag 1, hat 1. The top is socks.
Step 3 (cosine similarity)The normalised version divides by the geometric mean of the two popularity counts. sim(shoes, socks) = 2 / √(3 × 2) = 0.816, sim(shoes, bag) = 1 / √(3 × 1) = 0.577.
AnswerBoth recommend socks here; cosine does not just reward the most popular item (shoes), it also adjusts for popularity.
CheckCosine is between 0 and 1; 1 would mean the two items are always bought together.
Why it matters"Customers who bought this also bought" is item-item co-occurrence. It is simple, fast and strong.

Cosine similarity of two item columns a, b of the 0/1 user-item matrix: sim(a, b) = (a · b) / (‖a‖ ‖b‖). For a user, the score of an unseen item is the sum of its similarities to the items the user already has: score = X · Sim, where X is the user-item matrix.

Content-based and kNN

  • Content-based: describe items by features (text TF-IDF, category, embeddings) and recommend items similar to those the user liked. It needs no other users, so it works for new items.
  • User-user kNN: find the 50 users most similar to you and recommend what they bought.
  • Item-item kNN: as above; usually the more stable.

Metrics for ranking

For one user with a recommended list of K items and a set of items they really interacted with later ("relevant" items):

MetricMeaning
Precision@Kshare of the K shown that are relevant
Recall@Kshare of all relevant items that appear in the K
Hit rate@K1 if at least one relevant item is in the K, else 0
MRR1 / rank of the first relevant item
NDCG@Ka score that gives more credit to relevant items near the top
Coverageshare of the catalogue that is ever recommended
Diversity, novelty, serendipityhow different, unfamiliar and pleasantly surprising the list is

NDCG (normalised discounted cumulative gain): each relevant item at position r earns 1 / log₂(r + 1). Add them (DCG), then divide by the best possible DCG (IDCG) so the score is between 0 and 1.

Problem 10.1.2 A list of 5 items. Relevant flags by position: [0, 1, 0, 1, 0]. The user has 2 relevant items in total. Compute NDCG@5, Precision@5 and MRR.

FormulaDCG = Σ rel / log₂(position + 1)
Plug inPosition 2: 1 / log₂(3) = 0.6309. Position 4: 1 / log₂(5) = 0.4307.
ComputeDCG = 0.6309 + 0.4307 = 1.0616. The ideal list has both relevant items at positions 1 and 2: IDCG = 1 + 0.6309 = 1.6309. NDCG = 1.0616 / 1.6309 = 0.651.
AnswerNDCG@5 = 0.651, Precision@5 = 2/5 = 0.40, MRR = 1/2 = 0.5.
CheckIf the relevant items were at positions 1 and 2, NDCG would be exactly 1.
Why it mattersPrecision does not care where the hits are. NDCG does, and users mostly look at the top.

Check yourself

  1. What is the difference between Precision@K and NDCG@K? Answer: NDCG rewards hits near the top; precision ignores the positions.
  2. Why can cosine beat raw co-occurrence? Answer: it divides by item popularity, so popular items are not always on top.
  3. Is a click an explicit or implicit signal? Answer: implicit.

10.2 Matrix Factorization

INTUITION
Imagine each customer has a hidden taste profile (loves sport, hates formal wear) and each item has a hidden style profile. If the two profiles point the same way, the customer will probably like the item. We never see profiles, but we can learn them from who bought what. Matrix factorization (MF) learns a short list of numbers (a latent factor vector) for each user and each item, so that the dot product of a user's vector and an item's vector predicts the interaction.

How it works

The user-item matrix X has 2,000 rows and 600 columns, almost all zeros. MF finds a user matrix P (2,000 × f) and an item matrix Q (600 × f) with f small (say 16) so that X ≈ P · Qᵀ. The predicted score of item i for user u is P[u] · Q[i].

Methods:

  • SVD (singular value decomposition): the exact best low-rank approximation of the matrix. It treats every empty cell as a real zero.
  • ALS (alternating least squares) for implicit feedback: gives each observed interaction a higher confidence c = 1 + α·x and solves for P with Q fixed, then Q with P fixed, again and again. Each solve is a small linear system: p_u = (QᵀCᵤQ + λI)⁻¹ QᵀCᵤ x_u. λ (regularisation) keeps the vectors small.
  • BPR (Bayesian personalised ranking): learns by pairs: "this user prefers the item they bought over a random item they did not". It optimises ranking directly. Not run here.
  • Factorization machines: extend the dot product to include side information such as category and device. Not run here.

Problem 10.2.1 (tiny numbers, by hand) User vector [1.0, 0.5]. Item A vector [0.8, 0.2], item B vector [−0.3, 0.9]. Which item scores higher?

Formulascore = Σ (user factor × item factor)
Plug inA: 1.0 × 0.8 + 0.5 × 0.2. B: 1.0 × (−0.3) + 0.5 × 0.9.
ComputeA = 0.8 + 0.1 = 0.90. B = −0.3 + 0.45 = 0.15.
AnswerItem A (0.90) beats item B (0.15) for this user.
CheckThe user's first factor is large and A's first factor is large, so the dot product is large. That is what "pointing the same way" means.
Why it mattersScoring every item for one user is a matrix-vector product; at serving time a vector index finds the top items without scoring all of them (Section 10.3).

CheckALS recall@10 = 0.1505 and SVD rank-5 = 0.1502. The two models agree on the broad structure.

Connection to the real job

MF is the classic "model-based collaborative filtering". Its learned item vectors (embeddings) are also used for "similar items" lists and as input features to a ranking model.

Applications

  • Music and video: user and item embeddings for "similar to".
  • Retail: next-basket ideas.
  • Ads: click-through prediction with factorization machines.
  • Banking: product propensity.
COMMON MISTAKE
Choosing a factor count or regularisation on the test set. Tune on a validation period earlier than the test period, then evaluate once on the test.

Check yourself

  1. What is a latent factor? Answer: a learned hidden number describing a taste or style.
  2. What does ALS alternate between? Answer: solving for the user vectors with items fixed, then item vectors with users fixed.
  3. Why λ? Answer: to prevent overfitting by keeping the vectors small.

10.3 The Two-Stage Architecture, Learning to Rank and Evaluation Traps

INTUITION
A recruiter with 50,000 CVs does not read each one in depth. First a quick filter shortlists 100 who look plausible. Then a careful interview ranks those 100. Recommenders work the same way: a cheap stage must not miss the good items, and an expensive stage orders them well.

The stages

 all items (millions)
        |
        v
 1. CANDIDATE GENERATION   fast, recall-focused, vector search (ANN)   -> ~100-1000 items
        |
        v
 2. RANKING                precise, many features, ML model            -> ordered list
        |
        v
 3. RE-RANKING             diversity, business rules, de-duplication   -> final ~10 items
  • Candidate generation can use several sources at once (popular items, item-item neighbours, MF top-k, recently viewed). Vector retrieval uses approximate nearest neighbour (ANN) search (libraries like faiss): not run here, because we only have 600 items, so we score all of them by brute force. The key metric of this stage is recall@N: "is the item the user wants inside the shortlist?"
  • Ranking uses a model that sees features of the user, the item and the pair (history, price, time of day, the MF score).
  • Re-ranking adds rules: do not show five near-identical items, boost new sellers, remove items out of stock.

Learning to rank trains the ranker on lists. Pointwise predicts one score per item (like our classifier). Pairwise learns "A should rank above B" (RankNet). Listwise optimises a list metric directly (LambdaMART in LightGBM's ranker is the industry classic: not run here). Two-tower neural networks learn a user vector and an item vector separately and take their dot product, which allows precomputed item vectors and ANN retrieval. Sequence models (GRU4Rec, SASRec/transformers) use the order of recent clicks.

Problem 10.3.1 How large must the candidate list be?

ALS recall@10   0.150

  • Step 2 At 200 candidates the ceiling is 0.629, but the ranker has to score twice as many.
  • Answer The candidate stage sets the ceiling. Choose N from the recall curve and your latency budget (here, 100 is a reasonable compromise).
  • Check Recall must rise with N; it does (0.15, 0.37, 0.49, 0.63).
  • Why it matters Engineers often tune only the ranker. If the candidate stage misses the item, no ranker can recover it.

  • Check The ranker was trained on an earlier window than the test window, so no leakage.
  • Why it matters Two-stage architecture is what you describe in a system-design interview; the improvement comes mostly from adding rich features in stage two.

Evaluation traps

Random vs time split. Random splits let the model see future interactions. With item-item cosine, switching from a time split to a random 80/20 split on the same data inflated Recall@10 from 0.183 to 0.224 (+22%) and NDCG@10 from 0.167 to 0.190. A model must be tested on the future, like in forecasting (Chapter 9).

Offline versus online. Offline metrics measure how well you predict the past behaviour that the OLD system caused. The new system can differ. Use offline metrics to filter ideas, then A/B test (Chapter 7). Other tools: interleaving (mix the results of two rankers in one list and see which gets the clicks; needs far fewer users than an A/B test) and counterfactual evaluation with inverse propensity scoring (IPS, Chapter 8).

Connection to the real job

"Design a recommendation system" is a standard interview. Draw the three stages, name the metric of each stage, state the baseline, the data split and the online test.

Applications

  • E-commerce: home page and cart recommendations.
  • Video/music: multi-source candidates then a neural ranker.
  • Job portals: candidate-to-job matching.
  • Ads: candidate ads then click-through-rate ranking.
COMMON MISTAKE
Leaking the future in the ranker's training: using item popularity computed over ALL time, including the test period. Compute every feature as of the prediction time.

Check yourself

  1. What is the metric of the candidate stage? Answer: recall@N.
  2. Why can the ranker not fix a bad candidate list? Answer: it only reorders the candidates it receives.
  3. What is interleaving? Answer: showing a mixed list from two rankers and counting which one gets more clicks.

10.4 Cold Start, Position Bias and Search

INTUITION
A new employee has no history and nobody knows their strengths. You can read the CV (content), ask the manager for a reference (similar people), or give them a trial project (explore). New users and new items need the same three tricks.

Cold start

Cold start means a new user or new item with no interactions. Item-item and MF models assign it no score, so it is never shown, and so it never gets data. A vicious circle.

Remedies:

  • Content features: learn a mapping from item attributes to the factor vector.
  • Popularity or recent popularity as a default for new users.
  • Onboarding questions: ask three taste questions at signup.
  • Exploration or bandits (Chapter 7): show new items to a small share of users to collect data.

Problem 10.4.1 In our data, 20% of test interactions are on the 30 brand-new items (about 0.86 of 4.35 test interactions per user). For those items the history models have nothing. We learn a ridge regression that maps each item's (noisy) content features to its ALS vector, trained on the 570 warm items, and predict vectors for the 30 cold items. We evaluate only on the cold items (ranking the 30).

cold items only         Precision@10   Recall@10

  • Step 1 A random order among 30 items puts 10 on the list and finds 35% of relevant items.
  • Step 2 Content-mapped factors find 75% and double the precision.
  • Answer Mapping from item attributes to the model's space gives new items sensible scores on day one, before any interaction exists.
  • Check Random recall of 0.351 is close to 10/30 = 0.333, as expected by chance.
  • Why it matters Cold start is where the content model earns its keep. In production you blend both: content-based for new items, collaborative for items with history.

Position bias and counterfactuals

Users look at the top more. A click at position 1 is partly "it was at the top". Our simulation: item A has a true click rate of 10% when examined. The chance of being examined is 1.0 at position 1 and 0.4 at position 2. Item A is placed at either position with equal chance (200,000 impressions).

  • Observed click rate at position 1: 9.8%. At position 2: 3.9%.
  • The simple overall average: 6.9%, which understates the item's true appeal of 10%.
  • IPS correction: weight each click by 1 / examination probability. Corrected rate: 9.8%, near the true 10%.

Position bias is a feedback loop: whatever you put on top collects clicks, and then the model learns to put it on top. Solutions: randomise a small share of positions, include position as a training feature (and set it to a fixed value at serving), or use IPS weighting. Other ethical points: filter bubbles (users see only more of the same), fairness to sellers and creators, and manipulation by fake clicks.

Search: BM25 and relevance

Search ranking has a query instead of a user. The classic first stage is BM25, a keyword score. For a query word w in document d:

idf(w) = ln(1 + (N − df + 0.5) / (df + 0.5))
term score = idf × tf × (k1 + 1) / (tf + k1 × (1 − b + b × len(d) / avglen))
BM25(q, d) = sum of term scores over query words

N is the number of documents, df the number of documents containing the word, tf the count of the word in this document. Rare words count more (idf), repeated words help but with diminishing returns (k1), and long documents are discounted (b). Typical values are k1 = 1.5, b = 0.75.

Problem 10.4.2 Four product titles: (1) "cheap red running shoes", (2) "blue running shoes for men", (3) "red leather office bag", (4) "running shorts red". Query: "red running". Score document 4.

Step 1N = 4. "red" appears in 3 documents, "running" in 3. idf = ln(1 + (4 − 3 + 0.5) / (3 + 0.5)) = ln(1.4286) = 0.3567.
Step 2The titles have 4, 5, 4 and 3 words, so the average length is (4 + 5 + 4 + 3) / 4 = 4.0 (our code printed the same).
Step 3Document 4 has 3 words. Each term: tf = 1. 1 − b + b × 3/4 = 0.25 + 0.5625 = 0.8125. Denominator: 1 + 1.5 × 0.8125 = 2.21875. Term score = 0.3567 × 2.5 / 2.21875 = 0.4019.
Step 4Two query words: 2 × 0.4019 = 0.8038.
AnswerReal output: doc 4 = 0.804, doc 1 = 0.713, doc 3 = 0.357, doc 2 = 0.321. The shortest title with both words ranks first.
CheckDocument 3 contains only "red" (0.357), doc 2 only "running" (0.321, longer and so discounted more).
Why it mattersBM25 is the strong, cheap baseline that modern search still uses as a first stage.

A modern search stack: query understanding (spelling, synonyms, intent) → retrieval (BM25 plus vector search on text embeddings, combined as hybrid search) → learning-to-rank model using click logs and relevance judgments (human labels such as "perfect / good / bad") → evaluation with NDCG. Embedding models and vector databases are covered in the GenAI part of the plan.

Connection to the real job

Search teams spend as much time on judgments and query logs as on models. Measuring search quality means fixed query sets with graded labels, NDCG, and online tests on click and conversion.

Applications

  • E-commerce search: BM25 plus embedding retrieval, ranker with price and popularity.
  • Job and property portals: query plus filters plus personalisation.
  • Customer support: retrieval of the best help article.
  • Enterprise: document search with hybrid retrieval.
COMMON MISTAKE
Judging a new ranker only by offline NDCG against labels collected from the old ranker's top results. Items the old system never showed have no labels, so the new system is punished for being different. Add fresh judgments, interleaving or an A/B test.

Check yourself

  1. Name two ways to handle a new item. Answer: content-based features, and exploration (showing it to a small share of users).
  2. What does IPS do? Answer: weights observations by 1 / probability of being seen, to undo position (exposure) bias.
  3. What does a high idf mean? Answer: the word is rare, so it is informative.

Chapter recap

  • Recommenders choose a few items from many. Most work today is top-N ranking from implicit feedback.
  • Implicit feedback has exposure, popularity and position bias. "No interaction" is not "dislike".
  • Start with baselines: popularity and item-item cosine. In our test item-item (NDCG 0.167) beat popularity (0.065) and MF (0.132).
  • Matrix factorization learns user and item vectors; the score is their dot product. Tune it and compare it to the baselines.
  • Two-stage systems: candidate generation (recall, ANN), ranking (precision, features), re-ranking (rules). Recall@N of the first stage caps everything after.
  • Use ranking metrics (NDCG, MRR, Recall@K), plus coverage and diversity. Evaluate on a time split; random splits inflated recall by 22%.
  • Offline metrics filter ideas; A/B tests decide. Interleaving and IPS help with cheaper or counterfactual evaluation.
  • Cold start: content features, popularity, onboarding questions and exploration. Mapping content to factors doubled precision on new items.
  • Position bias creates feedback loops; correct with IPS or randomisation.
  • Search starts with BM25 (rare words matter, long documents are discounted), then adds vector retrieval and learning to rank.

Practice set

1. (easy) Recommended list of 10, 3 relevant. Precision@10? Answer: 3/10 = 0.30.

2. (easy) First relevant item at position 4. Reciprocal rank? Answer: 1/4 = 0.25.

3. (easy) User vector [2, 1], item vector [0.5, 3]. Score? Answer: 2 × 0.5 + 1 × 3 = 4.

4. (medium) User has 4 relevant future items. The top-10 list contains 2 of them. Recall@10 and Precision@10? Answer: Recall 2/4 = 0.50; Precision 2/10 = 0.20.

5. (medium) Cosine between item A (bought by users {1, 2, 3, 4}) and item B (bought by {3, 4, 5}). Answer: Overlap = 2. 2 / √(4 × 3) = 2 / 3.464 = 0.577.

6. (medium, what is the output) Relevance flags [1, 0, 0] with 1 relevant item in total. NDCG@3? Answer: DCG = 1, IDCG = 1, so NDCG = 1.0.

7. (medium, find the bug) A team evaluates with a random 80/20 split of interactions, and the model gets Recall@10 of 0.45. After launch, the online hit rate is far lower. Why? Answer: The random split leaks the future (and includes items that had no history online), inflating the offline score; use a time-based split and an A/B test.

8. (hard) The candidate generator has recall@100 of 0.40 and the ranker has a perfect ordering. What is the maximum possible recall@10? Answer: At most 0.40, and it could be lower if there are more than 10 relevant candidates; the ranker cannot recover items the first stage dropped.

9. (hard) Position 3 has examination probability 0.2 and an item got 30 clicks from 1,000 impressions at that position. IPS-corrected click rate? Answer: Raw rate 3.0%. Divide by 0.2: 15%.

10. (hard, scenario) A new streaming app launches with 5,000 users and 300 titles, with 3 interactions per user. What approach? Answer: Start with popularity (recent) and content-based similarity, ask taste questions at signup, use item-item cosine once data accumulate, add exploration for new titles, and move to MF or two-stage later when there are enough interactions.

11. (interview) "Design a recommender for an e-commerce home page." Answer: Define goal and metrics (conversion, revenue, guardrails), data (clicks, purchases, with time), baselines (popularity, item-item), candidate generation from multiple sources with recall@N, a ranker with user-item-context features, a re-ranker for diversity and stock, time-split offline evaluation, cold-start plan, then an A/B test with long-term metrics.

12. (interview) "Offline NDCG improved 10% but the A/B test is flat. Why?" Answer: Offline data come from the old system's exposure, so improvements may be on items the old system already showed; position and popularity bias; the metric (NDCG on clicks) may not match the business metric (revenue, retention); effect may be too small for the test's power; or novelty effects. Check by interleaving, counterfactual evaluation, and metric alignment.
