Many good models die in a folder called "final_v3_REAL". The reason is rarely maths. The manager did not understand the slide, the engineer could not rerun the notebook, the risk team found a fairness problem on the day of launch, or nobody noticed the model was broken for six weeks. Senior data scientists are paid largely for avoiding these outcomes. In this chapter you will learn to write an answer-first memo, explain a model with a counterfactual instead of a formula, manage a vague or oversized request, run a basic fairness and proxy check, make a project rerunnable with one command, catch training-serving skew, and write a handover and monitoring plan. We use the shop churn model from Chapters 1 and 12 (from ds_common import *). Code outputs are real runs (numpy, pandas, scikit-learn, seed 42 data). The snippets assume d, X, y and the cost-based threshold are built as in Chapter 12.1 (features rec, logf, pro, ads) and that p holds the model scores. The FastAPI and Streamlit snippets are standard but "not run here".

13.1 Communicating Results: Answer First, Uncertainty Honest

BASIC EXPLANATION
Communicating results means presenting findings so that the audience can act. This means stating the answer first, showing the key evidence and being honest about uncertainty and limits.
INTUITION
You are the pilot's voice on the intercom. Passengers do not want the engine temperature log. They want: "We will land 20 minutes late, because of wind, and here is what that means for connections." Start from the decision. Then give the evidence. Then the risk. The same order works for slides, emails and interviews.

Words you need.

WordMeaningExample
Answer first (BLUF)State the recommendation in the first line"Send the offer to 273 customers"
Counterfactual explanation"If X were different, the result would change by Y""If she had ordered 10 days ago, risk falls from 31% to 18%"
Model cardA 1 to 2 page record of what a model is for, how it was tested, and its limitsSection 13.4 template
GuardrailA metric that must not get worseUnsubscribe rate
Handover docWhat the next owner needs to run and fix your workREADME plus runbook

The one-page memo. Use this shape for any analysis. Each part has one job.

Title: one sentence that is the conclusion, not the topic
1. Recommendation (2 lines)   What should we do, by whom, by when?
2. Why (3 bullets)            The three facts that matter most, each with a number
3. How sure are we?           Range, assumptions, what would change my mind
4. Risks and next steps       What could go wrong, what we will measure
5. Appendix                   Method, data, links to code (readers can skip)

Problem 13.1.1 Turn this raw finding into a memo opening. Raw: "Logistic regression on 1,700 customers, AUC 0.878, threshold 0.22, test profit 53,250 rupees, save rate assumed 30%, 10.4% chance of loss in simulation (Chapter 12)."

Step 1Find the decision: who gets the offer?
Step 2Put one money number and one risk number in the opening line.
Step 3Keep AUC out of the first lines. It goes in the appendix.
Answer"Recommendation: send the retention offer to the 273 highest-risk customers (about 32% of the active base). We expect about 53,000 rupees of extra profit on the test sample. The main uncertainty is whether the offer really saves 30% of churners: if the save rate is lower, there is about a 1 in 10 chance we lose money. We propose a two-week A/B test on 20% of the list first."
CheckEvery number in the paragraph appears in Chapters 12.1 and 12.3 (273 of 862 customers is 31.7%, profit 53,250, P(loss) 0.104). A reader can act on it without knowing what AUC means.
Why it mattersExecutives decide in the first 30 seconds of reading. Put the decision there.

Explain a model with counterfactuals, not formulas. We fit logistic regression for churn on four features (days since last order, log of order count, pro plan, ads channel) and take one customer with a risk near 31%.

feats = ["rec", "logf", "pro", "ads"]
mu, sd = X.mean(0), X.std(0)
lr = LogisticRegression().fit((X - mu) / sd, y)
p = lr.predict_proba((X - mu) / sd)[:, 1]
i = int(np.argmin(np.abs(p - 0.31)))
print("cust", d.customer_id[i], "p", p[i].round(3), d.loc[i, feats].to_dict())
x = X[i].copy(); x[0] -= 10                       # she ordered 10 days more recently
print("rec - 10 days:", lr.predict_proba(((x - mu) / sd)[None, :])[0, 1].round(3))
x = X[i].copy(); x[1] = np.log1p(d.f[i] + 3)       # she has 3 more orders in history
print("+3 orders   :", lr.predict_proba(((x - mu) / sd)[None, :])[0, 1].round(3))
print("odds ratio per 10 days:", np.exp(lr.coef_[0][0] / sd[0] * 10).round(3))

Before you run it: which change helps more, 10 days more recent, or 3 more orders?

cust 215 p 0.311 {'rec': 26.0, 'logf': 2.70805020110221, 'pro': 0.0, 'ads': 0.0}
rec - 10 days: 0.179
+3 orders   : 0.306
odds ratio per 10 days: 2.072
  • Answer Customer 215 has been silent for 26 days with a 31.1% churn risk. If she had ordered 10 days ago (16 days of silence) the risk would be 17.9%. Three more past orders would hardly change it (30.6%).
  • Check The logistic coefficient on days is 1.647 per standard deviation (22.61 days). Per 10 days that is exp(1.647 / 22.61 × 10) = exp(0.7285) = 2.072, so every 10 extra silent days doubles the odds of churn.
  • Why it matters "Recency is what the model watches; history length hardly matters" is a sentence a marketing manager can use. "Coefficient 1.647" is not. Note: this says what the model uses, not that ordering earlier would cause lower churn (Chapter 4 and 8).

Uncertainty, in words. Replace "the lift is 3.2%" with: "We think the lift is between 1% and 5%. The most likely value is 3%. If we repeated this test 100 times, about 95 intervals would contain the true effect." Also say what would change your mind ("a drop in order count among ads customers would make me retest"). For a boss, three ranges are enough: pessimistic, likely, optimistic (Chapter 12.3).

Charts. One message per chart. The title is the message ("Customers silent for over 60 days churn far more often than active ones"). Label the axes with units. Avoid dual axes. Show the baseline. If you need a long legend, you need two charts.

Connection to the job. In interviews, case-study rounds and take-home tasks are graded mostly on clarity of recommendation and honesty about limits. In the job, the same skill decides if your project is funded next quarter.

Applications

  • Data Analytics: weekly insight memos, dashboards with annotated charts.
  • Data Science: experiment reports, model cards, executive summaries.
  • ML Engineering: design docs and runbooks.
  • Consulting-style roles: slide decks (one message per slide).
COMMON MISTAKE
Opening with method ("We trained several models...") and putting the recommendation on slide 12. The reader who stops at slide 3 learned nothing. Another version: saying "the model is 88% accurate" with no baseline, which sounds good until someone asks what the simple rule scores.

Check yourself

  1. Where does AUC belong in a business memo? Answer: In the appendix; the opening should state the decision, the money and the main risk.
  2. What does an odds ratio of 2.07 per 10 days mean in plain words? Answer: Ten more silent days roughly double the odds of churn, other things equal.
  3. Why add "what would change my mind"? Answer: It shows the limits of the claim and tells people what to monitor.

13.2 Stakeholder Management and Working in Small Iterations

BASIC EXPLANATION
Stakeholder management is working with the people who sponsor and use the work. Working in small iterations means delivering small pieces early and adjusting based on feedback.
INTUITION
A restaurant customer who says "something light" is not giving a recipe. A good waiter asks two questions, brings a small sample, and adjusts. Stakeholders are the same: the first request is a starting point, not a specification. Think of a builder: "make the kitchen bigger" becomes "add 2 metres to the east wall, budget 4 lakh rupees, finish before Diwali" only after a conversation.

Who they are.

StakeholderCares aboutTypical worryWhat helps
Business owner (marketing, risk head)Revenue, cost, deadlines"Will this move my KPI?"Money numbers, a pilot
Product managerUser impact, roadmap"How will users see it?"Clear scope, guardrails
EngineeringLatency, maintainability"Who supports this at 2 am?"Interfaces, tests, runbook
Data engineeringData contracts, pipelines"Why do you need a new table?"Early requirements, volumes
Legal and compliancePrivacy, fairness, audit"Can we explain a rejection?"Model card, explainability
LeadershipStrategy, risk"Do I trust the number?"Honest ranges, one clear ask

Clarify the ask. Five questions that rescue most projects:

  1. What decision will this change, and who makes it?
  2. What do you do today, and how good is it?
  3. What would a successful result look like in rupees, hours or risk?
  4. What data exists, and who owns it?
  5. By when is the answer useful, and what is the smallest useful version?

Problem 13.2.1 A manager asks: "Build a model to predict customer churn, 3 months, full pipeline." You estimate: data and framing 2 weeks, baseline and first model 1 week, validation and thresholding 1 week, deployment 4 weeks, monitoring 1 week. Propose a smaller plan that delivers value in 4 weeks.

Step 1Find the smallest piece that changes a decision: a weekly list of high-risk customers in a spreadsheet needs no deployment.
Step 2Plan: week 1 framing and data; week 2 baseline rule (silent over 60 days) and first model; week 3 threshold and cost analysis (Chapter 12); week 4 pilot list to marketing, with a holdout of 20%.
Step 3Move deployment (4 weeks) and monitoring (1 week) to phase 2, to be funded if the pilot shows lift.
ComputeOriginal: 2 + 1 + 1 + 4 + 1 = 9 weeks of work, results at the end. New: 4 weeks to first value. Weeks spent before the first business feedback fall from 9 to 4 (a cut of 55.6%).
AnswerOffer a 4-week pilot with a manually delivered list and a holdout, and plan production only after the pilot works.
CheckIf the pilot fails (no lift), we have spent 4 weeks instead of 9, and learned that early.
Why it matters"Baseline, improve, ship, measure" beats a three-month black box. Stakeholders see progress, and wrong assumptions die early.

Saying no without saying no. Use "not yet, here is what we need".

Request: "Predict which employees will resign, by name."
Reply:   "I can build a team-level attrition forecast now. Named predictions need
         HR policy, consent and a plan for how managers act on them. If we agree
         on those, I can scope phase 2."

Managing expectations. Share the baseline early ("a simple rule gets 0.88 AUC; the model will probably add a little more"). Share the effort and risk in ranges. Never promise an accuracy number before you have seen the data.

Connection to the job. The usual failure of junior data scientists is silent work followed by a big reveal. Seniors give a weekly 5-line update: done, next, blocked, risks, decisions needed.

Applications

  • Product analytics: scoping experiments with PMs.
  • ML Engineering: agreeing on interfaces and SLAs with consumers.
  • Risk and compliance: early review meetings avoid late vetoes.
COMMON MISTAKE
Saying "yes" to everything, then delivering late with a weak result. Equally bad: saying "no" without an alternative. Offer a smaller deliverable and a clear condition for the bigger one.

Check yourself

  1. Name the first question to ask a vague request. Answer: What decision will it change, and who makes it?
  2. Why plan a pilot with a holdout? Answer: It measures the true effect and limits the cost of being wrong.
  3. What is wrong with "I will tell you the accuracy in advance"? Answer: Accuracy depends on data you have not examined; promise a process and a baseline instead.

13.3 Ethics and Fairness in Practice

BASIC EXPLANATION
Fairness in machine learning means checking that a model does not treat groups unjustly. Ethics in practice also covers privacy, transparency, consent and the possible harm of a model's decisions.
INTUITION
A bridge engineer does not only ask "will it stand?" but "who walks on it, and what if it fails?" A model that decides who gets a loan, a job interview or a medical test is a bridge for people. Ethics here is a checklist you run before launch, not a lecture.

Words you need.

WordMeaningExample
Protected attributeA trait where unequal treatment is harmful or illegal (gender, caste, religion, disability, in India also region in some settings)Caste, gender
Proxy variableAn ordinary feature that carries a protected attributePincode standing in for religion or caste
Selection rateShare of a group receiving the favourable decision30% of group B approved
Disparate impact ratioLower group selection rate / higher group selection rate0.30 / 0.40 = 0.75
Equal opportunityEqual true positive rate across groupsTPR 79% vs 76%
Appeal / overrideA human way to challenge a decisionManual review of a rejection

Four-fifths rule of thumb (80% rule). In US hiring law a selection-rate ratio below 0.8 is treated as a warning sign. It is a screening heuristic, not a legal test in India, and a pass does not prove fairness. Many fairness definitions exist (equal selection rate, equal opportunity, calibration within groups), and they cannot all hold together when base rates differ. Pick the one that matches the harm, and write down why.

A fairness check on the churn offer. In our synthetic shop, we create a group label: group B = customers in Pune and Chennai (510 of 2,562), group A = everyone else. The model never sees the group or city. We compute rates at the cost-based threshold 0.222 (Chapter 12).

d["group"] = np.where(d.city.isin(["Pune", "Chennai"]), "B", "A")
s = p >= 0.222
for gr in ["A", "B"]:
    m = (d.group == gr).values
    print(gr, "n", m.sum(), "selection rate", round(s[m].mean(), 3),
          "TPR", round(s[m & (y == 1)].mean(), 3), "FPR", round(s[m & (y == 0)].mean(), 3))
print("churn rate by group", d.groupby("group").churn.mean().round(3).to_dict())
A n 2052 selection rate 0.305 TPR 0.793 FPR 0.143
B n 510 selection rate 0.324 TPR 0.757 FPR 0.159
churn rate by group {'A': 0.249, 'B': 0.275}

Problem 13.3.1 Compute the disparate impact ratio and the TPR gap from the table, and decide whether to worry.

Formularatio = lower selection rate / higher selection rate
Plug in0.305 / 0.324
Compute0.305 / 0.324 = 0.941. TPR gap = 0.793 − 0.757 = 0.036
AnswerThe ratio of 0.94 is above 0.8, and the TPR gap is 3.6 points. For a discount offer (a benefit with small harm) this is acceptable, and we document it. Group B has slightly higher base churn (27.5% vs 24.9%), which explains why they are selected a bit more often.
CheckIf the offer were a loan approval and ratios were 0.30 and 0.40, then 0.30 / 0.40 = 0.75, below 0.8, and we would investigate.
Why it mattersThe same number is a different level of alarm depending on the harm. Rate the harm first (a discount versus a rejected loan).

Proxy check. Even if you drop a sensitive attribute, other features may rebuild it. Test: train a model to predict the group from your features; if it does well, you have proxies.

from sklearn.model_selection import cross_val_predict
from sklearn.metrics import roc_auc_score
gy = (d.group == "B").astype(int).values
pp = cross_val_predict(LogisticRegression(), (X - mu) / sd, gy, cv=5, method="predict_proba")[:, 1]
print("proxy AUC, model features -> group:", round(roc_auc_score(gy, pp), 3))
C = pd.get_dummies(d.city).values.astype(float)
pp2 = cross_val_predict(LogisticRegression(), np.c_[(X - mu) / sd, C], gy, cv=5, method="predict_proba")[:, 1]
print("proxy AUC, with city added:", round(roc_auc_score(gy, pp2), 3))
proxy AUC, model features -> group: 0.485
proxy AUC, with city added: 1.0
  • Answer Our four features carry no information about the group (AUC 0.485 is about a coin flip), but adding city gives AUC 1.0 because the group was built from city. In real data the proxy is rarely that perfect, but pincode, college name, device price and first name often give 0.7 or more.
  • Check AUC 0.5 means no signal. 1.0 means perfect prediction. We designed this case, so 1.0 is expected; the lesson is the test, not the number.
  • Why it matters "We do not use caste or religion" is not a defence if pincode does the job.

The ethics checklist before launch.

  1. Who could be harmed, and how badly? (Rejected loan, wrongly flagged fraud, a missed diagnosis.)
  2. Which groups are under-represented in the training data? Check the metrics per group, with sample sizes and intervals.
  3. What does a mistake cost, in each direction, and for whom?
  4. Can the person affected get an explanation, an appeal and a human override?
  5. Are we using only the data we need, with consent for this purpose? (Chapter 2.4: DPDP Act and GDPR basics.)
  6. Is there a proxy for a sensitive attribute? (Run the test above.)
  7. How will we notice harm after launch? (Monitoring by group.)
  8. Who signs off, and where is the decision recorded?

Responsible use. Scraping, face recognition, emotion detection and surveillance applications need explicit legal and ethical review before any work starts. Do not collect personal data you cannot protect or justify.

Connection to the job. Banks, insurers, health and HR teams have review boards that ask these exact questions. In India, RBI expectations on model risk and fair practice, and the DPDP Act on personal data, set the frame; exact rules change, so read the current text for your sector.

Applications

  • Lending: adverse-action reasons and group metrics.
  • Hiring and HR analytics: screening tools need strict review.
  • Healthcare: performance by age, sex and region.
  • Ads and recommendations: avoid exploiting vulnerable users.
COMMON MISTAKE
Treating fairness as a number that is computed once. Fairness needs a defined harm, a chosen metric, per-group checks with sample sizes, and ongoing monitoring. A model that passes on average can fail on a small group where the sample is tiny, so show intervals.

Check yourself

  1. Selection rates of 0.45 and 0.30: the ratio? Answer: 0.30 / 0.45 = 0.667, below 0.8, so investigate.
  2. A model does not use gender, but predicts gender with AUC 0.85 from its inputs. What does that show? Answer: The inputs contain proxies for gender; the model can learn its effects indirectly.
  3. Why include an appeal process? Answer: Models make mistakes; people need a way to correct decisions that affect them.

13.4 Reproducibility: One Command, Same Answer

BASIC EXPLANATION
Reproducibility means that the same code and data give the same results. It is achieved with fixed seeds, recorded versions, scripted pipelines and a single command that runs everything.
INTUITION
A good recipe card can be cooked by a friend next month in a different kitchen and give the same dish, because the ingredients, the quantities, the oven temperature and the timing are all written down. A notebook that runs only on your laptop, on today's data, with a lucky seed, is the opposite.

The five pillars.

PillarWhat to doTool or command
CodeVersion control; small commits; no notebooks with hidden stategit
EnvironmentPin library versionspip freeze > requirements.txt or uv or conda env export
SeedsSet random seeds everywherenp.random.default_rng(42), random_state=42
DataSnapshot it or record its fingerprint and queryParquet snapshot plus a hash
RunOne command from raw data to reportmake report or python run.py

Seeds. Randomness in training, splitting and sampling must be repeatable:

def run(seed):
    r = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    return r.normal(size=5).round(3).tolist()
print(run(1)); print(run(1)); print(run(2))
[0.346, 0.822, 0.33, -1.303, 0.905]
[0.346, 0.822, 0.33, -1.303, 0.905]
[0.189, -0.523, -0.413, -2.441, 1.8]

Same seed, same numbers. A different seed gives a different result, which is why you should also report the spread over several seeds for anything important (Chapter 4.4).

Data fingerprint. Store a hash of the exact training table next to every model, so you can tell later if the data changed:

import hashlib
h = hashlib.sha256(pd.util.hash_pandas_object(d[["customer_id", "churn"]], index=False).values.tobytes()).hexdigest()[:16]
print("data fingerprint", h)
data fingerprint caebcf2047b44370

(The hash depends on the pandas version's hashing method; record the pandas version too.)

Notebooks. Notebooks are excellent for exploring. Before sharing: restart the kernel and run all cells in order; move reusable code to src/; remove paths such as C:/Users/me/; never store secrets in a notebook (use environment variables).

Connection to the job. Reproducibility is what lets another person take over your project, an auditor review it, or MLOps automate it (Chapter 7 of the MLOps material).

Applications

  • Regulated domains (banking, health): audit trails of data and code versions.
  • Research-style roles: papers and experiments others must reproduce.
  • Teams: onboarding in an hour instead of a week.
COMMON MISTAKE
Pinning nothing: "scikit-learn" in requirements with no version, so six months later a new release changes a default and your numbers move. Another: saving a model without the data and code version that made it.

Check yourself

  1. Why store a data fingerprint? Answer: To prove later that a model was trained on exactly this data, and to detect silent changes.
  2. What is the risk of running a notebook out of order? Answer: Hidden state: results depend on cells run earlier, so a fresh run gives different results.
  3. Where do thresholds and costs belong? Answer: In a config file under version control, not hard-coded in several places.

13.5 Prototype to Production, Monitoring and Handover

BASIC EXPLANATION
Moving a prototype to production means turning an experimental model into a reliable service. Monitoring tracks data and model quality over time, and handover documents the system so that others can maintain it.
INTUITION
A new bus route that works on paper is not yet a bus service. Production is real passengers, odd inputs and breakdowns at 3 am. A prototype shows an idea works; production keeps it working. The gap between them is mostly boring things: same features in both places, monitoring, rollback and an owner.

Words you need.

WordMeaning
PrototypeA demo that shows value (Streamlit app, notebook, spreadsheet list)
Batch scoringScore many rows on a schedule (nightly)
Online / real-time scoringScore one request in milliseconds through an API
Training-serving skewThe feature is computed differently in training and serving
DriftInputs or outcomes change over time
RunbookSteps to follow when the model misbehaves

Pick the simplest serving mode. If the business decision happens weekly (retention list), a batch job and a CSV is production. Real-time APIs cost far more to build and run. Choose online only when the decision must happen during a user request (fraud at payment, recommendations on a page).

A prototype in 20 lines (not run here).

# app.py   run with:  streamlit run app.py        (standard usage, not run here)
import streamlit as st, joblib, numpy as np
model = joblib.load("models/churn_v1.joblib")
st.title("Churn risk")
rec = st.slider("Days since last order", 0, 90, 26)
orders = st.number_input("Past orders", 1, 200, 14)
pro = st.checkbox("Pro plan"); ads = st.checkbox("Acquired via ads")
p = model.predict_proba([[rec, np.log1p(orders), int(pro), int(ads)]])[0, 1]
st.metric("Churn probability", f"{p:.1%}")
# api.py   run with:  uvicorn api:app             (standard usage, not run here)
from fastapi import FastAPI
from pydantic import BaseModel
import joblib, numpy as np
app = FastAPI(); model = joblib.load("models/churn_v1.joblib")
class Customer(BaseModel):
    rec: float; n_orders: int; pro: int; ads: int
@app.post("/score")
def score(c: Customer):
    return {"p_churn": float(model.predict_proba([[c.rec, np.log1p(c.n_orders), c.pro, c.ads]])[0, 1])}

A prototype app helps stakeholders play with the model and find odd behaviour before launch. Handing over to ML engineering means giving them the saved model, the feature code, the model card, test cases, expected latency and the monitoring plan.

Training-serving skew: a real failure. The most common silent bug: the feature rec (days since last order) is computed in training relative to the prediction date T = 2025-01-01. A serving script computes it relative to "today" (the last data date, 30 April 2025).

d["rec_bad"] = (END_DATE - d["last"]).dt.days             # serving bug: wrong reference date
Xs = d[feats].copy(); Xs["rec"] = d.rec_bad
ps = lr.predict_proba((Xs.values - mu) / sd)[:, 1]
print("mean days since last order:", d.rec.mean().round(1), "vs", d.rec_bad.mean().round(1))
print("mean risk:", p.mean().round(3), "vs", ps.mean().round(3))
print("share flagged:", (p >= .222).mean().round(3), "vs", (ps >= .222).mean().round(3))
mean days since last order: 19.4 vs 138.4
mean risk: 0.254 vs 0.998
share flagged: 0.309 vs 1.0
  • Answer With the wrong reference date the model sees every customer as silent for 138 days, assigns a 99.8% churn risk to everyone, and flags 100% of customers. No error message appears. The code ran perfectly.
  • Check 138.4 − 19.4 = 119.0 days, exactly the gap between 1 January and 30 April 2025 (31 + 28 + 31 + 29 = 119). The bug adds the same constant to every customer.
  • Why it matters This is why a feature must have one definition, in one place (features.py), used by both training and serving, and why monitoring must watch input distributions, not only errors.

What to monitor. Three layers, from fast to slow:

LayerExamplesAlert when
Service healthResponse time of the scoring service (95th percentile), error rate, requests per minute, null inputsAn agreed service target is breached
Data and score driftInput means, missing share, PSI of the score (Chapter 11.2)PSI above 0.25, or feature missing share jumps
Outcome qualityRealised churn by score decile, profit vs holdout, per-group metricsQuality drops once labels arrive (60 days later here)

Also keep a holdout group that never receives the action, so you can measure true impact, not just model accuracy. Have a rollback: the previous model, or a simple rule, one command away. Define retraining triggers (PSI, calendar, drop in performance) and an owner who reads the alerts.

Problem 13.5.1 Weekly score PSI is 0.04, 0.06, 0.08, 0.31. Which week needs action, and what is your first move?

Connection to the job. In many Indian GCCs and product companies, "data scientist" means you own the model after launch, at least in a review role. ML engineers and MLOps (Chapter 7 in the MLOps material) own the platform. Showing you understand their concerns is a hiring signal.

Applications

  • Retail and e-commerce: nightly batch scores to CRM.
  • Payments: real-time scoring under a latency budget.
  • Healthcare: decision support with human sign-off.
  • Any team: shadow mode first (score but do not act), then pilot, then full rollout.
COMMON MISTAKE
Launching with no way to tell if the model is working. Labels arrive late, so people assume "no news is good news". Monitor inputs and scores immediately; monitor outcomes as soon as labels arrive.

Check yourself

  1. What is training-serving skew? Answer: A feature is computed differently when training than when serving, so the model sees data that does not match what it learned from.
  2. Why start in shadow mode? Answer: You compare model decisions with real outcomes and with the current process without any risk.
  3. When is a batch job better than an API? Answer: When decisions are made on a schedule (weekly list), because it is simpler, cheaper and easier to test.

Practice set

1. (Easy) Rewrite this as an answer-first sentence: "We analysed 12 months of data and built three models; XGBoost had AUC 0.91; the best threshold is 0.30." Add what is missing. Answer: Start with the decision and money: "Send the offer to customers with risk above 30%; we expect X rupees profit, assuming Y save rate." AUC moves to the appendix. What is missing is the money, the assumption and the risk.

2. (Easy) Selection rates are 0.50 for group A and 0.35 for group B. Disparate impact ratio and verdict? Answer: 0.35 / 0.50 = 0.70, below 0.8; investigate.

3. (Easy) What is the odds ratio for 20 silent days if it is 2.07 per 10 days? Answer: 2.07² = 4.28.

4. (Medium) What is wrong with this requirements file? pandas / scikit-learn / numpy Answer: No versions. A future install can get different versions and change results. Pin with pip freeze.

5. (Medium) Find the bug: training computes days_since_signup = (T - signup).days with T = 2025-01-01. The API computes (datetime.now() - signup).days. Answer: Training-serving skew. The API uses "now", so the feature keeps growing after training. Fix: pass the reference date explicitly, or share one function, and add a test on the distribution of the feature in production.

6. (Medium) A model is said to be "fair because it does not use religion". Give two ways to test the claim. Answer: (1) Train a model to predict religion from the inputs and check the AUC; (2) compare selection rate, TPR and FPR across groups using a held-out set that has the attribute.

7. (Medium) Project plan: 12 weeks, first feedback at week 12. Rewrite it with the first business feedback at week 4 and say what you cut. Answer: Weeks 1 to 4: framing, baseline rule, first model, list to the business with a holdout. Cut or defer: deployment, API, dashboards and fine-tuning. Phase 2 only if the pilot shows lift.

8. (Medium) Weekly PSI of a score is 0.02, 0.03, 0.12. What do you do? Answer: Watch zone (0.1 to 0.25): look at PSI per feature, check data changes (launches, pipeline edits), and watch next week; no retrain yet.

9. (Hard) The model passes fairness overall, but group C has only 40 customers. What do you report? Answer: Show the group's metrics with a confidence interval (with n = 40 the interval is wide) and say that the evidence is weak. Collect more data or monitor this group closely after launch instead of declaring it fair.

10. (Interview) "You built a great model and the business does not use it. What do you do?" Answer: Find the reason: unclear decision, no trust, wrong format, no capacity to act. Talk to users, show a counterfactual explanation and a pilot result, fit the output into their workflow (a weekly list, not an API), define the metric of success together, and rerun a small pilot.

11. (Interview) "How do you make sure your analysis can be reproduced by a teammate?" Answer: Version control, a pinned environment file, fixed seeds, a recorded data snapshot or query and fingerprint, config for parameters, one command to rerun, a README, tests, and a model card.

12. (Interview) "Walk me through taking a churn model to production." Answer: Decide batch or online from the decision frequency; one feature definition shared by training and serving; test cases; shadow mode, then a pilot with a holdout; monitoring of service health, drift (PSI) and outcomes; rollback and a retraining trigger; handover docs (README, model card, runbook) and a named owner.
