CHAPTER 9

Forecasting and Time Series for Decisions

How to predict demand, orders or traffic honestly: beat the baselines, test with backtests, state the uncertainty, and turn the forecast into a stock or staffing decision.

Every operations team asks the same question: "How many will we need next week?" Orders to pack, support agents to roster, servers to keep warm, units to stock. A forecast is a number that someone will use to spend money. If it is too low, you lose sales or crash under load. If it is too high, you pay for idle capacity. Anyone who has planned staffing for a busy shop knows the feeling. This chapter teaches the data-science version. You will learn what a time series is made of, the simple baselines that beat many fancy models, the classical models (exponential smoothing, ARIMA, and a Prophet-style regression), machine learning with lag features, the one correct way to validate (walk-forward backtesting), prediction intervals, and finally how to turn a forecast into a decision. By the end you will be able to run a fair model contest and report honestly who won.

Running example. Daily orders of a delivery app over two years (730 days, from 1 Jan 2023). We built them: a base level of 200 growing by 0.25 per day, a weekly pattern (weekends are 20 to 25% busier, Mondays 10% quieter), random noise of about 6%, and a five-day festival spike (up to 1.8 times normal) around 12 Nov 2023 and 1 Nov 2024. All values are synthetic with a fixed seed (default_rng(4)) and all numbers below come from real runs. statsmodels, prophet and lightgbm were not installed here, so we coded Holt-Winters and a Prophet-style regression by hand with numpy, and used scikit-learn's HistGradientBoostingRegressor for the lag model. Those library calls are shown as "not run here".

9.1 Components, Baselines and Error Metrics

INTUITION
A time series is a story told in order: today depends on yesterday, last week and last year. Before hiring a clever storyteller (a model), ask a lazy one: "What happened last Sunday? Then assume the same this Sunday." That lazy guess is a baseline. If a model cannot beat it, the model is not worth its complexity.

Words you need

WordPlain meaningIn our orders data
LevelThe average height of the series nowAbout 390 orders per day in late 2024
TrendSlow, steady rise or fall+0.25 orders per day each day
SeasonalityA pattern that repeats at a fixed periodWeekly: Sunday high, Monday low
Holiday / eventIrregular, known-date effectFestival around 1 Nov
NoiseRandom wiggle nobody can predictAbout 6% either way
HorizonHow far ahead we forecastNext 91 days
DecompositionSplitting a series into those partsWeekday index by dividing by a 7-day average

Decomposition. A 7-day centred moving average removes the weekly cycle and leaves the trend. Dividing the series by it leaves the seasonal ratios. For our data the weekday indexes, Monday to Sunday, came out as 0.854, 0.897, 0.951, 0.941, 1.014, 1.153, 1.189. Sunday is 19% above the average day. (We built 1.25 with the trend added; the moving average mixes the trend in, so estimates differ a little. STL decomposition in statsmodels does this more carefully: not run here.)

Problem 9.1.1 (tiny numbers) Orders for 14 days start at Monday. Week 1: 90, 95, 100, 100, 105, 120, 125. Week 2: 99, 104, 110, 110, 115, 132, 138. Forecast week 3 with seasonal naive, and then compare with a simple exponential smoothing on the first four numbers [10, 12, 11, 13] with α = 0.5.

Step 1 (seasonal naive)Week 3 forecast = week 2 repeated: 99, 104, 110, 110, 115, 132, 138.
Step 2 (exponential smoothing, SES)The rule is level_new = α · today + (1 − α) · level_old. It weights recent days more, with weights that shrink geometrically (that is why it is called exponential). Start with level = 10.
Step 3After 12: 0.5 × 12 + 0.5 × 10 = 11. After 11: 0.5 × 11 + 0.5 × 11 = 11. After 13: 0.5 × 13 + 0.5 × 11 = 12.
AnswerThe SES forecast for every future day is 12 (flat). Seasonal naive repeats last week's pattern, including the weekend rise.
Check12 is between the recent values 11 and 13, as a weighted average must be. Week 2 is about 1.1 times week 1 in the example, so seasonal naive would be 10% low for week 3 if the growth continues. Baselines are simple, not perfect.
Why it mattersSES is the building block of all Holt-Winters models: add a trend term and a seasonal term and you have ETS.

Problem 9.1.2 Actuals [100, 120, 80, 0.5], forecasts [110, 100, 90, 5]. Compute MAPE and WAPE.

Step 1 (percent errors)10 / 100 = 10%. 20 / 120 = 16.7%. 10 / 80 = 12.5%. 4.5 / 0.5 = 900%.
Step 2 (MAPE)(10 + 16.7 + 12.5 + 900) / 4 = 234.8%.
Step 3 (WAPE)Sum of absolute errors = 10 + 20 + 10 + 4.5 = 44.5. Sum of actuals = 300.5. WAPE = 44.5 / 300.5 = 14.8%.
AnswerMAPE says 235%, which is absurd, because one tiny actual (0.5) dominates. WAPE says 14.8%, which matches our feeling of "we were off by about 15% of volume".
CheckThe three big items were each off by 10 to 20%; WAPE reflects that.
Why it mattersFor slow-moving items, MAPE is a trap. Use WAPE, MASE or MAE.

The MASE here divides each model's test MAE by the average size of a one-week-apart change in the training data (the in-sample seasonal naive error). A value of 1.06 means the test error is 6% larger than that in-sample yardstick; so for this metric lower is better, and values near 1 are good. Connection to the real job

Baselines are the first slide of every forecasting project: "Our current rule gives WAPE 9.4%. The new model gives 5.8%." That one sentence is how you justify the project.

Applications

  • Supply chain: store-level demand for fresh items.
  • Workforce: call-centre staffing by half-hour.
  • Engineering: daily active users and capacity planning for servers.
  • Finance: monthly revenue guidance.
COMMON MISTAKE
Reporting one error number for a model without the baseline next to it. A MAPE of 9% sounds good or bad depending on whether the naive rule gets 8% or 25%.

Check yourself

  1. Which metric breaks when actual values are near zero? Answer: MAPE.
  2. What does a MASE below 1 mean? Answer: the model beats the naive forecast (measured on training data).
  3. Why is "same weekday last week" so strong for weekly data? Answer: it copies the seasonal pattern and the current level with no fitting.

9.2 Stationarity, ACF and the Classical Models

INTUITION
A stationary series is a calm sea: its average and its wobble stay the same over time. Most real series are not calm: they trend and have seasons. Classical methods either model the trend and season directly (exponential smoothing) or first remove them by subtracting (ARIMA's "differencing").

Stationarity and differencing

Stationary means the mean, the spread and the pattern of correlation do not change with time. Differencing replaces each value with its change: d_t = y_t − y_(t−1). This removes a steady trend. Seasonal differencing uses y_t − y_(t−7) and removes a weekly cycle.

The autocorrelation function (ACF) at lag k is the correlation between the series and itself k steps earlier. It shows what repeats. Unit root tests (ADF, KPSS) check stationarity formally; they live in statsmodels (not run here).

Problem 9.2.1 Read the ACF of our orders data at lags 1, 2, 7, 14.

                    lag 1   lag 2   lag 7   lag 14
raw series           0.72    0.53    0.86    0.85
after d(1)          -0.18   -0.28    0.67    0.66
after d(7)           0.24    0.13   -0.52    0.02
  • Step 1 The raw series has high correlation at lags 7 and 14 (0.86, 0.85). That is the weekly cycle plus trend.
  • Step 2 After ordinary differencing, lag 1 and 2 are negative, but lags 7 and 14 stay high (0.67, 0.66). The weekly cycle survives, so one ordinary difference is not enough.
  • Step 3 After a seasonal difference (lag 7), the lag-14 value falls to 0.02. The weekly pattern is gone. The −0.52 at lag 7 is a typical side effect of differencing noise.
  • Answer For ARIMA on this series we would use a seasonal difference of 7 (that is the "D = 1, m = 7" of SARIMA).
  • Check A spike in the ACF at lag 7 on a daily series always signals weekly seasonality; this is quick to confirm on your own data.
  • Why it matters ACF plots tell you the model structure (how many past terms) before you fit anything.

ETS / Holt-Winters

Exponential smoothing with trend and season (ETS, Holt-Winters) keeps three running numbers: a level, a trend and 7 seasonal factors. Each is updated with its own smoothing weight (α for level, β for trend, γ for season, between 0 and 1). Small weights mean "change slowly, trust the long past". It handles trend and season, needs no stationarity, and is fast. We coded the multiplicative version and chose α, β, γ by minimising the error on the training data from a small grid: it picked α = 0.1, β = 0.01, γ = 0.05.

ARIMA and SARIMA

ARIMA(p, d, q) has three parts: AR (autoregressive, p past values), I (integrated, d differences), MA (moving average, q past forecast errors). SARIMA adds a seasonal set. SARIMAX adds outside predictors (price, promotion flags). We can show the AR idea by hand: after a log transform and a lag-7 seasonal difference, fitting AR(1) by least squares gave φ = 0.05, meaning the seasonally-differenced growth has almost no memory. For this synthetic series, the seasonal difference already removes most structure, so ARIMA would not beat Holt-Winters. In practice use statsmodels SARIMAX or pmdarima / statsforecast AutoARIMA (not run here). Also see TBATS for multiple seasonalities (such as daily and weekly in hourly data).

The Prophet-style regression

Prophet (a library from Meta) is a regression with named pieces: y = trend + weekly seasonality + yearly seasonality + holidays. The seasonal pieces are made from sine and cosine waves (a Fourier series: a few smooth waves added together can draw any repeating shape). We did the same by hand: log(orders) = a + b·t + weekly waves (3 pairs) + yearly waves (2 pairs) + five festival-day effects (−2 to +2 days around the festival), fitted with ordinary least squares. The festival effects are shared across years, so 2023 teaches the model what 2024 will look like. (The real prophet package adds changing trend slopes and Bayesian fitting: not run here.)

Problem 9.2.2 Which of Holt-Winters or the regression is better on the 91-day test, and is it the same on a fair backtest?

Step 1 (one split)From the table: Holt-Winters MAE 24.13, regression MAE 22.88. RMSE: 40.84 against 27.88. The regression's RMSE is much smaller because it caught the festival spike (big errors are punished by RMSE).
Step 2 (rolling backtest, next section)Ten origins, 28-day horizon: Holt-Winters 24.68, regression 19.50, seasonal avg 4 weeks 23.85, seasonal naive 33.49.
AnswerThe regression is best in both, mostly because it knows the festival date. Holt-Winters is competitive and needs no holiday list.
CheckOn the one split, the 4-week seasonal average beats seasonal naive (33.1 against 37.0). Averaging a few weeks reduces noise, as the 6% daily noise is large.
Why it mattersIf a model knows the calendar (holidays, paydays, sales), give it that information. Calendar knowledge often beats a better algorithm.

Connection to the real job

Capacity and demand planning teams run ETS and SARIMA as the "workhorse" models, because they are cheap, stable and need no GPU. At scale (thousands of series) people use statsforecast, which fits them very fast.

Applications

  • Retail: weekly seasonality in store sales.
  • Telecom: traffic with daily and weekly cycles.
  • Finance: monthly cash flows with quarter-end effects.
  • SRE: request-rate forecasts for autoscaling.
COMMON MISTAKE
Picking ARIMA orders (p, d, q) by staring at the ACF only. Use the ACF as a hint, then compare candidates on a backtest. And never difference data that is already stationary (it adds noise).

Check yourself

  1. What does differencing remove? Answer: a trend (ordinary) or a seasonal cycle (seasonal differencing).
  2. A daily series has an ACF spike at lag 7. What does it mean? Answer: weekly seasonality.
  3. Why Fourier terms? Answer: they let a regression fit smooth repeating shapes with few columns.

9.3 Machine Learning with Lags, and How to Validate

INTUITION
Turn forecasting into a spreadsheet. Each row is a day. Columns are what we knew BEFORE that day: yesterday's orders, orders a week ago, the 28-day average, the weekday. The target column is that day's orders. Now any tabular model can learn it. The only new rule: rows must never see the future.

Features

FeatureExampleWhy
Lag korders 1, 7, 14, 28 days agoRecent level and weekly echo
Rolling meanmean of the previous 7 or 28 days (shifted by 1)Smooth level
Calendarday of week, day of year, holiday flag, paydayKnown future information
Known future inputsplanned promotions, priceOnly if you will know them at forecast time

Remember the leakage rule from Chapters 2 and 3: rolling means must be computed on data BEFORE the target day (shift(1) first, then rolling).

Two ways to forecast many days ahead. Recursive: predict tomorrow, feed it back as "yesterday" for the day after. Errors compound. Direct: train a separate model for each horizon (1 day, 2 days, ...). More work, no compounding. Our run is recursive.

Global models. If you have 5,000 products, you can train ONE model on all series together with an "item" column, instead of 5,000 small models. A global model shares what is common (weekly shape, holiday effect) and helps new or short series (cold start). This is usually how LightGBM or neural networks are used for retail forecasting.

The result, honestly

On the same 91-day test, the recursive gradient boosting model with the lags above scored MAE 35.5, RMSE 49.4, WAPE 8.98%, MASE 1.65. That is worse than Holt-Winters (24.1) and the regression (22.9), and about the same as the seasonal averages.

  • Why: two years of data with a growing trend. Tree models predict averages of training rows, so they cannot extrapolate above the highest levels they saw in training. Lag features carry the level, but errors compound in the recursive loop. The festival occurs once per year, so two examples is too little to learn from.
  • Takeaway: ML shines with many series, many rows, many external features (price, promotions, weather). On one short, clean, trending series, classical methods often win. This is "Rule 1" from the skill map in action: compare against the baselines. See the ML document (study/ml/) for gradient boosting itself.

Deep and foundation models (awareness only, not run here): LSTM/GRU, N-BEATS, Temporal Fusion Transformer, PatchTST, and pretrained "foundation" models like Chronos and TimesFM. They can win with large collections of series. They need more data, more tuning and more care, and still must beat the baseline on a backtest.

Validation: never random k-fold

If rows are shuffled, a row from 15 Dec can be in the training set while its neighbour 14 Dec is in the test set. The model sees the future. Walk-forward (rolling-origin) backtesting copies how the model is used:

origin 1:  train |==========|  test [----]
origin 2:  train |==============|  test [----]
origin 3:  train |==================|  test [----]
           time ------------------------------->

Each origin: train only on data up to the origin, forecast the next 28 days, record errors, move the origin forward 28 days.

Problem 9.3.1 Show how much random k-fold flatters the same gradient boosting model on one-step-ahead prediction.

# one-step features: lag1, lag7, lag14, lag28, r7, r28, dow, doy
for tri, tei in KFold(5, shuffle=True, random_state=0).split(X): ...   # random folds
m = HistGradientBoostingRegressor(...).fit(X.iloc[:600], np.log(y.iloc[:600]))  # time split

Real output:

random 5-fold one-step MAE      20.39
time split (train first 600 rows, test last 102)   29.10
  • Step 1 Random folds put days from every period in training, including the neighbours of each test day. The model effectively interpolates.
  • Step 2 The time split trains only on the past, and the same model scores 29.1, which is 43% worse. (The test sets are not identical; the second one is the last 102 days, which also includes the festival and the highest levels. Both effects are real parts of a real forecast.)
  • Answer Random k-fold would have told us "MAE 20" when the honest figure is about 29.
  • Check The honest number is the one in production. If your score drops when you switch to a time split, the earlier score was leaking.
  • Why it matters Teams that validate with random splits ship models that fail in the first week.

Problem 9.3.2 A rolling-origin backtest: 10 origins, 28-day horizon, starting at day 450. Compare errors by horizon.

model                   mean MAE over 10 backtests   days 1-7   days 22-28
seasonal naive                 33.49                  33.91       37.94
seasonal avg 4 weeks           23.85                  24.12       27.47
Holt-Winters                   24.68                  24.13       31.44
Regression (Prophet-style)     19.50                  19.87       20.60
  • Step 1 The ranking is stable across ten origins: regression first, then the 4-week average and Holt-Winters, then seasonal naive.
  • Step 2 Error grows with horizon. Holt-Winters worsens from 24.1 (days 1 to 7) to 31.4 (days 22 to 28), because its trend estimate drifts. The regression barely changes (19.9 to 20.6), because its trend is global.
  • Answer Report error by horizon. A model for next-week staffing and a model for next-quarter planning may be different.
  • Check 10 origins is small; with real data use many origins (weekly) to get stable estimates.
  • Why it matters A single split can crown the wrong model by luck.

Connection to the real job

Interviewers ask: "How do you validate a forecasting model?" Answer: rolling-origin backtest, horizon-specific errors, always against seasonal naive; no random k-fold; no future-known features that will not exist at forecast time.

Applications

  • E-commerce: one LightGBM global model for all SKUs.
  • Ride-hailing: demand per zone per 15 minutes.
  • Energy: hourly load with temperature forecasts.
  • MLOps: a backtest is the unit test of a forecasting pipeline.
COMMON MISTAKE
Using actual future values of a feature (like next week's real price or real weather) in a backtest when, at forecast time, you would only have a forecast of it. The backtest looks great and production does not.

Check yourself

  1. Why shift(1) before rolling(7)? Answer: so the rolling mean uses only days before the target day.
  2. Why can tree models struggle with trending series? Answer: they cannot predict values beyond the range seen in training.
  3. What is a global model? Answer: one model trained across many series.

9.4 Intervals, Special Cases and Forecast to Decision

INTUITION
A weather report that says "30 degrees" is less useful than "between 27 and 33, 80% sure". You pack differently. A forecast of "300 orders" tells the warehouse nothing about how many extra packers to keep on call. An interval says how wrong it might be.

Prediction intervals

A prediction interval covers a future single value (wider than a confidence interval for a mean, which covers an average; see Chapter 4.4). Three practical ways:

  1. Model-based (ETS, ARIMA give them), assuming normal errors.
  2. Empirical: take the relative errors from backtests, and use their 10th and 90th percentiles.
  3. Conformal prediction: a general recipe that wraps any model: compute errors on held-out windows, take a quantile, and add it to forecasts; it carries a coverage guarantee when data are exchangeable (a strong assumption for time series, so check coverage by backtest anyway).

We use method 2. Relative backtest errors (actual − forecast) / forecast from nine earlier origins (252 values) had 10th and 90th percentiles of −9.6% and +5.1%.

Problem 9.4.1 Apply the 80% interval to the 91 test days and check its coverage.

Step 1The interval is [forecast × (1 − 0.096), forecast × (1 + 0.051)].
Step 2Count test days whose actual falls inside: 70 of 91 = 76.9%.
Step 3Average width = 60.5 orders.
AnswerThe nominal 80% interval covered 76.9% of the test days. Slightly low, because the test period included the festival (rare, big errors) and the backtest windows had mostly calm days.
CheckA normal-based interval, forecast × (1 ± 1.2816 × 0.0607), covered 74.7%. Both under-cover a little.
Why it mattersAlways measure coverage on held-out data. If "80%" covers 60%, the interval is lying.

Quantile (pinball) loss scores a quantile forecast: for quantile q, loss = max(q·(a − f), (q − 1)·(a − f)). Over-forecasting costs (1 − q) per unit, under-forecasting costs q. For the 90th and 10th percentile bounds on the test set the average pinball losses were 4.08 and 4.18. Other probabilistic scores include CRPS. Many tools (LightGBM with objective="quantile", statsforecast) can fit quantiles directly: not run here.

Bias example from our test: the seasonal naive forecast averaged 28 orders per day below actual (because it ignores growth), while the regression ran 15 per day above actual (it over-predicted the festival days). Which hurts more depends on the cost of each type of error, which is the next topic.

Forecast to decision

A forecast alone does nothing. The decision uses its uncertainty and the costs.

Newsvendor rule. You order stock once, then sell. If you order too few, you lose margin per missed sale (c_under). If too many, you lose cost per leftover unit (c_over). The best quantity is the forecast quantile at the critical ratio c_under / (c_under + c_over).

Problem 9.4.2 Next week's demand for a fresh item is forecast at mean 300 units with a standard deviation of 8% of the mean (24 units). A missed sale loses ₹40 margin. A leftover unit costs ₹15 (spoilage). How many to order?

FormulaCritical ratio = c_under / (c_under + c_over). Order = mean + z × sd.
Plug in40 / (40 + 15) = 0.7273. The normal z-value for 0.7273 is 0.6046.
ComputeOrder = 300 + 0.6046 × 24 = 300 + 14.5 = 314.5, so order 315.
AnswerOrder about 315, which is 15 above the mean forecast. The ₹40 loss from a missed sale is larger than the ₹15 loss from a leftover, so it pays to lean high.
CheckIf the costs were equal (critical ratio 0.5, z = 0), you would order the mean, 300. If a stockout cost ₹400, the ratio 0.964 gives z = 1.80 and an order of 343.
Why it mattersThe same forecast produces different orders for different costs. The decision depends on the interval, not only the average.

Safety stock for a service level: safety stock = z × σ_daily × √lead time. With a 95% service level (z = 1.645), daily demand standard deviation 25 and lead time 4 days: 1.645 × 25 × √4 = 82.25 units, so keep about 82 units as a buffer. Staffing works the same way: staff to a high quantile, not the mean.

Connection to the real job

This is the whole point: weekly inventory, rosters, cloud capacity. Everyday capacity planning shows why. Capacity planning is "forecast peak load plus headroom", and headroom is exactly a quantile of the forecast.

Applications

  • Retail and food delivery: perishables ordering.
  • Call centres: staffing at the 90th percentile of call volume.
  • Cloud: reserve capacity for the forecast 95th percentile.
  • Finance: cash buffers.
COMMON MISTAKE
Handing the business a single "most likely" number and forgetting that decisions are asymmetric. Always ask "what does it cost to be 10% too low, and 10% too high?"

Check yourself

  1. What do you check after building a prediction interval? Answer: its coverage on held-out data.
  2. Costs: missed sale ₹30, leftover ₹10. Critical ratio? Answer: 30 / 40 = 0.75.
  3. Why separate bias from error? Answer: a forecast can have small errors but always lean the same way, which costs money systematically.

Chapter recap

  • A series has level, trend, seasonality, events and noise. Decompose it to see them.
  • Always run baselines first: naive, seasonal naive, moving average, drift. Seasonal naive halved our error (87.6 to 37.0).
  • Pick metrics that suit the data: MAE and WAPE for volumes, MASE across series, avoid MAPE near zero, track bias.
  • Differencing and the ACF reveal trend and seasonality; use seasonal differencing for weekly cycles.
  • ETS (Holt-Winters), SARIMA and Prophet-style regressions are strong, cheap workhorses. Give models the calendar.
  • ML with lag features needs shift before rolling, and shines with many series and external features, not necessarily on one short series.
  • Validate with walk-forward backtests over many origins, report error by horizon. Never random k-fold.
  • Give a prediction interval, and check its coverage; ours claimed 80% and covered 77%.
  • Decisions need quantiles: newsvendor critical ratio and safety stock turn a forecast into an order.
  • Anomaly detection, hierarchies, intermittent demand and cold start have their own tools.

Practice set

1. (easy) Seasonal naive on daily data with m = 7: forecast for next Tuesday if last Tuesday was 120? Answer: 120.

2. (easy) SES with α = 0.3, previous level 50, new observation 60. New level? Answer: 0.3 × 60 + 0.7 × 50 = 18 + 35 = 53.

3. (easy) Actuals 50 and 150, forecasts 60 and 120. Compute the WAPE. Answer: (10 + 30) / 200 = 20%.

4. (medium) MASE: model MAE is 12, and the naive forecast's MAE on the training data is 15. MASE and meaning? Answer: 12 / 15 = 0.8. Beats the naive by 20%.

5. (medium, what is the output) A model is evaluated with KFold(shuffle=True) on daily data and shows MAE 20. With a time split it shows 29. Which do you trust? Answer: The time split; random folds leak neighbouring days.

6. (medium, find the bug) df["r7"] = df.y.rolling(7).mean() is used to predict y on the same row. What is wrong? Answer: The window includes today's y, so the target leaks into the feature. Use df.y.shift(1).rolling(7).mean().

7. (medium) Costs: stockout ₹60, leftover ₹20, demand ~ Normal(mean 100, sd 10). Order quantity? (z for 0.75 is 0.674.) Answer: Ratio 60/80 = 0.75, z = 0.674, order = 100 + 6.7 = 106.7, so about 107.

8. (hard) Your 90% interval covers only 65% of backtest days. List two likely causes and fixes. Answer: Intervals too narrow because errors are not normal or the variance grows with level; use empirical or relative-error quantiles, or conformal calibration. Also possible: the backtest windows contain events (promotions) not in the calibration data; add those features or widen.

9. (hard, scenario) A new item has 3 weeks of sales. Which approach? Answer: A global model trained on similar items using item attributes (category, price), or an analogue product's profile scaled to the first weeks; update quickly as data arrive; give wide intervals.

10. (hard) A model has MAPE 8% but a bias of +12% (always over-forecasts). Is it fine? Answer: No. Errors of 8% that always lean 12% high mean systematic over-stocking. Investigate (maybe it learns promotion periods wrongly); fix or de-bias, and measure bias separately.

11. (interview) "Your LightGBM forecast loses to seasonal naive on the backtest. What do you do?" Answer: Report it honestly. Check leakage in features, whether trend or level shifts break the tree model, try differencing the target or predicting the ratio to last week's value, add calendar and promotion features, then compare again. If still behind, ship the baseline.

12. (interview) "How do you turn a demand forecast into an inventory decision?" Answer: Get a probabilistic forecast, estimate the costs of stockout and overstock, set order quantity at the critical-ratio quantile, add safety stock for lead time uncertainty, and monitor realised service level and bias.
