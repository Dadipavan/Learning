Survival Analysis and Mixed (Hierarchical) Models

Two tools for data that most simple models handle badly: "time until something happens" and "data that comes in groups".

Picture two questions from a real business. First: "How long does a new subscriber stay before cancelling?" Many have not cancelled yet, so their final stay is unknown. Second: "What is the typical delivery time (in minutes) of each of our 200 depots?" Some depots have thousands of deliveries, some only three. A plain average or plain regression gives wrong or over-confident answers for both. Survival analysis solves the first problem and mixed (hierarchical) models solve the second. You will build the main ideas by hand with small numbers, reusing the exponential distribution (Chapter 2) and MLE (Chapter 3). The libraries lifelines and statsmodels are not installed where this chapter was written, so their calls are marked "not run here". Everything else uses numpy and scipy, and every number was computed.

11.1 Time-to-Event Data and Censoring

INTUITION
You start a stopwatch on ten customers the day they sign up. You must write the report on day 12. Some customers have already left. Others are still there. You cannot write "stayed 12 days" for a customer who is still active, because they may stay 40 more. But you also cannot throw them away, because "survived at least 12 days" is real information. Survival analysis is the method that keeps this half-known information instead of lying about it.

Words you need

WordPlain meaningExample
EventThe thing we wait forCustomer cancels, machine breaks down, loan defaults
Duration (time)Time from the start until the event or until we stop watching5 months
CensoredWe stopped watching before the event happened, so we only know "at least this long"Still subscribed at month 12
Survival function S(t)Probability that the event has NOT happened by time tS(6) = 0.46
Hazard h(t)Risk of the event in the next instant, given you survived until t9% per month

Each row of survival data has two columns: time and event (1 = event seen, 0 = censored).

The running example

We will use ten subscribers for the whole chapter. Time is in months.

Customer 3 (time 3, event 0) was lost at month 3 without cancelling. Customers 9 and 10 are still active at month 12, the end of the study.

Problem 11.1.1 What average stay do you get if you (a) average all ten times as if every customer had cancelled, and (b) average only the customers who cancelled? Why are both wrong?

Step 1All ten times: 2+3+3+5+6+6+8+9+12+12 = 66. Mean = 66 / 10 = 6.6 months.
Step 2Only the six cancellers (events = 1): times 2, 3, 5, 6, 6, 9, sum = 31. Mean = 31 / 6 = 5.17 months.
Answer(a) is 6.6 and (b) is 5.17. Method (a) treats "still active at month 12" as "left at month 12", so it is too small for the people still active. Method (b) throws away the long stayers, so it is biased low.
CheckWe can verify with code below: t.mean() and t[e==1].mean().
Why it mattersCensoring is the reason ordinary averages and ordinary regression fail on duration data. Dropping censored rows always makes retention look worse than it is.
import numpy as np
t=np.array([2,3,3,5,6,6,8,9,12,12])
e=np.array([1,1,0,1,1,1,0,1,0,0])
print("mean naive all",t.mean(), "mean events only",t[e==1].mean())

Real output:

Where it is used: churn and customer lifetime (Data Science), time between failures (SRE), time to readmission (Healthcare), model drift time (MLOps).

COMMON MISTAKE
Computing "average tenure" from only the customers who already left. This is the survival analysis version of survivorship bias, and it is very common in dashboards. Always ask: "who is still active, and where did they go in my calculation?"

11.2 The Kaplan-Meier Estimator

INTUITION
Think of a marathon with runners dropping out. At each moment someone drops, ask: "Of the runners still on the road, what fraction just dropped?" The chance of lasting past that point is the previous survival chance times (1 − that fraction). Runners who leave the race for another reason (censored) simply stop counting from then on. You multiply these small survival fractions along the road.

How it works

Look at each time tᵢ where at least one event happened.

  • nᵢ = number at risk: customers still being watched just before tᵢ (time ≥ tᵢ).
  • dᵢ = number of events at tᵢ.
  • Fraction who fail at that moment: dᵢ / nᵢ.
  • Kaplan-Meier (KM) estimate: S(t) = product over tᵢ ≤ t of (1 − dᵢ / nᵢ).

The name: Kaplan and Meier published this in 1958. The curve is a staircase: flat between events and a step down at each event. Censored customers cause no step, but they shrink nᵢ later, which makes later steps bigger.

Problem 11.2.1 Four customers have (time, event): (2,1), (4,0), (5,1), (7,1). Compute the KM survival curve.

FormulaS(t) = ∏ (1 − d/n) over event times
Step 1At t=2: n = 4 at risk, d = 1. Factor = 1 − 1/4 = 0.75. S(2) = 0.75.
Step 2At t=4: a censoring, not an event, so no step. S stays 0.75. But the customer leaves the risk set.
Step 3At t=5: at risk = customers with time ≥ 5 → the ones at 5 and 7, so n = 2, d = 1. Factor = 0.5. S(5) = 0.75 × 0.5 = 0.375.
Step 4At t=7: n = 1, d = 1. Factor = 0. S(7) = 0.
AnswerS = 0.75 after month 2, 0.375 after month 5, 0 after month 7.
CheckIf we wrongly treated the censored customer as an event at t=4, we would get S(4) = 0.75 × (1 − 1/3) = 0.5 and S(5) = 0.5 × (1 − 1/2) = 0.25. The correct answer 0.375 is higher, as it should be, because we do not know that customer left.
Why it mattersThe same arithmetic, just with bigger tables, is what KaplanMeierFitter in lifelines does.

Problem 11.2.2 Compute the KM table for the ten-customer running example.

S=1.0
print("time n_risk d  q  S")

Where it is used: retention curves by cohort (Data Analytics), comparing churn of two plans (Data Science), hours until half of a disk fleet fails (Reliability), time to first purchase where non-buyers are censored (Marketing).

COMMON MISTAKE
Reading the end of a KM curve as precise. At the right end only a few customers remain at risk, so one cancellation makes a big step and the estimate is very uncertain. Always show the confidence band and the number at risk under the curve.

Check yourself

  1. If nobody is censored, what does KM reduce to? Answer: the plain fraction of customers still alive (the empirical survival function).
  2. Does a censored customer cause a step down? Answer: no, but they leave the risk set.
  3. What is the median survival? Answer: the first time when S(t) ≤ 0.5.

11.3 Hazard, a Simple Model, the Log-rank Test and Cox Regression

INTUITION
Survival S(t) is "how many are still in the room". Hazard is "how fast people are leaving the room right now, among those still in it". Two rooms can have the same number of people now but very different exit speeds. Hazard is the speed. Models in survival analysis usually work with hazards, because it is natural to say "this plan makes the leaving speed 2 times higher".

The constant-hazard (exponential) model

If the hazard is a constant λ (for example 9% per month), then the time to the event follows the exponential distribution from Chapter 2, and S(t) = e^(−λt). With censoring, the MLE (Chapter 3 idea) is simple:

λ̂ = (number of events) / (total time everyone was observed)

The bottom is called exposure: all months watched, from events and censored people alike. The median survival is ln(2) / λ̂.

Problem 11.3.1 Using the ten customers: 6 events and 66 total months of exposure. Estimate λ and the median.

Formulaλ̂ = events / exposure; median = ln 2 / λ̂
Plug inλ̂ = 6 / 66
Computeλ̂ = 0.0909 per month. Median = 0.6931 / 0.0909 = 7.62 months. Approximate standard error λ̂ / √events = 0.0909 / 2.449 = 0.0371.
AnswerAbout 9.1% of active customers cancel each month. The model says half are gone by month 7.6.
CheckKM said median 6 months; the exponential model says 7.6. They differ because ten points are very few, and a constant hazard may not be true. Always compare a parametric model with KM.
Why it mattersEvents divided by exposure is the same idea as breakdowns per million machine-hours; censored time counts in the denominator.

Comparing two groups: the log-rank test

Suppose the first five customers are on a monthly plan (group A) and the last five on an annual plan (group B). The log-rank test asks: if the two groups had the same hazard, how many events would we expect in group A at each event time, given who is at risk? At each event time with n at risk (nA in A), d events: expected events in A is d × nA / n. Add up expected events, compare with observed events, and divide the squared gap by the sum of variances. The result follows a chi-square distribution (Chapter 2) with 1 degree of freedom.

Group A is times 2,3,3,5,6 (events 1,1,0,1,1). Group B is times 6,8,9,12,12 (events 1,0,1,0,0). At every event time we compute EA = d*nA/n and a variance d*(nA/n)*(nB/n)*(n-d)/(n-1). The table from that loop:

Problem 11.3.2 Read the table. What does it say?

Step 2If A and B had the same risk, we would expect only E = 0.5 + 0.444 + 0.286 + 0.333 + 0 = 1.563 in A.
Step 3Test statistic = (4 − 1.563)² / 0.923 = 5.936 / 0.923 = 6.43.
AnswerThe p-value is 0.011. Monthly-plan customers cancelled earlier than annual-plan customers more than chance explains, in this tiny toy sample.
Why it mattersIn practice you call lifelines.statistics.logrank_test (not run here), but it is only "observed versus expected events at each event time".

Cox proportional hazards regression

KM and log-rank handle one grouping variable. Real questions have many: plan type, country, age, number of support tickets. Cox regression (David Cox, 1972) models the hazard like this:

h(t | x) = h₀(t) × exp(β₁x₁ + β₂x₂ + ...)

  • h₀(t) is the baseline hazard: an unknown curve shared by everyone. Cox's trick is that you never need to estimate it.
  • exp(β) is the hazard ratio (HR): the factor by which the hazard is multiplied when x goes up by 1. HR = 1 means no effect. HR = 0.5 means half the leaving speed.
  • "Proportional" means the ratio between any two customers stays constant over time. This is the main assumption, and you must check it.

How is β found? At each event time, ask: "Given that exactly one customer in the risk set fails now, how likely is it to be the one who did?" That is exp(β·xᵢ) / sum over risk set of exp(β·xⱼ). Multiplying over event times gives the partial likelihood, which we maximise (the MLE idea of Chapter 3).

Problem 11.3.3 Fit a Cox model with one binary variable x = 1 for the annual plan, using our ten customers.

from scipy.optimize import minimize
def nll(b):                          # negative log partial likelihood
    ll=0
    for i in np.where(E==1)[0]:
        R=T>=T[i]                    # risk set at this event time
        ll+=b*G[i]-np.log(np.exp(b*G[R]).sum())
    return -ll
r=minimize(lambda z:nll(z[0]),[0.0])
b=r.x[0]; print("beta",r.x,"HR",np.exp(r.x))
h=1e-4; se=1/np.sqrt((nll(b+h)-2*nll(b)+nll(b-h))/h**2)   # curvature of the loglik
print("se",se,"ci",np.exp(b-1.96*se),np.exp(b+1.96*se),"loglik",-r.fun,-nll(0.0))

T, E, G are times, events and group codes (0 = monthly, 1 = annual). Real output:

se 1.1539892590234444 ci 0.009525486613028183 0.8779669083001508 loglik -8.334197210954885 -11.127851046509797
  • Step 1 β̂ = −2.392.
  • Step 2 Hazard ratio = e^(−2.392) = 0.0914.
  • Step 3 95% CI for β: −2.392 ± 1.96 × 1.154, then exponentiate: from 0.0095 to 0.878.
  • Answer The annual plan has about 91% lower cancellation hazard in this toy sample. But the interval is huge (0.01 to 0.88) because there are only 10 customers and 6 events.
  • Check The log-likelihood improved from −11.13 (β = 0) to −8.33. Twice the gap is 5.59, a likelihood-ratio statistic comparable to the log-rank 6.43; the two tests are closely related, as theory says.
  • Why it matters In a real churn model you would add many columns. Reading exp(coef) as "this feature multiplies the leaving speed by this factor" is the whole skill.

Note: our loop uses Breslow handling of tied event times; lifelines uses Efron by default, so its numbers can differ slightly. With 10 customers, do not trust the effect size.

Applications: churn drivers with confidence intervals (Data Science), effect of firmware on time to failure (Reliability), time to default (Finance), survival forests that predict a whole curve per customer (Machine Learning, same data format).

COMMON MISTAKE
Treating a hazard ratio as a ratio of probabilities or of times. HR 0.5 means half the instantaneous risk, not half the time to the event and not "50% more likely to survive". Also: never ignore the proportional hazards assumption. If a feature's effect fades over time (a promotion that helps for 2 months), HR is not constant and one number misleads.

  2. exp(β) = 1.8 for "has a support ticket". Meaning? Answer: the cancellation hazard is 1.8 times higher.
  3. What does Cox regression NOT need to estimate? Answer: the baseline hazard shape.

11.4 Grouped Data and the Idea of Partial Pooling

INTUITION
You record delivery times for 200 depots. Depot A has 3 deliveries, average 400 minutes. Depot B has 3,000 deliveries, average 400 minutes. Do you believe both equally? No. The mean of A is shaky. A good analyst pulls the shaky mean a bit toward the typical depot, and leaves the solid mean almost alone. That pulling is called shrinkage, and the model that does it automatically is a mixed (hierarchical) model.

The problem with the two simple answers

  • Complete pooling: one global average; erases real differences.
  • No pooling: each group alone; noisy for small groups, and the extreme groups are mostly noise.
  • Partial pooling: a compromise whose weight depends on each group's amount of data.

The random-intercept model

For observation j in group g:

y_gj = μ + a_g + ε_gj, with a_g ~ Normal(0, τ²) and ε_gj ~ Normal(0, σ²)

  • μ is the overall mean (a fixed effect: one number you want to know).
  • a_g is the group's own deviation (a random effect: you do not care about each exactly, you model them as draws from a population of groups).
  • τ² = variance between groups. σ² = variance inside groups (noise).
  • The intraclass correlation (ICC) = τ² / (τ² + σ²): the share of total variance that belongs to groups. It also equals the correlation between two measurements from the same group.

Given known τ², σ², the best estimate for group g with n observations and sample mean ȳ is

estimate = w × ȳ + (1 − w) × μ, where w = τ² / (τ² + σ²/n)

w is the weight on the group's own data. It goes to 1 for large n and towards 0 for small n. This formula comes from Bayes thinking in Chapter 1 and the Math PDF: a prior (the population of groups) combined with data.

Problem 11.4.1 Three depots with mean delivery times: A has n = 1, ȳ = 400 min. B has n = 4, ȳ = 300. C has n = 25, ȳ = 220. Suppose μ = 250, τ² = 400, σ² = 900. Compute the partially pooled estimates.

Formulaw = τ² / (τ² + σ²/n); estimate = μ + w (ȳ − μ)
Plug in(A) w = 400 / (400 + 900/1) = 400 / 1300 = 0.3077. (B) w = 400 / (400 + 225) = 0.64. (C) w = 400 / (400 + 36) = 0.9174.
Compute(A) 250 + 0.3077 × 150 = 296.15. (B) 250 + 0.64 × 50 = 282.0. (C) 250 + 0.9174 × (−30) = 222.48.
AnswerA's single 400-minute delivery is pulled hard, to 296 min. B is pulled a little, to 282. C, with 25 observations, hardly moves (220 to 222.5).
CheckEach estimate lies between ȳ and μ, and the one with more data stays closer to its own mean. ICC = 400 / 1300 = 0.31.
Why it mattersWithout shrinkage, the "slowest depot" list is dominated by depots with few deliveries. This is a classic ranking error (also seen in "best-rated restaurant with one 5-star review").

Estimating τ² and σ² from data

Usually μ, τ², σ² are unknown. A mixed model estimates them by maximum likelihood (or REML, a version that corrects a small-sample bias in the variance estimates). This means choosing numbers to maximise the probability of seeing your data (Chapter 3). Below we do this by hand with scipy.optimize. The data is simulated: 8 endpoints with 3 to 60 measurements each, true values μ = 250, τ = 30, σ = 40.

from scipy.optimize import minimize
rng=np.random.default_rng(7)
ns=[3,5,5,8,10,20,40,60]
true=rng.normal(250,30,8)                      # true group means (tau = 30)
data={g:rng.normal(true[g],40,ns[g]) for g in range(8)}   # sigma = 40
def nll(p):                          # negative log-likelihood, random-intercept model
    mu,lt,ls=p; tau2=np.exp(lt); sig2=np.exp(ls); ll=0
    for v in data.values():
        v=np.array(v); n=len(v); ybar=v.mean(); ssq=((v-ybar)**2).sum()
        logdet=(n-1)*np.log(sig2)+np.log(sig2+n*tau2)
        quad=ssq/sig2+n*(ybar-mu)**2/(sig2+n*tau2)
        ll+=-0.5*(n*np.log(2*np.pi)+logdet+quad)
    return -ll
r=minimize(nll,[250,np.log(500),np.log(500)],method='Nelder-Mead')
mu,tau2,sig2=r.x[0],np.exp(r.x[1]),np.exp(r.x[2])
print("mu",mu,"tau",np.sqrt(tau2),"sig",np.sqrt(sig2),"icc",tau2/(tau2+sig2))
print("g n true_mean nopool pool w   err_nopool err_pool")
en=[];ep=[]
for g,v in data.items():
    w=tau2/(tau2+sig2/len(v)); pool=mu+w*(v.mean()-mu)
    en.append(v.mean()-true[g]); ep.append(pool-true[g])
    if g in (0,2,5,7): print(g,len(v),round(true[g],1),round(v.mean(),1),round(pool,1),round(w,3),round(abs(en[-1]),1),round(abs(ep[-1]),1))
print("(rows 1, 3, 4, 6 omitted here)")
print("rmse nopool",np.sqrt(np.mean(np.square(en))),"rmse pool",np.sqrt(np.mean(np.square(ep))))

COMMON MISTAKE
Treating 1,000 rows from 10 users as 1,000 independent observations ("pseudoreplication"). Rows from the same user are correlated, so standard errors are far too small and p-values far too optimistic. Use a mixed model, or cluster-robust errors, or aggregate per user first.

Check yourself

  3. Fixed or random effect: "the effect of plan type" versus "the effect of each of 5,000 stores"? Answer: plan type fixed; stores random.

11.5 Random Slopes and Going Further

A random intercept says "each depot has its own starting level". A random slope adds "each depot also reacts differently to order volume". Each group gets its own line, shrunk toward the average line: y = (μ + a_g) + (β + b_g) × volume + ε. In statsmodels: smf.mixedlm("delivery_min ~ load", df, groups=df["depot"], re_formula="~load") (not run here). Ignore for now: crossed random effects, non-normal outcomes (generalized linear mixed models, which join Chapter 8's GLMs with random effects), Bayesian hierarchical models (Chapter 9 priors do the shrinking), and survival "frailty" models. "Mixed", "multilevel", "hierarchical" and "random-effects" model all name the same family.

Chapter recap

  • Duration data has censoring: for some people you only know "at least this long". Deleting them, or treating them as events, biases results.
  • Kaplan-Meier multiplies (1 − d/n) at each event time; censored people only shrink the risk set.
  • Exponential rate = events / exposure; the log-rank test compares observed and expected events between groups.
  • Cox regression gives hazard ratios exp(β) without a baseline; check proportional hazards.
  • Mixed models handle grouped data and avoid pseudoreplication.
  • Partial pooling pulls small-group means toward the overall mean; ICC says how much the group matters.

Answer: New sign-ups had little time to cancel, so that plan looks better. Use KM or events / exposure.

Problem 11.6.7 τ² = 100, σ² = 400, a group has n = 4, ȳ = 60, μ = 50. Pooled estimate and ICC?

Answer: w = 100 / (100 + 100) = 0.5, estimate = 50 + 0.5 × 10 = 55. ICC = 100 / 500 = 0.20.

Problem 11.6.8 (Interview) Why not run linear regression on tenure after deleting customers who have not churned?

Answer: Deleting them removes the long stayers and biases durations low. Survival methods keep censored rows in the risk sets without pretending the event happened.

Problem 11.6.9 (Interview) 1,000 page-load times from 20 users; you test a new design. What do you watch for?

Answer: Rows are clustered by user, so they are not independent. Use a mixed model with a random intercept per user, or average per user. A plain t-test on 1,000 rows gives standard errors that are too small.
