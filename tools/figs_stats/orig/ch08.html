CHAPTER 8

Generalized Linear Models

Regression for yes/no answers and for counts: logistic, Poisson and negative binomial models.

Ordinary linear regression predicts a number that can be anything: 120 minutes, minus 5, 3.7. But many real questions are different. "Will this parcel arrive late, yes or no?" has only two answers. "How many complaints will we get this hour?" is a count: 0, 1, 2, never 2.5 and never negative. If you force a straight line onto these, it predicts probabilities of 130 percent and error counts below zero. Generalized linear models (GLMs) fix this with one clean idea. In this chapter you learn the idea, fit logistic and Poisson regressions with a short NumPy routine, read odds ratios and marginal effects, test whether a model fits using deviance, and handle the common failure of "too much variation" with the negative binomial model. We build on OLS and MLE (see the Math PDF for MLE and log-loss) and on AIC from Chapter 7.

Two running examples, both simulated with a fixed seed so the numbers are repeatable:

  • Late deliveries: 500 parcels, with load (in units of 100 orders per hour, 1 to 10) and distance (in 10 km units, 0 to 5). The outcome is 1 if the parcel arrived late. Result: 184 of 500 were late (36.8 percent).
  • Complaint counts: complaints per hour against traffic (thousands of orders per hour).

statsmodels is not installed in this environment, so I fitted the models with my own iteratively reweighted least squares (IRLS) code. In real work you would call statsmodels.api.GLM or sklearn; the numbers match because it is the same maths. Library calls shown below are standard but "not run here".

8.1 The GLM Idea and Link Functions

INTUITION
A normal regression builds a score η = β₀ + β₁x₁ + β₂x₂. That score can be any number from minus to plus infinity. A probability must stay between 0 and 1. A count must stay at or above 0. So we pass the score through a bent pipe (a link function) that squeezes it into the allowed range. The score itself stays a simple straight-line formula.

Words you need

WordPlain meaningExample
Linear predictor ηThe straight-line score β₀ + β₁x₁ + …η = −0.65
Link function gConnects the mean of y to η: g(mean) = ηlogit, log
Inverse linkTurns η back into a mean1/(1 + e^(−η))
FamilyThe distribution of y around its meanBernoulli, Poisson
DevianceA fit score: 2 × (ℓ_saturated − ℓ_model), lower is better500.8

A GLM has three parts: (1) a distribution for y (the family), (2) a linear predictor η, (3) a link g(μ) = η, where μ is the mean of y.

ModelFamilyLink g(μ)Mean μ in terms of ηy looks like
Linear regressionNormalidentity: μμ = ηany number
Logistic regressionBernoulli / Binomiallogit: ln(μ/(1−μ))μ = 1/(1+e^(−η))0 or 1
Poisson regressionPoissonlog: ln μμ = e^ηcount 0, 1, 2…
Negative binomialNeg. binomiallog: ln μμ = e^ηoverdispersed count
Gamma regressionGammalog (or inverse)μ = e^ηpositive, skewed (delivery time)

Why not just predict the mean with a straight line? Because the line leaves the allowed range. Why the log for counts? Effects become multiplicative: adding 1 to x multiplies the expected count by e^β. That matches how busy periods tend to act on complaint counts.

How the fit works

We use maximum likelihood (MLE). For these models there is no closed formula, so we use IRLS: repeat a weighted least-squares fit, updating weights from the current fit, until the coefficients stop changing. This is Newton's method (see Math PDF, second-order optimisation) in a special form. It typically converges in 5 to 8 rounds. Our code converged in 6 for both examples.

COMMON MISTAKE
Reading the coefficient of a logistic or Poisson model as if it were the change in y. It is the change in η (log-odds or log-count). Convert with exp or compute probabilities.

Connection to the real job. statsmodels.api.GLM(y, X, family=sm.families.Binomial()) and …Poisson(); sklearn.linear_model.LogisticRegression, PoissonRegressor, GammaRegressor. Logistic regression is the first classifier you will meet in ML.

Applications

  • Data Science: churn, fraud, conversion (logistic); orders per day, tickets per hour (Poisson).
  • Machine Learning: logistic regression is a one-layer neural net with a sigmoid and log-loss.
  • Insurance: claim counts (Poisson, NB), claim size (Gamma).
  • Operations: late-delivery probability and complaint counts as a function of order load.

Check yourself

  1. Which link is used for counts? Answer: The log link.
  2. In logistic regression, what does η = 0 mean? Answer: Probability 0.5 (odds 1).
  3. Why is IRLS used? Answer: GLM likelihoods have no closed-form maximum, so we solve by repeated weighted least squares.

8.2 Logistic Regression and Odds Ratios

INTUITION
Horse-racing people say "3 to 1 against" instead of "25 percent". Odds are probability expressed as a ratio of "for" to "against". Odds are handy because they run from 0 to infinity, and their logarithm runs from minus to plus infinity, which is exactly the range of a straight-line score.

Formal definition

odds   = p / (1 − p)
logit  = ln(odds) = ln(p / (1 − p)) = β₀ + β₁x₁ + β₂x₂
p      = 1 / (1 + e^(−logit))

Each coefficient βⱼ is the change in log-odds for one unit of xⱼ, others fixed. e^βⱼ is the odds ratio (OR): the factor by which the odds are multiplied. OR = 1 means no effect, above 1 raises the odds, below 1 lowers them. The likelihood to maximise is Σ [y ln p + (1−y) ln(1−p)], whose negative is the log-loss (see Math PDF).

Problem 8.2.2 (combining ideas) What is the late-delivery probability at load = 6 and distance = 2? What happens if load goes to 7?

Step 1η = −4.395 + 0.5209 × 6 + 0.3087 × 2 = −4.395 + 3.125 + 0.617 = −0.653.
Step 2p = 1/(1 + e^0.653) = 1/(1 + 1.921) = 0.342. Odds = 0.521.
Step 3At load 7: η = −0.132, p = 0.467, so the new odds are 0.467/0.533 = 0.877. New odds / old odds = 0.877/0.521 = 1.683 (code: 1.6835), equal to the OR.
Answer34.2 percent at load 6, and 46.7 percent at load 7. The probability rose by 12.5 percentage points while the odds rose by a factor of 1.68.
CheckThe ratio of odds equals e^β exactly, a good test of the algebra.
Why it mattersThe OR is constant but the probability change is not: it depends on where you start.
COMMON MISTAKE
Saying "the odds ratio is 1.68, so the probability is 1.68 times higher". Odds are not probabilities. Only when events are rare (p small) are the two nearly equal. Here p moved from 0.342 to 0.467, which is a factor of 1.36, not 1.68.

Marginal effects

A marginal effect answers "how much does the probability change per unit of x?" For logistic regression it is p(1−p) × β, so it changes with p. The average marginal effect (AME) averages this over all rows. Here, AME for load = 0.0862, so on average one more 100 orders per hour adds about 8.6 percentage points of timeout probability; AME for payload = 0.0511, about 5.1 points per 10 KB. Non-linear models need this to give a plain-language answer.

KEY INSIGHT
Logistic regression outputs a probability. The threshold of 0.5 is a business choice, not a law. If missing a late parcel is expensive, use 0.3. Evaluation with precision, recall, ROC and calibration belongs to the ML chapters.

Connection to the real job. Logistic regression is the standard baseline for any yes/no prediction: it is fast, explainable, and gives odds ratios you can show to a manager. It is also the model inside many risk scorecards at banks.

Applications

  • Data Analytics: which factors raise churn odds.
  • Machine Learning: baseline classifier; L1/L2 penalty from Chapter 7 applies directly (LogisticRegression(C=…), where C = 1/λ).
  • Healthcare: odds ratios for risk factors.
  • Fraud and credit: scorecards.
COMMON MISTAKE
Perfect separation. If one feature lets you split yes from no without error, the MLE does not exist: the coefficient grows towards infinity and software warns or gives huge numbers. Add a penalty (Chapter 7) or remove the feature.

  1. If β = 0.693, what is the OR? Answer: e^0.693 = 2.0. The odds double.
  2. Does OR = 2 mean the probability doubles? Answer: No, only the odds double.
  3. What does an OR confidence interval of 0.8 to 1.3 say? Answer: It includes 1, so no clear effect.

8.3 Poisson Regression for Counts

INTUITION
Complaints arrive at random, like cars passing a point on a road. In each hour the number of cars varies, but there is a typical rate. The Poisson model says the count in an hour is random around a rate, and that the rate itself depends on traffic. Higher traffic means a higher rate, multiplied, not added.

Formal definition

y ~ Poisson(μ),   ln μ = β₀ + β₁x      so   μ = e^(β₀ + β₁x)

A key property of the Poisson distribution is mean = variance = μ (see Chapter 2 on distributions). e^β₁ is the rate ratio: the factor by which the expected count is multiplied for a unit increase in x.

Exposure and offsets. If hours differ in length, or counts come from systems of different size, model the rate: ln μ = ln(exposure) + β₀ + β₁x. The ln(exposure) term is an offset: a column whose coefficient is forced to 1 (GLM(..., offset=np.log(exposure)) in statsmodels; not run here). Without it you would compare a 10-minute window with a 60-minute window unfairly.

Problem 8.3.1 Fit Poisson regression for complaints per hour on 300 hours of traffic data.

Compute(from code): intercept 0.1488 (SE 0.0885), traffic slope 0.3482 (SE 0.0202), 6 iterations. True values used to simulate: 0.2 and 0.35.
AnswerRate ratio = e^0.3482 = 1.416. Each additional 1,000 orders per hour multiplies the expected complaint count by 1.42, a 42 percent rise.
CheckSlope z = 0.3482/0.0202 = 17.2: very significant. Data mean count 4.37 and variance 9.65: the variance is bigger than the mean, but part of that is explained by the changing μ across hours, not by overdispersion, as the next check shows.
Why it mattersCompounding is the point: 5 more thousand multiplies by 1.416⁵ = 5.7, not by 1 + 5 × 0.42.

Problem 8.3.2 Is the Poisson model a good fit? Use the deviance and the Pearson ratio.

FormulaResidual deviance = 2 Σ [y ln(y/μ̂) − (y − μ̂)]. Pearson dispersion = Σ (y − μ̂)² / μ̂ / (n − p).
ComputeDeviance = 347.41 on 298 degrees of freedom (300 rows − 2 parameters). Pearson ratio = 1.056.
AnswerFor a well-fitting Poisson model both ratios are close to 1. Deviance/df = 1.17 and Pearson = 1.06 are fine. No sign of overdispersion.
CheckMean and variance of residual counts should be close once μ̂ is used. Pearson near 1.0 says so.
Why it mattersThis test costs nothing and prevents wrong confidence intervals.
COMMON MISTAKE
Using Poisson regression without checking dispersion. Real counts are usually more variable than Poisson. The standard errors then come out too small, and everything looks significant.

Connection to the real job. Counts per time unit (orders, tickets, defects, calls), rate modelling with exposure, and demand forecasting inputs. Also the loss in many deep-learning count models.

Applications

  • Operations: tickets per day, delivery complaints per week.
  • Insurance and healthcare: claim counts, hospital admissions.
  • Marketing: purchases per customer.
  • Web analytics: page views per session (often overdispersed, see next).

Check yourself

  1. Poisson mean = 6. What is its variance? Answer: 6.
  2. β = 0.2 in a Poisson model. Rate ratio? Answer: e^0.2 = 1.22.
  3. What is an offset for? Answer: To model rates when exposure (time or size) differs between rows.

8.4 Overdispersion and the Negative Binomial Model

INTUITION
The Poisson model assumes every hour with the same traffic has the same complaint rate. In real life some hours are "bad days" (a flash sale, a storm) and others quiet. The rate itself wobbles, so counts vary more than Poisson allows. The negative binomial model adds one more number that measures this wobble.

Formal definition

Overdispersion means variance is greater than the mean. The negative binomial (NB) regression keeps the log link and the mean μ = e^η, but the variance is

Var(y) = μ + α × μ²        (α ≥ 0; α = 0 is Poisson)

It arises when the Poisson rate is itself random with a Gamma distribution (a "Gamma-Poisson mixture"). A big α means lots of extra wobble.

Problem 8.4.1 Simulate 400 hours where the true model has mean e^(0.2 + 0.3 × traffic) and α = 0.5. Fit Poisson first. Then fit NB.

Step 1Data: mean count = 3.76 but variance = 15.19. Variance is four times the mean. Red flag.
Step 2Poisson fit: intercept 0.016, slope 0.3358 (SE 0.0192). Pearson dispersion = 2.87. A value well above 1 confirms overdispersion.
Step 3The quasi-Poisson fix: multiply the SE by √2.87 = 1.695: slope SE becomes 0.0326, which is 1.7 times larger. The point estimate stays the same.
Step 4NB fit by maximum likelihood (scipy.optimize, BFGS): intercept 0.007, slope 0.3379 (numerical SE 0.0317), α = 0.507. True α was 0.5.
Step 5Compare: Poisson log-likelihood −1078.21, AIC 2160.4. NB log-likelihood −924.49, AIC 1854.97 (3 parameters including α).
AnswerNB wins by 305 AIC points; α is recovered correctly at 0.507. The Poisson SE (0.0192) was too small by about 40 percent compared to the NB SE (0.0317).
CheckThe NB-based SE (0.0317) and the quasi-Poisson SE (0.0326) agree. LR statistic for α = 0 is 307.4 (a very large number, so overdispersion is real).
Why it mattersWith the Poisson SE, you would have reported a confidence interval that is 40 percent too narrow, and "significant" results that are not.

The right panel of the figure above shows this. Grey dots are the counts. The solid line is the fitted mean. The dashed line is "mean + 2 standard deviations" under Poisson, which cuts across the cloud, so many dots lie above it. The red dotted line is the same band under NB, wider and about right.

KEY INSIGHT
The slope estimates from Poisson and NB are similar (0.336 versus 0.338). Overdispersion mostly damages the uncertainty (SEs, p-values), not the average effect.

Other counts problems you may meet: too many zeros (zero-inflated models), counts that can never be zero (truncated models). Both are extensions; ignore them until you see a histogram with a large spike at 0.

Connection to the real job. sm.GLM(..., family=sm.families.NegativeBinomial(alpha=…)) or sm.NegativeBinomial (the latter estimates α; not run here). In scikit-learn there is PoissonRegressor but no standard NB regressor.

Applications

  • Web and product analytics: visits, clicks, purchases per user.
  • Epidemiology: case counts by region.
  • Reliability: failures per machine across a fleet.
COMMON MISTAKE
Fixing overdispersion by "just switching to linear regression on the log of counts". Zeros break the log, and the variance behaviour is still wrong. Use NB or quasi-Poisson.

Check yourself

  1. Variance 15, mean 4: Poisson or NB? Answer: NB (variance far above the mean).
  2. What happens to point estimates and SEs when you use Poisson on overdispersed data? Answer: Estimates stay roughly right; SEs are too small.
  3. What is α = 0 in NB? Answer: The Poisson model.

REAL JOB TIP
For the late-delivery problem, nothing stops you using time-of-day as a factor, or regularising with L1 (Chapter 7). A GLM is a framework: choose the family from the shape of y, choose the features from the business.
PROFESSOR'S NOTE
Mixed-effects (hierarchical) GLMs, where each customer or branch gets its own small effect, are the next step when rows are grouped. Learn the idea: partial pooling means a group with few rows borrows strength from the others. Not covered in depth here.

Chapter recap

  • A GLM has a distribution (family), a linear predictor η, and a link function connecting mean to η.
  • Logit link for yes/no, log link for counts, identity for ordinary regression.
  • Fit by maximum likelihood using IRLS (repeated weighted least squares), 5 to 8 rounds.
  • Logistic: e^β is the odds ratio. Odds are not probability; report marginal effects for probability changes.
  • Deviance and the likelihood-ratio test compare models; AIC compares non-nested ones; McFadden pseudo-R² is not OLS R².
  • Poisson: e^β is a rate ratio, effects multiply; use an offset for unequal exposure.
  • Always check dispersion (Pearson/df near 1). If above 1, use negative binomial or quasi-Poisson.
  • Overdispersion mostly corrupts the standard errors, not the point estimates.

Practice set

1 (easy). p = 0.75. Give odds and log-odds. Answer: Odds = 0.75/0.25 = 3. Log-odds = ln 3 = 1.099.

2 (easy). A logistic coefficient is β = −0.7. What is the OR, and what does it say? Answer: e^(−0.7) = 0.497. The odds are halved for each unit increase.

3 (easy). A Poisson model has η = 2. What is the expected count? Answer: e² = 7.39.

4. Output: log-odds = ln(4) for a parcel. What is p? Answer: Odds 4, p = 4/(1+4) = 0.8.

5. A logistic model for conversion has intercept −3 and slope 0.5 per page view. Find p for 4 page views and the AME-like slope at that point. Answer: η = −3 + 2 = −1. p = 1/(1+e) = 0.269. Slope = p(1−p)β = 0.269 × 0.731 × 0.5 = 0.098 per page view.

6 (find the bug). A report says: "OR = 3.0 for the new UI, so users are 3 times as likely to convert." Baseline conversion was 40 percent. What is wrong, and what is the real new probability? Answer: OR describes odds, not probability. Baseline odds = 0.4/0.6 = 0.667. New odds = 2.0. New p = 2/3 = 0.667. Users are 1.67 times as likely (66.7 vs 40 percent), not 3 times.

7. Poisson fit gives Pearson dispersion 3.4 with SE of slope 0.02. What do you do and what is the corrected SE (quasi-Poisson)? Answer: Overdispersed. Use NB or quasi-Poisson. Quasi SE = 0.02 × √3.4 = 0.02 × 1.844 = 0.0369.

8. A log-likelihood is −120 for the model and −150 for the intercept-only model, with 3 extra parameters. Compute LR statistic, the McFadden R² and say whether it's significant (chi-square 5 percent critical value with 3 df = 7.81). Answer: LR = 2 × (−120 − (−150)) = 60 > 7.81, highly significant. McFadden = 1 − 120/150 = 0.20.

9 (scenario). Complaint counts are recorded for windows of 10, 30 and 60 minutes. How do you model them? Answer: Poisson (or NB) with a log link and offset ln(window length in minutes), so coefficients describe complaints per minute.

10 (scenario). A logistic model on 20 features has a coefficient of 18.4 on one feature and a convergence warning. What probably happened and what do you do? Answer: Perfect or near-perfect separation by that feature. Check the crosstab of that feature against y, remove or bin it, or add L2 regularization.

11 (interview). Explain the odds ratio to a product manager in two sentences. Answer: "For every extra 100 orders per hour, the odds that a parcel arrives late go up by about 68 percent (odds ratio 1.68). Odds are a different way to express chance, so in probability terms the jump depends on where you start: from 34 percent it goes to about 47 percent."

12 (interview). Why use negative binomial rather than Poisson, and what changes in your results? Answer: Real counts often have variance larger than the mean. Poisson assumes equality, so its standard errors are too small and p-values too optimistic. NB adds a dispersion parameter α with Var = μ + αμ². The estimated effects stay similar, but standard errors become honest and the fit (AIC) improves a lot when overdispersion is present.
