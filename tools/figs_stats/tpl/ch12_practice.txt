@P 12.P.11 | (CUPED) The naive standard error of a lift is 0.30. The pre-period metric has correlation 0.7 with the outcome. What is the CUPED standard error, and how does the required sample size change?
@A Variance is multiplied by 1 − 0.7² = 0.51, so the SE is 0.30 × √0.51 = 0.214. Required sample size is proportional to variance, so you need 51% of the users (a 49% saving).
@P 12.P.12 | (SRM) A 50/50 test shows 5,150 users in control and 4,850 in treatment. Compute chi-square and decide.
@A Expected 5,000 each. χ² = 150²/5,000 + 150²/5,000 = 4.5 + 4.5 = 9, p = 0.0027. This is an SRM: investigate the assignment and logging before reading the metric.
@P 12.P.13 | (Peeking) Why is the O'Brien-Fleming boundary at the first of five looks as high as 4.55?
@A Early in the test the estimate is very noisy, so a large z is easily produced by chance. By demanding z above 4.55 you spend almost no false-positive budget early and keep the boundary near 2.03 for the final look, where most data has arrived.
@P 12.P.14 | (Delta method) n = 1,000 users, mean sessions 4, var(x) = 4, var(y) = 100, cov(x, y) = 10, ratio R = 2. Find the SE of R.
@A Var(R) = (100 + 2² × 4 − 2 × 2 × 10) / (1,000 × 4²) = (100 + 16 − 40) / 16,000 = 76 / 16,000 = 0.00475. SE = √0.00475 = 0.0689.
@P 12.P.15 | (IV) A random email raises the outcome by 0.5 and raises tool use by 0.25 (25 points). What is the IV estimate, and name the assumption that is most worrying.
@A 0.5 / 0.25 = 2.0 per user moved by the email. The exclusion restriction is the worry: the email must affect the outcome only through tool use. If the email itself changes behaviour (for instance it reminds people to log in), the estimate is biased.
@P 12.P.16 | (Propensity) A treated user has propensity 0.2 and a control user also has 0.2. What are their unstabilised IPW weights? What does that tell you about the two kinds of user?
@A Treated: 1/0.2 = 5. Control: 1/(1 − 0.2) = 1.25. A treated user at this score is rare, so they count for 5 users; controls at the same score are common, so each counts for 1.25. Rare combinations carry large weights, which is why extreme propensities make estimates unstable.
@P 12.P.17 | (Collider) A study of hospital patients finds that diabetes and cancer are negatively associated. Give a non-causal explanation.
@A Being in the hospital is a collider: both conditions raise the chance of admission. Among admitted patients, someone without diabetes is more likely to be there because of cancer, so the two look negatively linked. This is Berkson's bias. In the general population the association need not exist.
@P 12.P.18 | (RD) The estimated intercepts at the cutoff are 49.5 from the left and 54.5 from the right, SE of the jump 2.0. Report the effect and a 95% interval, and name one check you would run.
@A Jump = 54.5 − 49.5 = 5.0. Interval 5.0 ± 1.96 × 2.0 = (1.08, 8.92). Check that the density of scores does not pile up just above the cutoff (people gaming the threshold), and repeat with other bandwidths.
@P 12.P.19 | (Find the bug) "We matched treated to control users on propensity score, then also included 'purchases in the first week after exposure' as a covariate in the propensity model."
@A That covariate is measured after treatment and is a descendant of the treatment. Adjusting for it blocks part of the real effect (a mediator) or opens a collider path. Use only pre-treatment covariates.
@P 12.P.20 | (Scenario) Your team shipped a new onboarding flow to Germany only, with 14 months of weekly signup data for 8 other countries. Which method do you choose, and what do you plot before reporting?
@A Synthetic control or DiD with several countries as controls. Plot the pre-launch paths of Germany and the weighted control, and show the gap after launch. If Germany does not track the synthetic version before launch, do not trust the gap. Add a placebo test across the other countries and state the p-value limit (1 over countries plus 1).
