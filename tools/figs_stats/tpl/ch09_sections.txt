<h2>9.6 Checking a Bayesian Model: Prior and Posterior Predictive Checks</h2>
<p>A posterior is only as good as the model that produced it. Bayes' rule will happily give you a tight, confident interval for the rate of a Poisson model even if the data are nothing like Poisson. The Bayesian workflow therefore has two checks that use the model as a data generator.</p>
<ul><li><strong>Prior predictive check</strong> (before seeing data): draw parameters from the prior, simulate data from each, and ask "do these fake datasets look possible?" It catches priors that sound vague but imply absurd data.</li><li><strong>Posterior predictive check</strong> (after fitting): draw parameters from the posterior, simulate a replicated dataset for each, and compare a summary of the real data (variance, maximum, number of zeros) with the same summary in the replicas. If the real value sits far outside the replicas, the model cannot reproduce that feature of the world.</li></ul>
<div class="callout blue"><div class="lab">INTUITION</div><div class="txt">The posterior says which parameter values are plausible <em>if the model is right</em>. A predictive check asks whether the model, at its best parameters, can generate data that look like yours. If it cannot, no amount of posterior precision helps.</div></div>

<h2>9.7 Bayes Factors: Comparing Two Models</h2>
<p>Credible intervals describe a parameter inside one model. Sometimes the question is which of two models the data favour: "is this coin fair (θ = 0.5) or biased (θ unknown)?" The <strong>Bayes factor</strong> answers it:</p>
<div class="eq">BF<sub>10</sub> = p(data | H<sub>1</sub>) / p(data | H<sub>0</sub>)&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; posterior odds = BF<sub>10</sub> × prior odds</div>
<p>Each p(data | H) is the <strong>marginal likelihood</strong>: the probability of the data averaged over the prior for that hypothesis. For a coin with k heads in n flips, H<sub>0</sub> gives 0.5<sup>n</sup> and H<sub>1</sub> with a Beta(a, b) prior gives B(k + a, n − k + b) / B(a, b), where B is the Beta function. The binomial coefficient is common to both and cancels.</p>
<p>Read BF<sub>10</sub> = 3 as "the data are 3 times more probable under H<sub>1</sub>", mild evidence; 10 is strong, 100 is decisive. A BF below 1 is evidence <em>for</em> the null, something a p-value can never give you.</p>

@S
Step 1 | 62 heads of 100: p = 0.021, which a frequentist calls significant. The Bayes factor is only 2.2 (flat prior), anecdotal at best. The posterior probability of H1 is 0.69 at even prior odds.
Step 2 | 55 of 100: BF = 0.20, so the data favour the fair coin by about 5 to 1. A p-value of 0.37 could only say "no evidence against".
Step 3 | 5,100 of 10,000: p = 0.047 (significant) but BF = 0.093, which favours the null by 11 to 1. This is the <strong>Lindley paradox</strong>: with huge samples, a tiny deviation can be statistically significant and still more probable under "exactly fair" than under a wide alternative.
Step 4 | 5,250 of 10,000: p = 6e-7 and BF = 3,376. Now both agree.

@C red | COMMON MISTAKE | Using an extremely wide prior "to be uninformative" when computing a Bayes factor. Unlike in estimation, vague priors in a BF are not harmless: the wider the prior on H1, the more it penalises H1 (it spreads probability over values the data rule out), so the BF drifts toward the null for no good reason. Pick a prior that states what size of effect you consider plausible.
<h3>Going deeper</h3>
<p>Bayes factors for nested hypotheses can be computed from MCMC output with the Savage-Dickey ratio (posterior density at the null value divided by prior density there). For model choice among predictive models, practitioners often prefer cross-validation scores such as LOO (leave-one-out) because they are less sensitive to prior width and work when no model is exactly true.</p>
<h2>9.8 Hierarchical Models: Partial Pooling</h2>
<p>Suppose you run 8 stores and want each store's conversion rate. Two obvious answers are both flawed. <strong>No pooling</strong> uses each store's own rate. It is unbiased, but a store with 50 visitors and 0 sales gets a rate of exactly 0.000. <strong>Complete pooling</strong> uses one rate for everyone. It is stable but ignores real differences. A <strong>hierarchical model</strong> sits between them: each store has its own rate, and those rates are drawn from a shared population distribution, here Beta(α, β). The data estimate α and β, which decide how much to trust each store.</p>
<p>The posterior mean for store i is (k<sub>i</sub> + α) / (n<sub>i</sub> + α + β), which is a weighted average of the store's raw rate and the population mean, with weight n<sub>i</sub> / (n<sub>i</sub> + α + β) on the store's own data. Big stores keep their own numbers; small stores borrow strength. This is the same shrinkage idea as the mixed models in chapter 11 and ridge in chapter 7.</p>

<h2>9.9 MCMC Diagnostics: R-hat, ESS and Divergences</h2>
<p>Section 9.4 showed that a bad sampler can look fine. Three numbers tell you whether to trust a run. Always look at all three before you read a posterior.</p>
<ul><li><strong>R-hat</strong> compares variation between chains with variation inside chains. If all chains explore the same distribution, R-hat is near 1. Modern guidance is to require R-hat below 1.01. Compute it on split chains (each chain cut in half), which also catches a chain that drifts.</li><li><strong>Effective sample size (ESS)</strong>: draws in an MCMC chain are correlated, so 4,000 draws may carry the information of only 400 independent ones. The standard error of a posterior mean is sd / √ESS, not sd / √(draws).</li><li><strong>Divergences</strong> (Hamiltonian samplers such as NUTS): the trajectory fell off a steep, narrow part of the posterior. Even a handful means the sampler is missing part of the posterior, usually in hierarchical models with a small group variance. Fixes: reparameterise (non-centered form), tighten the prior, raise the target acceptance rate.</li></ul>
