<h2>10.7 Is the Model Finished? Residual Diagnostics and the Ljung-Box Test</h2>
<p>A forecasting model is finished when its residuals look like white noise: no pattern in the mean, constant spread, no autocorrelation. Any leftover autocorrelation is information the model failed to use. Look at the residual ACF first, then run a formal test.</p>
<p>The <strong>Ljung-Box test</strong> asks whether the first h autocorrelations of the residuals are jointly zero. Its statistic is Q = N(N + 2) Σ r<sub>k</sub>² / (N − k), summed for k = 1 to h, and it is compared with a chi-square distribution with h minus (number of fitted ARMA parameters) degrees of freedom. A small p-value says "structure remains".</p>
<p class="problem"><span class="pl">Problem 10.7.1</span> Simulate 300 points from an AR(1) with φ = 0.6. Fit two models: "just a mean" and AR(1). Compare the residual ACFs and the Ljung-Box test at 10 lags.</p>
@CODE ch10_ljung
@S
Step 1 | The white-noise band for the ACF is ±1.96 / √300 = ±0.113. Anything beyond it at a few lags is suspicious; one bar of 20 slightly outside is normal.
Step 2 | Mean-only residuals have lag-1, lag-2 and lag-3 autocorrelations of 0.607, 0.384, 0.270. All are far beyond the band, and they decay slowly, the AR signature.
Step 3 | The AR(1) fit gives φ̂ = 0.607 (truth 0.6). Its residual autocorrelations are -0.016, -0.011, 0.054: inside the band.
Step 4 | Ljung-Box: Q = 208.1 (p below 0.0001) for the mean-only model, Q = 10.8 (p = 0.289) for the AR(1) model. The degrees of freedom are 10 for the first and 9 for the second, since one parameter was fitted.
Answer | The mean-only model leaves structure behind; the AR(1) model does not. Only the second can be trusted for forecasting.
@C red | COMMON MISTAKE | Using the same degrees of freedom for a fitted model as for raw data. Subtract the number of fitted AR and MA parameters. Also choose the number of lags in advance (a common rule is min(10, N/5), or two seasonal cycles for seasonal data); testing many lag counts until one rejects is p-hacking.
<h2>10.8 Prediction Intervals and How They Grow</h2>
<p>A point forecast with no interval is half an answer. The interval should widen as you look further ahead, but how fast depends on the model. For an AR(1) with coefficient φ and shock SD σ, the h-step forecast error has variance σ² (1 + φ² + … + φ<sup>2(h−1)</sup>), which levels off at the long-run variance σ² / (1 − φ²). For a random walk, the variance is σ² h, which grows forever. Intervals from differenced (integrated) models keep growing; intervals from stationary models stop.</p>
<p class="problem"><span class="pl">Problem 10.8.1</span> An AR(1) with φ = 0.8, σ = 2, mean 50, and the last value at 50. Compute the forecast error SD at horizons 1, 2, 5, 10, 20 by the formula and by simulation (200,000 paths), and compare with a random walk.</p>
@CODE ch10_pi
@FIG ch10_pi.png | Two shaded bands of 95 percent prediction intervals for horizons 1 to 30. The random-walk band keeps widening, the AR(1) band flattens. | Prediction intervals reflect the model's belief about memory: stationary models forget shocks, random walks keep them forever. | The orange band is wide and flaring because a random walk never forgets a shock. The blue band stops growing at about ±6.5 because an AR(1) is pulled back toward its mean, so uncertainty at long horizons is just the ordinary spread of the series.
@S
Step 1 | Horizon 1 error SD = σ = 2.000. Both models agree here.
Step 2 | Horizon 2: 2 × √(1 + 0.64) = 2.561. Simulation gives 2.551.
Step 3 | Horizon 10: formula 3.314, simulation 3.312. The formula is right.
Step 4 | By horizon 20 the AR(1) SD is 3.333, equal to the long-run SD 2 / √(1 − 0.64) = 3.333, while the random walk is at 8.944.
Step 5 | A 95 percent interval at horizon 10 is 50 ± 6.5 for the AR(1) and ± 12.4 for a random walk with the same shock size. Choosing the wrong model roughly doubles the claimed uncertainty.
Answer | Interval width at long horizons is a statement about stationarity, so it is a model assumption, not a data fact. Check it with a stationarity test and a backtest of interval coverage.
@C red | COMMON MISTAKE | Reading ARIMA intervals as if they include parameter uncertainty and model uncertainty. They only include future shocks. Intervals are therefore usually too narrow; check empirical coverage in a rolling-origin backtest (does the "95 percent" interval contain the truth about 95 percent of the time?).
<h2>10.9 Rolling-Origin Cross-Validation: A Full Worked Comparison</h2>
<p>Section 10.6 introduced forecast error measures. Here is how to use them to choose among models without fooling yourself. Ordinary cross-validation shuffles rows, which lets the model peek at the future. Time series instead use a <strong>rolling origin</strong>: pick a cutoff, train on everything before it, forecast the next H steps, score, then move the cutoff forward and repeat. With an <em>expanding window</em> you keep all history; with a <em>sliding window</em> you keep only the last W points, which is better when the process changes.</p>
<p class="problem"><span class="pl">Problem 10.9.1</span> A monthly series of 144 points with trend 0.5 per month, a yearly seasonal pattern and noise SD 3. Compare four forecasters over 11 rolling origins (first at month 72, then every 6 months), each forecasting 12 months ahead: naive (last value), seasonal naive (same month last year), drift (straight line through first and last point) and a regression with trend plus 11 month dummies. Report mean MAE, the worst origin and MASE (MAE divided by the in-sample seasonal-naive MAE on the first 72 points).</p>
@CODE ch10_cv
@S
Step 1 | Naive: MAE 8.57. It ignores the seasonal swing of about ±10 and the trend, so it errs by roughly the size of the swing.
Step 2 | Seasonal naive: MAE 6.15, MASE 0.97. It captures the seasonal shape but not the trend, so it is always about 6 below (0.5 × 12) and carries a year of noise.
Step 3 | Drift: MAE 8.67, the worst origin 13.46. A straight line with no seasonality is worse than naive at some origins.
Step 4 | Trend plus seasonal dummies: MAE 2.70, MASE 0.43, worst origin 3.93. It matches the way the data were made. The noise SD is 3, so an MAE near 2.4 (0.8 × 3) is the floor for any forecaster; 2.70 is close.
Step 5 | MASE below 1 means better than the seasonal-naive benchmark; it is also unit-free, so you can compare across series.
Answer | Pick the regression. It wins at every origin on average and in the worst case, and it is simple. Always include the naive and seasonal-naive baselines: if a complex model cannot beat them, it is not earning its complexity.
@C red | COMMON MISTAKE | Evaluating on a single train/test split at the end of the series. One origin can be lucky or unlucky (the worst-origin column above is 27 to 55 percent higher than the mean, depending on the model). Use several origins, report the mean and the spread.
<h2>10.10 Regression with Autocorrelated Errors (ARIMAX)</h2>
<p>When a series depends on outside drivers (promotions, temperature, price), you want to estimate their effect and still respect that the leftover noise has memory. The model is y<sub>t</sub> = β<sub>0</sub> + β<sub>1</sub> x<sub>t</sub> + u<sub>t</sub> where u<sub>t</sub> follows an ARMA process. If you ignore the memory and use ordinary least squares, the coefficient estimate is still unbiased, but its standard error is wrong, usually too small when both the driver and the errors are positively autocorrelated. You then see "significant" effects that are not.</p>
<p class="problem"><span class="pl">Problem 10.10.1</span> Daily sales = 50 + 3 × promo + AR(1) noise with ρ = 0.7, 120 days, promos in 5-day blocks. Simulate 1,000 datasets. Compare OLS with a Cochrane-Orcutt fit (estimate ρ from the residuals, transform both sides by subtracting ρ times the previous day, refit). Check whether each reported 95 percent interval really covers the truth 95 percent of the time.</p>
@CODE ch10_regarma
@S
Step 1 | Both methods estimate the promo effect without bias: 3.002 and 3.003 against a true 3.0.
Step 2 | OLS reports an average SE of 0.246, but the real spread of its estimates across datasets is 0.279. The reported SE is about 12 percent too small.
Step 3 | As a result, nominal 95 percent OLS intervals cover the truth only 91.6 percent of the time.
Step 4 | The AR(1)-errors fit reports SE 0.229 against a true spread of 0.225, and its intervals cover 95.9 percent.
Answer | Same point estimate, honest uncertainty only when the error memory is modelled. In practice use <code>SARIMAX(y, exog=X, order=(1,0,0))</code> in statsmodels, or heteroscedasticity and autocorrelation consistent (Newey-West) standard errors, if you only need robust SEs.
@C red | COMMON MISTAKE | Regressing a trending series on a trending predictor and calling the high R² and tiny p-value an effect. Two unrelated random walks routinely show R² above 0.5. Difference both series (or model the errors as ARIMA) before reading the coefficient.

<h2>10.11 ETS in One Table, and Intermittent Demand</h2>
<p>Exponential smoothing (section 10.5) is a family. The <strong>ETS</strong> framework names a model by three letters: Error (additive A or multiplicative M), Trend (none N, additive A, damped Ad) and Seasonal (N, A or M). ETS(A,N,N) is simple exponential smoothing, ETS(A,A,N) is Holt's linear method, ETS(A,Ad,A) is damped-trend Holt-Winters with additive seasonality. Software fits all candidates, picks by AICc, and gives proper prediction intervals.</p>
<div class="tablewrap"><table class="data"><thead><tr><th>Pattern in the data</th><th>Choose</th><th>Why</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td>Flat level, noise</td><td>ETS(A,N,N)</td><td>Forecast is a smoothed level</td></tr><tr><td>Steady trend</td><td>ETS(A,A,N), better ETS(A,Ad,N)</td><td>Damping stops the trend from running forever</td></tr><tr><td>Seasonal swing of constant size</td><td>Additive seasonal</td><td>Swing does not grow with the level</td></tr><tr><td>Seasonal swing that grows with the level</td><td>Multiplicative seasonal (or log the series)</td><td>Swing is proportional to the level</td></tr><tr><td>Many zeros, occasional spikes</td><td>Croston or SBA, not plain ETS</td><td>Level and spacing of demand are two separate processes</td></tr></tbody></table></div>
<p><strong>Intermittent demand</strong> (spare parts, niche products) breaks ordinary methods because most periods are zero. <strong>Croston's method</strong> smooths the non-zero demand sizes z and the gaps q between demands separately and forecasts z / q per period. The <strong>SBA</strong> variant multiplies by (1 − α/2) to remove a small upward bias.</p>
<p class="problem"><span class="pl">Problem 10.11.1</span> 400 periods where demand occurs with probability 0.25 and, when it does, averages 5 units (true mean 1.25 per period). Compare SES, Croston, Croston-SBA and "always forecast zero" on forecasts for periods 101 to 400.</p>
@CODE ch10_croston
@S
Step 1 | 75 percent of periods are zero. The sample mean is 1.24, near the true 1.25.
Step 2 | SES, Croston and SBA all forecast close to the true mean (1.19, 1.23, 1.17). Croston's forecasts are steadier over time (SD 0.30 against 0.62 for SES) because spikes do not whip its level around.
Step 3 | RMSE is about 2.25 for all three, and 2.51 for the zero forecast. RMSE rewards forecasting the mean.
Step 4 | MAE tells the opposite story: the zero forecast wins at 1.16 against about 1.75. MAE is minimised by the median, which is 0 when most periods are zero.
Answer | Scoring intermittent demand with MAE or MAPE rewards a useless forecast. Use RMSE, or better, a measure aimed at what you plan: for stocking decisions, score the quantile (service-level) forecast with pinball loss.
@C red | COMMON MISTAKE | Evaluating intermittent demand with MAPE. Actual zero makes the percentage error undefined or infinite, and analysts then drop the zero periods, which removes the very thing that makes the problem hard.
<h2>10.12 Changing Variance: A First Look at GARCH</h2>
<p>Financial returns, sensor noise and demand shocks often show <strong>volatility clustering</strong>: calm weeks followed by wild ones. The returns themselves look uncorrelated, but their squares are not. The GARCH(1,1) model describes this with two equations: r<sub>t</sub> = σ<sub>t</sub> ε<sub>t</sub> and σ<sub>t</sub>² = ω + α r<sub>t−1</sub>² + β σ<sub>t−1</sub>². Today's variance is a baseline plus a reaction to yesterday's surprise plus memory of yesterday's variance. Persistence is α + β; the long-run variance is ω / (1 − α − β).</p>
<p class="problem"><span class="pl">Problem 10.12.1</span> Simulate 2,500 daily returns from a GARCH(1,1) with ω = 0.05, α = 0.10, β = 0.85. Test whether returns and squared returns are autocorrelated, fit the model by maximum likelihood, check the standardized residuals, and compare 1-day 99 percent Value-at-Risk (VaR) from GARCH and from a constant volatility.</p>
@CODE ch10_garch

Middle and right: ACF bars for returns and for squared returns with dashed significance lines. | Returns have no memory, squared returns do. The volatility band breathes with the size of recent moves. | In the middle panel all bars sit inside the dashed lines: no linear predictability. In the right panel the bars at the first lags are well above the lines: big moves follow big moves. In the left panel the orange band widens in turbulent stretches and narrows in calm ones.
@S
Step 1 | ACF of returns at lags 1 to 3 is about 0 (Ljung-Box p = 0.749). ACF of squared returns is 0.094, 0.067, 0.126 with p below 0.0001: the variance has memory.
Step 2 | MLE gives ω = 0.054, α = 0.083, β = 0.855. Persistence 0.938; long-run variance 0.87 (truth 1.0). Estimates are close; α and β are individually noisy, but their sum is well determined.

Step 4 | Overall 99 percent VaR breaches are 1.2 percent with GARCH and 1.1 percent with constant volatility: no difference on average.

Answer | A constant-variance model is right on average and wrong when it matters. GARCH puts the risk where it is.
@C red | COMMON MISTAKE | Fitting GARCH to a series that still has trend or autocorrelated mean. Model the mean first (a constant, or ARMA), then fit GARCH to the residuals. Also, normal innovations underestimate extreme tails; practitioners use Student-t innovations, and asymmetric versions (GJR, EGARCH) because losses raise volatility more than gains.
