<h2>8.6 Rates and Exposure: Offsets Done Properly</h2>
<p>Section 8.3 mentioned the offset in one line. It deserves a full example, because forgetting it silently flips conclusions. A count is only comparable between rows if every row had the same chance to produce events. If one site was watched for 5 days and another for 60, raw counts say more about the watching than about the sites. The fix is to model the <em>rate</em> (events per unit of exposure) and let the model handle the exposure.</p>
<div class="callout blue"><div class="lab">INTUITION</div><div class="txt">Write expected count = rate × exposure. Take logs: ln(count) = ln(rate) + ln(exposure). The second term has no coefficient, it is just added in. That fixed term is the <strong>offset</strong>: a predictor whose coefficient is forced to be exactly 1.</div></div>
<p class="problem"><span class="pl">Problem 8.6.1</span> 300 monitored sites, half with a new alert rule (treated). Treated sites were watched for 5 to 25 days, controls for 20 to 60 days. The true effect of the rule is +0.4 on the log rate (a 49 percent higher incident rate per day). Fit Poisson regression with and without the offset ln(days).</p>

@S
Step 1 | The raw counts per site are lower for treated sites, because treated sites were watched for 15 days on average and controls for 40.
Step 2 | Without the offset the model compares counts directly. It reports a coefficient of -0.539, a rate ratio of 0.58, and a tight SE of 0.039. It concludes the rule cuts incidents by 42 percent. That is wrong in sign.

<h3>Going deeper: why the offset is not just a predictor</h3>
<p>You could add ln(days) as an ordinary predictor and let the model estimate its coefficient. If that coefficient comes out near 1, the "rate" assumption holds. If it comes out at 0.6 or 1.4, events do not scale linearly with exposure (bursts early, saturation later), and a simple rate model is the wrong shape. Fit it both ways when exposure varies widely, and report the check.</p>

<h2>8.7 Excess Zeros: Zero-Inflated and Hurdle Models</h2>
<p>Many count variables have more zeros than any Poisson or negative binomial can explain: purchases per customer (many people never buy), claims per policy, sick days per employee. The usual story is two groups mixed together. Some units are <strong>structural zeros</strong> (they cannot produce events, such as never-buyers) and the rest follow an ordinary count process that also produces some zeros by chance.</p>
<div class="eq">P(Y = 0) = π + (1 − π) e<sup>−λ</sup>,&nbsp;&nbsp; P(Y = k) = (1 − π) e<sup>−λ</sup> λ<sup>k</sup> / k!&nbsp; for k ≥ 1</div>
<p>This is the <strong>zero-inflated Poisson (ZIP)</strong>. Here π is the share of structural zeros and λ is the mean among the active group. A <strong>hurdle model</strong> is the close cousin: one binary model decides zero versus positive, and a truncated count model describes the positives. In a hurdle, all zeros are "structural". In ZIP, zeros come from two sources.</p>
<p class="problem"><span class="pl">Problem 8.7.1</span> 2,000 customers. 30 percent never buy; the rest buy Poisson(3) items. Check how bad the plain Poisson is, then fit ZIP by maximum likelihood.</p>

<h2>8.8 Ordinal and Multinomial Outcomes</h2>
<p>Logistic regression handles two outcomes. Real labels often have more. Two cases need different tools and mixing them up wastes information or breaks assumptions.</p>
<ul><li><strong>Ordered categories</strong> (satisfaction low / mid / high, severity grades): use <strong>ordinal (proportional odds) logistic regression</strong>. One slope β for each predictor, plus thresholds. The model says logit P(Y ≤ k) = θ<sub>k</sub> − βx, so the same odds ratio e<sup>β</sup> applies at every cut point.</li><li><strong>Unordered categories</strong> (reason for churn: price / service / moved): use <strong>multinomial logistic regression</strong>. One coefficient vector for each class except a reference, and the probabilities come from a softmax.</li></ul>
<p class="problem"><span class="pl">Problem 8.8.1</span> Simulate 1,500 survey responses with three ordered levels driven by one standardized feature, fit the proportional odds model by maximum likelihood, then fit a 3-class unordered model for churn reasons on two features.</p>

<h2>8.9 Judging a Logistic Model: AUC, Calibration and Separation</h2>
<p>A logistic model can fail in two different ways: it can rank cases badly, or it can rank them well but give the wrong probabilities. They need different checks.</p>
<ul><li><strong>Discrimination</strong> is the ability to put positives above negatives. Measure it with the ROC curve and <strong>AUC</strong>, the probability that a random positive gets a higher score than a random negative. 0.5 is a coin flip, 1.0 is perfect.</li><li><strong>Calibration</strong> is whether "30 percent" means 30 percent. Sort cases by predicted probability, cut into 10 equal groups, and compare the average prediction with the observed rate in each. The <strong>Hosmer-Lemeshow</strong> statistic sums (observed − expected)² / (n p̄ (1 − p̄)) over groups, and is compared with chi-square on (groups − 2) degrees of freedom when computed on the training data.</li></ul>

<h3>Going deeper</h3>
<p>AUC is also the Mann-Whitney U statistic divided by n<sub>1</sub>n<sub>0</sub>, which links this chapter to the rank tests in chapter 4. Hosmer-Lemeshow depends on how you form the groups and has low power for large samples. Many practitioners prefer a calibration plot with confidence bands and the Brier score (mean squared error of the probabilities), which rewards both discrimination and calibration.</p>
