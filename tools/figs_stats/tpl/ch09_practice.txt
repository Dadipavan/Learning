<p><strong>13 (medium, Bayes factor).</strong> The Bayes factor for H1 over H0 is 3 and the prior odds are 1 to 1. Give the posterior probability of H1. Then redo it when you held prior odds of 1 to 4 against H1. <strong>Answer:</strong> Posterior odds = 3 × 1 = 3, so P(H1) = 3/4 = 0.75. With prior odds 1/4: posterior odds = 3 × 0.25 = 0.75, so P(H1) = 0.75 / 1.75 = 0.429.</p>
<p><strong>14 (medium, predictive check).</strong> Your Poisson model for daily orders has posterior mean rate 6. The real data have variance 24, and in 4,000 replicated datasets the variance never exceeds 9. What do you conclude and what do you try next? <strong>Answer:</strong> The model cannot reproduce the spread: the data are overdispersed (variance/mean = 4). Refit with a negative binomial (or add predictors that explain the day-to-day swings) and repeat the check.</p>
<p><strong>15 (medium, R-hat).</strong> Four chains give R-hat = 1.25 for one parameter and 1.00 for the others. May you report the other parameters? <strong>Answer:</strong> Not safely. R-hat 1.25 shows the chains disagree about at least one parameter, and parameters are usually linked. Run longer, check traces, reparameterise, and only report once all R-hats are below 1.01.</p>
<p><strong>16 (medium, ESS).</strong> 4,000 draws, ESS = 200, posterior sd = 2. Compute the Monte Carlo standard error of the mean and compare with the naive value. <strong>Answer:</strong> MCSE = 2 / √200 = 0.141. Naive: 2 / √4,000 = 0.032. The honest error is 4.5 times larger.</p>
<p><strong>17 (medium, partial pooling).</strong> The population prior is Beta(9, 91), so α + β = 100. A store has 36 conversions in 300 visits. Compute its posterior mean and the weight on its own data. <strong>Answer:</strong> Posterior mean = (36 + 9) / (300 + 100) = 0.1125. Weight on own data = 300 / 400 = 0.75. Check: 0.75 × 0.12 + 0.25 × 0.09 = 0.1125.</p>
<p><strong>18 (hard, Lindley).</strong> A test with n = 100,000 gives a p-value of 0.04 and a Bayes factor of 0.2. A colleague says the two methods contradict each other. Explain. <strong>Answer:</strong> They answer different questions. The p-value asks how surprising the data are if the null is exactly true. The BF compares the null against a specific, wide alternative. With huge n, a tiny difference is detectable (small p) but is more probable under the exact null than under a wide alternative that spreads its bets over large effects. Check the effect size and whether the alternative's prior is sensible.</p>
<p><strong>19 (hard, scenario).</strong> A hierarchical model with 30 groups reports 40 divergences and R-hat = 1.00 everywhere. Is it fine? <strong>Answer:</strong> No. R-hat can miss regions the sampler never reaches. Divergences signal exactly that, typically near a very small group variance (the funnel). Reparameterise with non-centered group effects, put a sensible prior on the group sd, raise the target acceptance rate, and re-check.</p>
<p><strong>20 (hard, interview).</strong> When would you choose a hierarchical Bayesian model over fitting each segment separately? <strong>Answer:</strong> When there are many segments, some with little data, and you expect them to be similar but not identical (stores, campaigns, doctors, regions). Partial pooling gives stable estimates for small segments and lets large segments speak for themselves, and the model gives honest uncertainty for each one.</p>
