@P 11.P.10 | (KM by hand) Five customers have (time, event): (3,1), (5,1), (5,0), (8,1), (10,0). Compute S(8) and the median survival.
@A At t = 3: n = 5, d = 1, factor 4/5 = 0.8. At t = 5: n = 4 (times 5, 5, 8, 10), d = 1 (the other 5 is censored), factor 3/4, S = 0.6. At t = 8: n = 2, d = 1, factor 1/2, S = 0.3. So S(8) = 0.30. The curve first falls to 0.5 or below at t = 8, so the median is 8 (the first time S ≤ 0.5).
@P 11.P.11 | (Design effect) A study has 600 rows from clusters of 20 rows each, ICC 0.1. What is the effective sample size?
@A deff = 1 + (20 − 1) × 0.1 = 2.9. Effective n = 600 / 2.9 = 206.9, about 207 independent observations. Standard errors are √2.9 = 1.70 times larger than the naive ones.
@P 11.P.12 | (Hazard ratio) A Cox model gives HR = 1.25 per support ticket. What is the hazard ratio for a customer with 4 tickets compared with one with none, and what is β?
@A β = ln(1.25) = 0.2231. For 4 tickets the log hazard ratio is 4 × 0.2231 = 0.8926, so HR = e<sup>0.8926</sup> = 1.25<sup>4</sup> = 2.44. Hazard ratios multiply across steps, they do not add.
@P 11.P.13 | (Competing risks) Voluntary cancellation has hazard 0.02 per month and payment failure 0.06 per month, both constant. Find the true chance of voluntary cancellation by month 36 and compare it with the naive 1 − KM answer.
@A Total hazard 0.08. CIF(36) = (0.02 / 0.08) × (1 − e<sup>−0.08 × 36</sup>) = 0.25 × (1 − e<sup>−2.88</sup>) = 0.25 × 0.9439 = 0.236. Naive: 1 − e<sup>−0.02 × 36</sup> = 1 − e<sup>−0.72</sup> = 0.513, more than double the truth, because it pretends payment failures cannot happen.
@P 11.P.14 | (Shrinkage) τ² = 100, σ² = 400, μ = 50. A group has n = 9 observations and mean 70. Give the partially pooled estimate.
@A w = 100 / (100 + 400/9) = 100 / 144.44 = 0.692. Estimate = 50 + 0.692 × (70 − 50) = 63.85. Its own mean (70) is pulled about a third of the way toward the overall mean.
@P 11.P.15 | (Interpretation) A Cox model reports HR = 0.50 for a loyalty programme, but the proportional hazards test gives p = 0.001 and the effect-over-time plot shows the HR rising from 0.3 in month 1 to 0.9 at month 12. What do you tell the product manager?
@A "One number is misleading. The programme cuts the risk of leaving a lot in the first months, but the benefit fades to almost nothing by month 12. I will report hazard ratios by period (for example 0 to 3, 3 to 6, 6 to 12 months) and check whether retention gains last or are only delayed."
@P 11.P.16 | (Find the bug) "Customers who contacted support at any time during their first year had 40% longer tenure, so support contact improves retention."
@A Contacting support at any time requires still being a customer at that time (immortal time bias), and long-lived customers have more time to make contact. Model support contact as a time-varying covariate, or start the clock at the first contact and compare with customers still active at the same age.
@P 11.P.17 | (Data layout) A customer joins at month 0, upgrades at month 3, and is censored at month 10. Write the counting-process rows.
@A Two rows: (start 0, stop 3, upgraded 0, event 0) and (start 3, stop 10, upgraded 1, event 0). The event flag stays 0 on both rows because the customer is censored, not cancelled.
@P 11.P.18 | (Quiz, 4 questions) (a) What does the ICC measure? (b) What happens to a group's shrinkage weight when its n grows? (c) Which method gives the population-average effect, mixed model or GEE? (d) Why is 1 − KM biased in competing risks?
@A (a) The share of total variance that lies between clusters, which is also the correlation of two rows from the same cluster. (b) It moves toward 1: the group's own mean is trusted more. (c) GEE. (d) It treats the competing event as censoring, as if it could never happen, so it estimates the cancellation probability in an imaginary world without the other cause.
