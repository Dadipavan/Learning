<p><strong>13 (medium).</strong> An ordinal model has β = 0.83 per SD. What is the odds ratio for a customer who is 2 SD higher? <strong>Answer:</strong> The log odds change by 2 × 0.83 = 1.66, so OR = e^1.66 = 5.26. Odds do not scale linearly with SD, they multiply.</p>
<p><strong>14 (medium, offset).</strong> A site had 18 incidents in 40 days. The rate ratio for a new rule is 1.5. How many incidents do you expect in 25 days with the rule? <strong>Answer:</strong> Baseline rate = 18/40 = 0.45 per day. With the rule: 0.45 × 1.5 = 0.675 per day. Over 25 days: 0.675 × 25 = 16.9 incidents.</p>
<p><strong>15 (medium, ZIP).</strong> A ZIP has π = 0.30 and λ = 2. Find P(Y = 0) and E[Y]. <strong>Answer:</strong> P(0) = 0.30 + 0.70 × e^(-2) = 0.30 + 0.70 × 0.1353 = 0.395. E[Y] = (1 − π) λ = 0.70 × 2 = 1.4.</p>
<p><strong>16 (medium, AUC by hand).</strong> Positives have scores 0.9, 0.6, 0.4. Negatives have 0.7, 0.3, 0.2. Compute AUC. <strong>Answer:</strong> There are 9 positive-negative pairs. The 0.9 positive wins all 3. The 0.6 positive beats 0.3 and 0.2 (2 wins), losing to 0.7. The 0.4 positive beats 0.3 and 0.2 (2 wins). Total 7 of 9, AUC = 0.778.</p>
<p><strong>17 (medium, calibration).</strong> In one decile of 500 users the model's average predicted probability is 0.20 and 120 users converted. Compute this group's Hosmer-Lemeshow term and say what it suggests. <strong>Answer:</strong> Expected = 100, observed = 120. Term = (120 − 100)² / (500 × 0.20 × 0.80) = 400 / 80 = 5.0. A single term of 5 against chi-square scale where the typical term is about 1 suggests under-prediction in this group (it converts 24 percent, not 20).</p>
<p><strong>18 (medium, softmax).</strong> A 3-class model has logits 0, 0.5 and −0.5 (class 0 is the reference). Find the probabilities. <strong>Answer:</strong> e^0 = 1, e^0.5 = 1.649, e^−0.5 = 0.607. Sum = 3.255. Probabilities are 0.307, 0.507 and 0.186.</p>
<p><strong>19 (hard, scenario).</strong> A daily count model for support tickets is fitted with Poisson and the Pearson dispersion is 1.1, but 40 percent of days have zero tickets while the model predicts 8 percent. What do you check next and why? <strong>Answer:</strong> Dispersion near 1 does not rule out excess zeros, because very high counts on some days can offset zeros in the variance. Check the weekday pattern first: zeros may be weekends and holidays, which a predictor can capture. If zeros remain in excess after adding it, fit ZIP or a hurdle model and compare AIC.</p>
<p><strong>20 (hard, interview).</strong> A teammate codes survey answers 1 to 5 and fits OLS. When is that acceptable and when should you switch? <strong>Answer:</strong> It is a fine first look when the scale has 5 or more levels, answers are not piled at the ends, and you only need the sign and rough size of an effect. Switch to ordinal logistic when answers pile at the ends, when you need probabilities of each level, or when the result will drive a decision where the unequal gaps between levels matter.</p>
<p><strong>21 (hard, design).</strong> You must predict churn reason (price, service, moved, other) from account features and give a retention team ranked lists per reason. Which model, which metric? <strong>Answer:</strong> The reasons have no order, so use multinomial logistic. Judge by log loss for the probabilities and per-class AUC (one-vs-rest) for the ranked lists. Check calibration per class, since the team will act on the probabilities.</p>
