<p><strong>13 (medium, Ljung-Box).</strong> Residuals of an ARIMA(1,0,1) fitted to 200 points give a Ljung-Box Q = 18.3 at 10 lags. The chi-square 5 percent critical values are 18.31 (df 10), 16.92 (df 9) and 15.51 (df 8). What do you conclude? <strong>Answer:</strong> Two parameters were fitted, so df = 10 − 2 = 8 and the critical value is 15.51. Q = 18.3 exceeds it, so residual autocorrelation remains. (With df = 10 it would have looked borderline-pass at 18.31, which shows why the correction matters.) Add terms or a seasonal part and re-check.</p>
<p><strong>14 (medium, interval growth).</strong> A random-walk forecast has one-step error SD 3. Give the 95 percent interval half-width at horizons 1 and 16. <strong>Answer:</strong> Half-width = 1.96 × 3 × √h. h = 1: 5.88. h = 16: 1.96 × 3 × 4 = 23.5. Four times the horizon doubled twice.</p>
<p><strong>15 (medium, AR interval).</strong> An AR(1) with φ = 0.5 and σ = 4. Find the 2-step forecast error SD and the long-run SD. <strong>Answer:</strong> 2-step: 4 × √(1 + 0.25) = 4.472. Long run: 4 / √(1 − 0.25) = 4.619. Already at 2 steps you are at 97 percent of the long-run value.</p>
<p><strong>16 (medium, MASE).</strong> A model has MAE 4.2 on a rolling-origin test. The seasonal-naive MAE on the training data is 6.0. Compute MASE and say whether the model beats the benchmark. <strong>Answer:</strong> MASE = 4.2 / 6.0 = 0.70. Below 1: it beats the seasonal-naive benchmark by 30 percent (note the benchmark scale uses the training data).</p>
<p><strong>17 (medium, Croston).</strong> Demand sizes smoothed to z = 6 units and the smoothed interval between demands is q = 4 periods. Give the Croston forecast per period and the SBA forecast with α = 0.1. <strong>Answer:</strong> Croston = 6/4 = 1.5 per period. SBA = (1 − 0.1/2) × 1.5 = 0.95 × 1.5 = 1.425.</p>
<p><strong>18 (hard, GARCH by hand).</strong> ω = 0.05, α = 0.10, β = 0.85. Yesterday's variance was 1.0 and yesterday's return was −2.0. Find today's variance and volatility, then the long-run variance. <strong>Answer:</strong> σ² = 0.05 + 0.10 × 4 + 0.85 × 1.0 = 0.05 + 0.40 + 0.85 = 1.30, so σ = 1.140. Long run: 0.05 / (1 − 0.95) = 1.0. The shock pushes variance 30 percent above its long-run level and it decays by 0.95 per day.</p>
<p><strong>19 (hard, scenario).</strong> A weekly retail forecast was chosen by comparing models on the last 8 weeks only. It was great in testing and poor in production. List three likely causes. <strong>Answer:</strong> (1) One origin and 8 points is a noisy estimate, so the winner may be luck; use rolling origins. (2) The test window may be unusual (holiday, promotion), so the model fitted that window rather than the typical one. (3) Information leakage, such as using a predictor that is only known after the fact, or tuning on the test window and then reporting it as the test result.</p>
<p><strong>20 (hard, interview).</strong> Explain why a regression of one trending series on another can show a tiny p-value with no real relationship, and how you avoid it. <strong>Answer:</strong> Trending (non-stationary) series share a shape just by drifting, so the residuals are strongly autocorrelated and the OLS standard errors are far too small. This is spurious regression. Avoid it by differencing both series, by modelling the errors as ARIMA, or by testing for cointegration when a long-run link is plausible. Always look at the residual ACF.</p>
