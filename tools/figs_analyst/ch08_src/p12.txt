
@@H2 8.12 Honest Charts: How Charts Lie and How to Fix Them
@@CALL blue|INTUITION|A chart is a claim. Most misleading charts are not made by liars; they are made by tired people who accepted a default, or who found the scale that made their number look good. Your job is the opposite: choose the scale and window that make the truth hard to miss, and tell the reader what you chose. If a viewer who sees only the picture would be wrong about the numbers, the chart has failed, whatever your intentions were.
<h3>Lie 1: the truncated bar axis, and the lie factor</h3>
<p>Edward Tufte's <strong>lie factor</strong> is the size of the effect shown in the picture divided by the size of the effect in the data. An honest graphic has a lie factor close to 1 (he suggests between 0.95 and 1.05).</p>
<div class="eq">lie factor = (visual ratio) / (real ratio)</div>
@@PY lie_truncated
#H
city = pd.Series({"Delhi": 3298, "Mumbai": 3047, "Pune": 2995})
start = 2900
visual = (city["Delhi"] - start) / (city["Pune"] - start)
real = city["Delhi"] / city["Pune"]
print(f"visible bar heights with axis starting at {start}: Delhi {city['Delhi'] - start}, Pune {city['Pune'] - start}")
print(f"visual ratio = {visual:.2f}; real ratio = {real:.2f}; lie factor = {visual / real:.1f}")
fig, (a, b) = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(9, 4.2))
a.bar(city.index, city.values, color=[BLUE, GREY, GREY], width=0.6)
a.set_ylim(start, 3400); a.set_title("Misleading: axis starts at 2,900")
a.text(0.97, 0.93, "Delhi looks 4.2x Pune", transform=a.transAxes, ha="right", va="top", color=RED, fontweight="bold")
b.bar(city.index, city.values, color=[BLUE, GREY, GREY], width=0.6)
b.set_ylim(0, 4000); b.set_title("Honest: axis starts at 0")
b.text(0.97, 0.93, "Delhi is really 1.10x Pune", transform=b.transAxes, ha="right", va="top", color=TEAL, fontweight="bold")
for ax in (a, b):
    ax.set_yticks(ax.get_yticks()[ax.get_yticks() >= ax.get_ylim()[0]])
    ax.bar_label(ax.containers[0], fmt="{:,.0f}", padding=3, fontsize=11)
fig.tight_layout()
fig.savefig("ch08v_lie_truncated.png", dpi=110)
print("saved")
@@END
@@FIG lie_truncated|Left: three bars of city sales on an axis starting at 2900 where Delhi looks four times Pune. Right: the same bars from zero where they look nearly equal|Same numbers, two axes. The left chart's lie factor is 3.8.
@@PROBLEM 8.12.1|A bar chart of Delhi 3,298 and Pune 2,995 uses an axis that starts at 2,900. Compute the lie factor.
@@STEPS
Formula ## visual ratio = (Delhi - start) / (Pune - start); real ratio = Delhi / Pune; lie factor = visual / real.
Plug in ## Visible heights: 3,298 - 2,900 = 398 and 2,995 - 2,900 = 95.
Compute ## Visual ratio = 398 / 95 = 4.19. Real ratio = 3,298 / 2,995 = 1.10. Lie factor = 4.19 / 1.10 = 3.8.
Check ## With the axis at zero, the ratio is 3,298 / 2,995 = 1.10 and the lie factor is 1.0.
Meaning ## The chart exaggerates the gap about 3.8 times. Bars encode value by length from the baseline, so cutting it breaks the code. (Line charts and dot plots encode value by position, so they may use a non-zero axis, but should say so.)
@@END
<h3>Lie 2: the cherry-picked window</h3>
<p>The same series can tell opposite stories depending on which stretch you show. Choosing the window after seeing the data, and not saying so, is the oldest trick in the book.</p>
@@PY lie_range
#H
#MONTHLY
fig, axs = plt.subplots(1, 3, figsize=(11, 3.8), layout="constrained")
windows = [("Nov 2024 to Jan 2025", "2024-11", "2025-01", RED), ("Jan to Oct 2024", "2024-01", "2024-10", ORANGE), ("All 18 months", "2024-01", "2025-06", BLUE)]
titles = ["'Sales crashed'", "'Steady growth'", "The honest picture"]
for ax, (label, a, b, color), title in zip(axs, windows, titles):
    s = monthly.loc[a:b]
    ax.plot(s.index, s.values, color=color, lw=2.8, marker="o", ms=4)
    ax.set_title(title, fontsize=13); ax.set_xlabel(label)
    ax.set_xticks(s.index[::max(1, len(s) // 4)], [d.strftime("%b %y") for d in s.index[::max(1, len(s) // 4)]], fontsize=9)
    ax.set_ylim(0, 700)
axs[0].set_ylabel("Sales (Rs thousand)")
fig.savefig("ch08v_lie_range.png", dpi=110)
print(f"Dec 2024 to Jan 2025: {350 / 620 - 1:+.1%}")
print(f"Jan 2024 to Oct 2024: {470 / 310 - 1:+.1%}")
print(f"Jan 2024 to Jun 2025: {455 / 310 - 1:+.1%}; Jan 2025 vs Jan 2024: {350 / 310 - 1:+.1%}")
print("saved")
@@END
@@FIG lie_range|Three line charts of the same sales data: a window of three months showing a sharp fall, a window of ten months showing steady growth, and the full eighteen months showing a December peak|One data set, three windows. Only the third shows the festive peak and that January 2025 is still 13% above January 2024.
<p><strong>How to read it.</strong> Left: Dec to Jan is a fall of 43.5%: a "crash" if that is all you show. Middle: Jan to Oct 2024 is growth of 51.6%: "steady growth" if you stop before the festive months. Right: the whole series shows a seasonal peak and a January that is 12.9% above the previous January. The fix is a rule: <strong>choose the window by the question, not by the answer</strong> (the whole season, a full year, or the same period last year), and compare like with like (January against January).</p>
<h3>Lie 3: geometry that exaggerates (radius, uneven spacing)</h3>
@@PY lie_scales
#H
vals = np.array([1, 2, 3])
years = np.array([2019, 2020, 2021, 2024]); sales = np.array([100, 120, 140, 160])
fig, (a, b, c) = plt.subplots(1, 3, figsize=(11, 3.8))
for i, v in enumerate(vals):                         # radius proportional to value: area grows with the SQUARE
    a.add_patch(plt.Circle((i * 2.2 + 1, 2.9), 0.3 * v, color=RED))
    a.add_patch(plt.Circle((i * 2.2 + 1, 0.85), 0.3 * np.sqrt(v) * 1.15, color=TEAL))
a.set(xlim=(0, 7), ylim=(-0.4, 4.2), aspect="equal"); a.axis("off")
a.text(3.5, 4.0, "Radius = value (wrong)", ha="center", color=RED, fontweight="bold")
a.text(3.5, -0.2, "Area = value (right)", ha="center", color=TEAL, fontweight="bold")
b.plot(range(4), sales, color=RED, lw=2.8, marker="o")             # categories equally spaced although years are not
b.set_xticks(range(4), years); b.set_title("Wrong: 2021 to 2024 squeezed", fontsize=12); b.set_ylim(80, 180)
c.plot(years, sales, color=TEAL, lw=2.8, marker="o")
c.set_xticks(years); c.set_title("Right: true time axis", fontsize=12); c.set_ylim(80, 180)
for ax in (b, c): ax.set_ylabel("Sales index")
fig.tight_layout()
fig.savefig("ch08v_lie_scales.png", dpi=110)
print("area ratio when radius is proportional to value (3 vs 1):", (3 / 1) ** 2)
print("growth per year:", [round(float(g), 1) for g in np.diff(sales) / np.diff(years)])
print("saved")
@@END
@@FIG lie_scales|Left: circles for values 1, 2, 3 drawn with radius proportional to value (area grows nine times) above circles drawn with area proportional. Middle: a line with equal spacing between 2019, 2020, 2021 and 2024. Right: the same data on a true time axis|Left: radius-scaling makes 3 look 9 times bigger than 1. Middle: equal spacing hides that 2021 to 2024 is three years. Right: the honest axis.
<p><strong>How to read it.</strong> Top-left circles have radius proportional to value: the circle for 3 has 9 times the area of the circle for 1 (printed), so the eye reads "9 times". The row below has area proportional to value. In the middle panel the x-axis treats 2019, 2020, 2021 and 2024 as equally spaced, so growth of 20 per year (2019 to 2021) and 6.7 per year (2021 to 2024) both look like the same straight climb. On the true time axis (right) the slowdown is visible.</p>
<h3>Lie 4: the plot that hides the shape (Anscombe's quartet)</h3>
<p>Summary numbers can be identical for completely different data. In 1973 the statistician Francis Anscombe published four small data sets with the same means, the same spread and the same correlation. Only a picture shows how different they are.</p>
@@PY anscombe
#H
x = np.array([10, 8, 13, 9, 11, 14, 6, 4, 12, 7, 5])
x4 = np.array([8, 8, 8, 8, 8, 8, 8, 19, 8, 8, 8])
ys = [np.array([8.04, 6.95, 7.58, 8.81, 8.33, 9.96, 7.24, 4.26, 10.84, 4.82, 5.68]),
      np.array([9.14, 8.14, 8.74, 8.77, 9.26, 8.10, 6.13, 3.10, 9.13, 7.26, 4.74]),
      np.array([7.46, 6.77, 12.74, 7.11, 7.81, 8.84, 6.08, 5.39, 8.15, 6.42, 5.73]),
      np.array([6.58, 5.76, 7.71, 8.84, 8.47, 7.04, 5.25, 12.50, 5.56, 7.91, 6.89])]
xs = [x, x, x, x4]
fig, axs = plt.subplots(2, 2, figsize=(8, 5.2), sharex=True, sharey=True)
for k, (ax, xx, yy) in enumerate(zip(axs.ravel(), xs, ys), start=1):
    r = np.corrcoef(xx, yy)[0, 1]
    slope, icpt = np.polyfit(xx, yy, 1)
    ax.scatter(xx, yy, color=BLUE, s=40, zorder=3)
    ax.plot([3, 20], [icpt + slope * 3, icpt + slope * 20], color=ORANGE, lw=2)
    ax.set_title(f"Set {k}", fontsize=12)
    print(f"set {k}: mean x {xx.mean():.2f}, mean y {yy.mean():.2f}, r {r:.3f}, line y = {icpt:.2f} + {slope:.2f}x")
fig.supxlabel("x"); fig.supylabel("y")
fig.suptitle("Four data sets, one set of summary statistics", x=0.02, ha="left", fontweight="bold")
fig.tight_layout()
fig.savefig("ch08v_anscombe.png", dpi=110)
print("saved")
@@END
@@FIG anscombe|Four scatter plots with the same orange regression line: a normal cloud, a smooth curve, a straight line with one outlier, and a vertical stack of points with one extreme point|Anscombe's quartet. Identical means, correlation and fitted line; four very different stories.
<p><strong>How to read it.</strong> All four sets print mean x 9.00, mean y 7.50, r about 0.816, and the line y = 3.00 + 0.50x. Set 1 is an ordinary cloud: the line is a fair summary. Set 2 is a curve: a straight line is the wrong model. Set 3 is a perfect line with one outlier that tilts the fit. Set 4 has a single point that alone creates the whole correlation. The rule for the whole chapter: <strong>always plot the data before you trust a summary.</strong></p>
@@TABLE
Trick | How it misleads | How to spot it | Fix
Truncated bar axis | Length no longer shows value | Axis starts above 0 on a bar chart | Start at 0; or use dots/lines and say so
Cherry-picked window | A short stretch gives any trend you like | Odd start and end dates | State the window; compare same-period last year; show full range
Dual axis | The author chooses each range, so any two lines can be made to agree | Two y-axes with different scales | Separate panels or index to 100
Radius scaling | Area grows with the square | Bubbles whose ratio looks bigger than the numbers | Scale by area
Uneven axis spacing | Time or categories squeezed | Equal spacing for unequal dates | Use a real date axis
3D and perspective | Front bars look bigger | Any 3D bar or pie | Draw flat
Counts instead of rates | Bigger groups look "worse" | Maps and bars with no denominator | Per-capita or per-customer rates
Percent without base | "Up 200%" from 1 to 3 | No n shown | Show counts and n; percentage points for changes in rates
Hidden denominators / small n | A 100% rate from 3 orders | Few observations | Show n; confidence intervals; combine small groups
Smoothing or averaging | Hides spread and outliers | Only a mean line | Show distribution (box/violin) or the raw points
Inconsistent scales across panels | Each panel auto-scaled | Different y-ranges in a grid | Share axes, or say clearly that they differ
Correlation as cause | Picture implies action works | Title says "drives", "causes" | Wording; experiment (Chapter 13)
@@END
@@PROBLEM 8.12.2|Conversion rose from 2% to 3%. A slide says "Conversion up 1%". Is it right?
@@STEPS
Idea ## Two different quantities: the change in the rate, and the relative change.
Percentage points ## 3% - 2% = 1 percentage point.
Relative change ## (3 - 2) / 2 = 0.5 = 50%.
Answer ## "Up 1%" is wrong. Say "up 1 percentage point (from 2% to 3%), a 50% relative increase".
Check ## Another trap: +100% then -50% returns to the start: 100 x 2 = 200, then 200 x 0.5 = 100.
@@END
@@CALL red|COMMON MISTAKE|Showing a big percentage from a tiny base. Wrong: "Returns in Pune rose 200%" (from 1 return to 3). Right: "Returns in Pune rose from 1 to 3 (n = 38 orders)". Always give the counts, and the denominator.
@@CALL red|COMMON MISTAKE|Letting the software choose a different y-range in every panel of a grid, then comparing panels by eye. In small multiples all panels must share the axis (<code>sharey=True</code>), or the difference in scale must be stated in each panel.
<h3>A honesty checklist you can run on any chart</h3>
<ol><li>Does every bar start at zero? If not, is it a line or dot chart, and is the axis labelled?</li><li>Is the time window chosen for a reason that you can state? Is the comparison like for like?</li><li>Does area scale with value, not radius? Is the axis spacing true?</li><li>Are rates used where groups differ in size? Are n and the denominator shown?</li><li>Are all panels on one scale?</li><li>Does the title claim no more than the picture proves (association, not cause)?</li><li>Would someone who sees only the chart, for ten seconds, get the numbers right?</li></ol>
<h3>Going deeper: legal non-zero axes, and the Datasaurus</h3>
<p>Non-zero axes are not always lies. A line chart of body temperature from 36 to 40 degrees, or a stock price, is fine because position, not length, carries the value, and zero is not a meaningful origin. The honest practice is to say the axis is cut (a small note, or a break mark) and to avoid cutting it just to dramatise a change. Next level of the Anscombe lesson: the <em>Datasaurus Dozen</em> (Matejka and Fitzmaurice, 2017) has thirteen data sets that share their means, standard deviations and correlation to two decimals, yet one of them is a drawing of a dinosaur. Summary numbers are compression; plots are the original.</p>
