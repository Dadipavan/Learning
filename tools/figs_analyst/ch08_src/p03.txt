
@@H2 8.3 Chart Cookbook I: Comparing Categories, Parts and Steps
<p>From here to the end of 8.6 is a cookbook. Every entry has the same shape so you can find things fast: the question the chart answers, when <em>not</em> to use it, the numbers, the matplotlib code with its picture and a numbered explanation, the same chart in seaborn, and the same chart in plotly (not run here). Pick the chart by question using the table in 8.1, then copy the block that matches.</p>
@@CALL blue|INTUITION|Comparing categories is the job of the ruler. Lay every category's bar against the same baseline and the eye does the subtraction for you. Every chart in this section is a variation on that idea, or a variation on "what happens when the ruler needs a break": parts of a whole, before and after, step by step.
<h3>Bar chart: how big is each category?</h3>
<p><strong>Answers:</strong> "How much per city / channel / product?" Our Chapter 1 question. <strong>Do not use when:</strong> the x-axis is time with many points (use a line), or there are more than about 12 categories or long labels (use horizontal bars), or you need to compare parts of a whole across many groups (use stacked or a heatmap). Always start at zero, always sort unless the order is natural (months, age bands).</p>
@@PY bar
#H
#SALES
by_city = sales.groupby("city")["amount"].sum().sort_values(ascending=False)
print(by_city.to_string())
print("grand total:", by_city.sum())
colors = [BLUE if c == by_city.idxmax() else GREY for c in by_city.index]
fig, ax = plt.subplots()
bars = ax.bar(by_city.index, by_city.values, color=colors, width=0.6)
ax.bar_label(bars, labels=[f"{v:,}" for v in by_city.values], padding=3)
ax.set_title("Delhi sold the most: Rs 3,298 of Rs 9,340")
ax.set_ylabel("Sales (Rs)")
ax.set_ylim(0, 3800)
ax.set_yticks([])
ax.spines["left"].set_visible(False)
fig.tight_layout()
fig.savefig("ch08v_bar.png", dpi=110)
print("saved")
@@END
@@FIG bar|Three vertical bars for Pune, Mumbai and Delhi sorted from largest, Delhi highlighted in blue with value labels|Bar chart of sales by city. One highlighted bar answers the Chapter 1 question.
<p><strong>How to read it.</strong> Each bar is a city, its height is total sales in rupees, and blue marks the answer. The differences are small (3,298 versus 3,047 versus 2,995) and the zero baseline makes that honest: Delhi leads by 8% over Mumbai (3,298 / 3,047 = 1.082).</p>
<ol><li><code>sales.groupby("city")["amount"].sum()</code> collapses 8 rows into 3 city totals; <code>sort_values(ascending=False)</code> puts the largest first, which is the order the bars will be drawn.</li><li><code>colors = [BLUE if c == by_city.idxmax() else GREY ...]</code> builds one colour per bar. <code>idxmax()</code> returns the label (Delhi) of the largest value. This is "grey for context, one colour for the message".</li><li><code>ax.bar(labels, heights, color=colors, width=0.6)</code> draws the bars. <code>width</code> is the fraction of the slot each bar fills; 0.6 leaves a gap so bars read as separate.</li><li><code>ax.bar_label(bars, labels=..., padding=3)</code> writes each value above its bar (the direct label). <code>f"{v:,}"</code> adds the thousands comma.</li><li><code>ax.set_ylim(0, 3800)</code> forces the baseline to zero and leaves headroom for labels. This line is what keeps the chart honest.</li><li><code>set_yticks([])</code> and hiding the left spine remove an axis whose job the labels already do (less ink).</li></ol>

<h3>Horizontal bar: ranking many items</h3>
<p><strong>Answers:</strong> "Who is top and bottom?" with long names. <strong>Do not use when:</strong> the categories have a natural left-to-right order such as months. Sort largest at the top, because people read from the top.</p>
@@PY hbar
#H
revenue = pd.Series({"Wireless earbuds": 412, "Smart watch": 388, "Air fryer": 301, "Yoga mat": 187,
                     "Backpack": 176, "Desk lamp": 152, "Water bottle": 129, "Phone case": 96,
                     "Notebook": 64, "Pen set": 41}, name="revenue_k").sort_values()
top3 = revenue.nlargest(3).sum() / revenue.sum()

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(8, 4.6))
colors = [BLUE if v >= revenue.nlargest(3).min() else GREY for v in revenue]
bars = ax.barh(revenue.index, revenue.values, color=colors, height=0.7)
ax.bar_label(bars, padding=4)
ax.set_title(f"Three products bring in {top3:.0%} of revenue")
ax.set_xlabel("Revenue (Rs thousand)")
ax.set_xlim(0, 470)
ax.tick_params(axis="y", length=0)
fig.tight_layout()
fig.savefig("ch08v_hbar.png", dpi=110)
print("saved")
@@END
@@FIG hbar|Ten horizontal bars sorted with the top three products in blue and the rest grey, with value labels|Horizontal ranking. Long product names read left to right without rotation.
<p><strong>How to read it.</strong> The three blue bars are the top three products; their total 412 + 388 + 301 = 1,101 is 56.6% of the 1,946 total, which is why the title says 57% (56.6% rounded to a whole number by the <code>:.0%</code> format).</p>
<ol><li><code>pd.Series({...}).sort_values()</code> sorts ascending. <code>barh</code> draws the first item at the bottom, so ascending order puts the largest on top without any reversing trick.</li><li><code>revenue.nlargest(3)</code> returns the three biggest; <code>.min()</code> of those is the cut-off (301) used to colour the bars.</li><li><code>ax.barh(names, values, height=0.7)</code> is the horizontal twin of <code>bar</code>; the <code>height</code> is the bar thickness.</li><li><code>ax.bar_label(bars, padding=4)</code> prints each value at the bar end. With labels, the x-axis ticks are optional.</li><li><code>f"...{top3:.0%}..."</code> computes the headline from the data, so the title can never go out of date.</li><li><code>tick_params(axis="y", length=0)</code> removes the tiny tick marks beside long names.</li></ol>

<h3>Grouped and stacked bars: two categories and a number</h3>
<p><strong>Answers:</strong> grouped: "How does each sub-group compare <em>inside</em> every group?" Stacked: "How big is each group in total, and what is it made of?" <strong>Do not use when:</strong> more than 4 or 5 sub-groups (use a heatmap or small multiples). In stacked bars only the bottom segment and the total share a baseline, so segments in the middle are hard to compare.</p>
@@PY grouped
#H
#ORD
pivot = orders.pivot_table(index="city", columns="category", values="amount", aggfunc="sum") / 1000
pivot = pivot.loc[["Delhi", "Mumbai", "Pune"], ["Electronics", "Home", "Fashion", "Grocery"]]
print(pivot.round(1).to_string())
colors = [BLUE, TEAL, ORANGE, PURPLE]
fig, (ax1, ax2) = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(9, 4.2), sharey=True)
width = 0.2
x = np.arange(len(pivot))
for i, cat in enumerate(pivot.columns):
    ax1.bar(x + (i - 1.5) * width, pivot[cat], width, color=colors[i], label=cat)
ax1.set_xticks(x, pivot.index); ax1.set_title("Grouped"); ax1.set_ylabel("Sales (Rs thousand)")
bottom = np.zeros(len(pivot))
for i, cat in enumerate(pivot.columns):
    ax2.bar(x, pivot[cat], 0.6, bottom=bottom, color=colors[i], label=cat)
    bottom += pivot[cat].to_numpy()
ax2.set_xticks(x, pivot.index); ax2.set_title("Stacked")
for xi, tot in zip(x, bottom): ax2.text(xi, tot + 2, f"{tot:.0f}", ha="center", fontweight="bold")
ax2.legend(frameon=False, loc="upper right")
fig.tight_layout()
fig.savefig("ch08v_grouped.png", dpi=110)
print("saved")
@@END
@@FIG grouped|Two panels: grouped bars of sales by city and category, and stacked bars of the same data with totals above|Grouped (left) and stacked (right) bars of the same city by category table.
<p><strong>How to read it.</strong> Left: within each city, compare the four colours; Electronics (blue) is the tallest in Delhi (about 49) and Mumbai (about 51). Right: the total height is the city total (Delhi 118, Mumbai 108, Pune 50 in Rs thousand), and the stack shows what it is made of. The left panel is better for comparing categories; the right is better for comparing totals. The data is synthetic (seed 8), so do not read it as real business facts.</p>
<ol><li><code>pivot_table(index="city", columns="category", values="amount", aggfunc="sum")</code> turns the 300-row table into a 3 by 4 grid (rows = cities, columns = categories). This grid is the shape bar-chart code wants. Dividing by 1000 converts to Rs thousand.</li><li><code>.loc[[rows], [cols]]</code> fixes the order of cities and categories so the colours are always assigned the same way.</li><li>Grouped: bar <code>i</code> of 4 is shifted by <code>(i - 1.5) * width</code> from the city's centre <code>x</code>, so the four bars sit side by side centred on the tick (offsets -0.3, -0.1, +0.1, +0.3).</li><li>Stacked: each category is drawn with <code>bottom=bottom</code>, the running total of earlier categories, and then <code>bottom += ...</code> grows it. The final <code>bottom</code> is each city's total, reused for the labels.</li><li><code>sharey=True</code> gives both panels the same y-scale so the heights are comparable across panels.</li></ol>

<h3>Pie and donut: when they are acceptable</h3>
<p><strong>Answers:</strong> "Roughly what fraction is each part?" <strong>Acceptable only when:</strong> there are 2 or 3 parts, the parts add to 100%, you label the slices with their percentages, and the exact comparison does not matter. <strong>Do not use when:</strong> there are more than 4 slices, slices are close in size, or you compare several pies.</p>
@@PY donut
#H
channel = pd.Series({"App": 180.658, "Web": 95.579})
share = channel / channel.sum()
print((share * 100).round(1).to_string())
revenue = pd.Series({"Wireless earbuds": 412, "Smart watch": 388, "Air fryer": 301, "Yoga mat": 187,
                     "Backpack": 176, "Desk lamp": 152, "Water bottle": 129}).sort_values(ascending=False)
fig, (ax1, ax2) = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(9, 4.2))
ax1.pie(channel, colors=[BLUE, GREY], startangle=90, counterclock=False, wedgeprops={"width": 0.38, "edgecolor": "white"})
ax1.text(0, 0, f"{share['App']:.0%}\nvia app", ha="center", va="center", fontsize=15, fontweight="bold")
ax1.set_title("Acceptable: 2 parts")
ax1.text(0, -1.3, f"Web: {share['Web']:.0%}", ha="center", color="#555")
ax2.pie(revenue, labels=revenue.index, startangle=90, counterclock=False, textprops={"fontsize": 9},
        colors=[BLUE, ORANGE, TEAL, RED, PURPLE, GREY, "#d9a400"], wedgeprops={"edgecolor": "white"})
ax2.set_title("Avoid: 7 close slices")
fig.tight_layout()
fig.savefig("ch08v_donut.png", dpi=110)
print("saved")
@@END

<h3>Dumbbell chart: before versus after, per item</h3>
<p><strong>Answers:</strong> "How did each item change between two points?" It shows both values and the gap. <strong>Do not use when:</strong> you have more than two points in time (use lines) or the gap is tiny compared with the axis.</p>
@@PY dumbbell
#H
#ORD
orders["quarter"] = np.where(orders["date"] < "2025-04-01", "Q1", "Q2")
aov = orders.pivot_table(index="category", columns="quarter", values="amount", aggfunc="mean").round(0)
aov = aov.sort_values("Q1")
aov["change_pct"] = ((aov["Q2"] / aov["Q1"] - 1) * 100).round(1)
print(aov.to_string())
fig, ax = plt.subplots()
y = np.arange(len(aov))
ax.hlines(y, aov["Q2"], aov["Q1"], color="#bbbbbb", lw=3, zorder=1)
ax.scatter(aov["Q1"], y, s=110, color=GREY, label="Q1 (Jan to Mar)", zorder=2)
ax.scatter(aov["Q2"], y, s=110, color=BLUE, label="Q2 (Apr to Jun)", zorder=2)
for yi, (q1, q2, pct) in enumerate(zip(aov["Q1"], aov["Q2"], aov["change_pct"])):
    ax.text(max(q1, q2) + 60, yi, f"{pct:+.0f}%", va="center", color=RED if pct < -10 else "#333")
ax.set_yticks(y, aov.index)
ax.set_xlim(0, 2400)
ax.set_xlabel("Average order value (Rs)")
ax.set_title("Fashion order value fell 21% in Q2")
ax.legend(frameon=False, loc="lower right")
fig.tight_layout()
fig.savefig("ch08v_dumbbell.png", dpi=110)
print("saved")
@@END
@@FIG dumbbell|Dumbbell chart of average order value for four categories, grey dot for Q1 and blue dot for Q2 joined by a line, with percentage change labels|Dumbbell chart: the length of each connecting line is the change.
<p><strong>How to read it.</strong> Each row is a category. The grey dot is Q1, the blue dot is Q2, and the grey bar is the change. Fashion has the longest bar (797 to 632, which is -20.7%, shown as -21%), so it is the story. The axis starts at 0 here because the values are in the thousands; for a dumbbell it is allowed to start higher, because position (not length) carries the value.</p>
<ol><li><code>orders["date"] &lt; "2025-04-01"</code> compares dates to a string that pandas converts to a date; <code>np.where(cond, "Q1", "Q2")</code> makes the label column.</li><li><code>pivot_table(..., aggfunc="mean")</code> averages the order amounts per category and quarter.</li><li><code>ax.hlines(y, x_start, x_end)</code> draws one horizontal line per category; <code>zorder=1</code> keeps it behind the dots (<code>zorder=2</code>).</li><li><code>ax.scatter(x, y, s=110)</code> draws the dots: <code>s</code> is the marker area. Two scatter calls, two colours, two legend entries.</li><li>The loop writes the percentage change right of the larger value; red marks a drop bigger than 10%.</li></ol>

<h3>Slope chart: ranking and change between two moments</h3>
<p><strong>Answers:</strong> "Who rose, who fell, and did the order change?" with few items. <strong>Do not use when:</strong> more than about 8 lines (they tangle), or you have more than two moments (use a line chart).</p>
@@PY slope
#H
#ORD
orders["quarter"] = np.where(orders["date"] < "2025-04-01", "Q1", "Q2")
city = (orders.pivot_table(index="city", columns="quarter", values="amount", aggfunc="sum") / 1000).round(1)
city["change_pct"] = ((city["Q2"] / city["Q1"] - 1) * 100).round(1)
print(city.to_string())
colors = {"Delhi": BLUE, "Mumbai": ORANGE, "Pune": GREY}
fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(6.5, 4.4))
for name, row in city.iterrows():
    ax.plot([0, 1], [row["Q1"], row["Q2"]], color=colors[name], marker="o", lw=3, ms=8)
    tc = "#666666" if name == "Pune" else colors[name]          # darker grey text for contrast

<h3>Waterfall: how did we get from the start to the end?</h3>
<p><strong>Answers:</strong> "Which parts added and which subtracted between two totals?" (revenue bridge, profit bridge, headcount change). <strong>Do not use when:</strong> the pieces do not add up to the end value, or there are more than about 10 steps.</p>
@@PY waterfall
#H
#ORD
orders["quarter"] = np.where(orders["date"] < "2025-04-01", "Q1", "Q2")
cat = orders.pivot_table(index="category", columns="quarter", values="amount", aggfunc="sum") / 1000
delta = (cat["Q2"] - cat["Q1"]).round(1)
start, end = cat["Q1"].sum().round(1), cat["Q2"].sum().round(1)
print(f"Q1 total {start} + changes {delta.sum().round(1)} = Q2 total {end}")
print(delta.to_string())
labels = ["Q1 total"] + list(delta.index) + ["Q2 total"]
values = [start] + delta.tolist() + [end]
fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(8, 4.4))
running = start
ax.bar(0, start, color=GREY)
for i, d in enumerate(delta.tolist(), start=1):
    ax.bar(i, d, bottom=running, color=TEAL if d >= 0 else RED)
    ax.text(i, max(running, running + d) + 3, f"{d:+.1f}", ha="center", fontweight="bold")
    ax.plot([i - 0.7, i + 0.4], [running, running], color="#777", lw=0.8)
    running += d
ax.bar(len(delta) + 1, end, color=BLUE)
ax.text(0, start + 3, f"{start:.1f}", ha="center", fontweight="bold")
ax.text(len(delta) + 1, end + 3, f"{end:.1f}", ha="center", fontweight="bold")
ax.set_xticks(range(len(labels)), labels)
ax.set_ylim(100, 160)
ax.set_ylabel("Sales (Rs thousand)")
ax.set_title("Fashion and Grocery explain the Q2 drop")
fig.tight_layout()
fig.savefig("ch08v_waterfall.png", dpi=110)
print("saved")
@@END
@@FIG waterfall|Waterfall chart from a grey Q1 total through red and teal category steps to a blue Q2 total|Waterfall bridge from Q1 sales (146.2) to Q2 sales (130.1), Rs thousand.
<p><strong>How to read it.</strong> Start with the grey total on the left, follow each floating step (teal = rise, red = fall) and land on the blue total. Fashion (-13.1) and Grocery (-5.1) outweigh the small gains in Electronics (+1.5) and Home (+0.6). Check: 146.2 - 13.1 - 5.1 + 1.5 + 0.6 = 130.1 (the printed line confirms the bridge adds up).</p>
@@CALL red|COMMON MISTAKE|A waterfall whose steps do not add up to the end bar, usually because a category was left out or rounding was done step by step. Always print and check <code>start + sum(changes) == end</code> before drawing. Also note this waterfall's y-axis starts at 100: floating bars encode the step as position of its top and bottom, so a cut axis is legal, but state it.
<ol><li><code>delta = Q2 - Q1</code> per category; <code>delta.sum()</code> must equal the difference of the totals. That assert-style print is the safety net.</li><li>The loop draws each floating bar with <code>ax.bar(i, d, bottom=running)</code>: the bar starts at the running total and extends by <code>d</code> (negative bars go down). <code>running += d</code> moves the baseline for the next step.</li><li>The thin grey connector line from the previous total keeps the eye following the chain.</li><li>Colours encode direction only (teal up, red down), so the two totals use neutral colours (grey and blue).</li><li><code>ax.set_ylim(100, 160)</code> trims the empty bottom so the steps are visible; this is the declared cut axis.</li></ol>

<h3>Funnel: where do people drop off?</h3>
<p><strong>Answers:</strong> "At which step do we lose most people?" <strong>Do not use when:</strong> the steps are not strictly ordered or a person can skip steps. Label both the count and the step-to-step conversion; the biggest drop is the story, not the widest bar.</p>
@@PY funnel
#H
steps = pd.DataFrame({"step": ["Visited site", "Added to cart", "Started checkout", "Paid"],
                      "users": [10000, 3800, 1900, 1330]})
steps["step_conv"] = (steps["users"] / steps["users"].shift(1) * 100).round(1)
steps["overall"] = (steps["users"] / steps["users"].iloc[0] * 100).round(1)
print(steps.to_string(index=False))
fig, ax = plt.subplots()
y = np.arange(len(steps))[::-1]
ax.barh(y, steps["users"], color=[GREY, GREY, GREY, BLUE], height=0.7)
ax.barh(y, -steps["users"], color=[GREY, GREY, GREY, BLUE], height=0.7)       # mirror for the funnel look
for yi, u, c in zip(y, steps["users"], steps["step_conv"]):
    ax.text(0, yi, f"{u:,}", ha="center", va="center", color="white", fontweight="bold")
    if not np.isnan(c):
        ax.text(u + 250, yi, f"{c:.0f}% of previous step", va="center", color="#333")
ax.set_yticks(y, steps["step"])
ax.set_xlim(-5200, 8800); ax.set_xticks([])
ax.spines[["left", "bottom"]].set_visible(False); ax.tick_params(length=0)
ax.set_title("Cart to checkout loses half of shoppers (50%)")
fig.tight_layout()
fig.savefig("ch08v_funnel.png", dpi=110)
print("saved")
@@END
@@FIG funnel|Funnel of four mirrored bars from 10,000 visitors down to 1,330 paying with conversion percentages|Funnel of one month of web traffic. Each bar shows users and the step conversion.
<p><strong>How to read it.</strong> Bars shrink downward. Only 38% of visitors add to cart (3,800 / 10,000), 50% of carts start checkout (1,900 / 3,800), and 70% of checkouts pay (1,330 / 1,900). Overall 13.3% convert. The step with the lowest rate that is also fixable is the one to investigate; here cart to checkout is where half are lost.</p>
<ol><li><code>steps["users"].shift(1)</code> moves the column down one row, so each step is divided by the one above it: step conversion. The first row has no predecessor, giving NaN (not a number).</li><li>Two <code>barh</code> calls, one with positive and one with negative widths, make the symmetric funnel shape; the widths are the same, so no information is invented.</li><li>The count goes at x = 0 (the middle) in white. The step conversion goes just right of each bar (<code>u + 250</code>) in dark text, so narrow bars never clip it. <code>np.isnan(c)</code> skips the first row, which has no previous step.</li><li><code>y = np.arange(4)[::-1]</code> numbers rows from the top: the first step gets the highest y.</li></ol>

<h3>Pareto chart: which few causes matter most?</h3>
<p><strong>Answers:</strong> "Which few reasons account for most of the problem?" (the 80/20 idea). <strong>Do not use when:</strong> there is no natural count or cost per cause, or the causes overlap.</p>
@@PY pareto
#H
reasons = pd.Series({"Size or fit": 124, "Damaged in transit": 86, "Late delivery": 51,
                     "Wrong item": 33, "Changed mind": 21, "Other": 15}).sort_values(ascending=False)
cum = reasons.cumsum() / reasons.sum() * 100
print(pd.DataFrame({"returns": reasons, "cum_pct": cum.round(1)}).to_string())
fig, ax = plt.subplots()
ax.bar(reasons.index, reasons.values, color=[BLUE, BLUE] + [GREY] * 4)
ax.set_ylabel("Returns (count)")
ax.set_xticks(range(len(reasons)), reasons.index, rotation=20, ha="right")
ax2 = ax.twinx()
ax2.plot(range(len(reasons)), cum.values, color=ORANGE, marker="o", lw=2.5)
ax2.set_ylim(0, 105); ax2.set_ylabel("Cumulative share of returns (%)")
ax2.spines["right"].set_visible(True)
ax2.axhline(80, color=ORANGE, lw=1, ls="--")
ax2.text(5, 82, "80%", color=ORANGE, ha="right")
ax.set_title("Two reasons cause 64% of returns")
fig.tight_layout()
fig.savefig("ch08v_pareto.png", dpi=110)
print("saved")
@@END
@@FIG pareto|Pareto chart with six sorted bars of return reasons and an orange cumulative percentage line reaching 100 percent|Pareto chart of 330 returns. The two blue bars and the cumulative line show where to act first.
<p><strong>How to read it.</strong> Bars (left axis) are counts sorted largest first. The orange line (right axis) is the running share. After "Size or fit" (124) and "Damaged in transit" (86), the line is at 63.6%: two causes make 64% of the 330 returns. The dashed line marks 80%, reached after the third cause.
