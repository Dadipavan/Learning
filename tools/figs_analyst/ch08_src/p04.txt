@@CALL blue|INTUITION|Time has a direction, so a chart of time should have a direction too: left to right, earlier to later. The eye follows a line like a finger tracing a road. That is why a line chart wins whenever the x-axis is time, and why the only question left is how many roads you can draw before the picture becomes a plate of spaghetti.
<h3>Line chart: how does one number change over time?</h3>
<p><strong>Answers:</strong> "What is the trend, the seasonality, the spike?" <strong>Do not use when:</strong> the x values are unordered categories (use bars), or the time points are so few (3 or fewer) that a slope chart or bars are clearer. A line's baseline need not be zero, because position, not length, carries the value; but if you cut the axis, make the cut obvious.</p>
@@PY line

<h3>Sparkline: a trend inside a table</h3>
<p><strong>Answers:</strong> "Is each row going up, down or flat?" A sparkline is a tiny line chart without axes, drawn at word size. <strong>Do not use when:</strong> exact values matter or the scale differs a lot between rows (add the number next to it).</p>
@@PY sparkline
#H
#ORD
orders["month"] = orders["date"].dt.to_period("M").dt.to_timestamp()
cat_m = (orders.pivot_table(index="month", columns="category", values="amount", aggfunc="sum") / 1000).round(1)
rows = cat_m.columns.tolist()
fig, axs = plt.subplots(len(rows), 1, figsize=(8, 3.6))
for ax, name in zip(axs, rows):
    s = cat_m[name]
    ax.plot(range(len(s)), s.values, color=GREY, lw=2)
    ax.scatter([s.values.argmax(), len(s) - 1], [s.max(), s.iloc[-1]], color=[BLUE, RED], zorder=3, s=30)
    ax.axis("off")
    change = (s.iloc[-1] / s.iloc[0] - 1) * 100
    ax.text(-0.9, s.mean(), name, ha="right", va="center", fontweight="bold")
    ax.text(len(s) - 0.4, s.mean(), f"Jun {s.iloc[-1]:.1f}k   ({change:+.0f}% vs Jan)", va="center")
    ax.set_xlim(-0.2, len(s) + 3.5)
    ax.margins(y=0.3)
fig.suptitle("Monthly sales by category, Jan to Jun (blue = best month, red = latest)", x=0.02, ha="left", fontweight="bold", fontsize=13)
fig.subplots_adjust(left=0.2, right=0.98, top=0.86, bottom=0.02, hspace=0.35)
fig.savefig("ch08v_sparkline.png", dpi=110)
print(cat_m.iloc[[0, -1]].to_string())
print("saved")
@@END

<h3>Dual-axis chart: why to avoid it</h3>
<p><strong>Answers:</strong> nothing you cannot answer better. Two y-axes let the author choose each axis range freely, so the author, not the data, decides whether two lines look linked. <strong>Alternatives:</strong> two stacked panels sharing the x-axis, or both series indexed to 100 at the start.</p>
@@PY dual_axis
#H
sales = np.array([350, 330, 375, 405, 430, 455])
returns = np.array([52, 55, 49, 58, 54, 57])
months = ["Jan", "Feb", "Mar", "Apr", "May", "Jun"]
print(f"sales change Jan to Jun: {sales[-1] / sales[0] - 1:+.1%}; returns change: {returns[-1] / returns[0] - 1:+.1%}")

def dual(ax, lim, title):
    ax.plot(months, sales, color=BLUE, lw=2.5, marker="o")
    ax.set_ylim(0, 500); ax.set_ylabel("Sales (Rs thousand)", color=BLUE)
    ax2 = ax.twinx()
    ax2.plot(months, returns, color=RED, lw=2.5, marker="s")
    ax2.set_ylim(*lim); ax2.set_ylabel("Returns (count)", color=RED)
    ax2.spines["right"].set_visible(True)
    ax.set_title(title, fontsize=12)

fig = plt.figure(figsize=(11, 4.4), layout="constrained")
gs = fig.add_gridspec(2, 3, width_ratios=[1.3, 1.3, 1])
dual(fig.add_subplot(gs[:, 0]), (0, 100), "A: returns look flat")
dual(fig.add_subplot(gs[:, 1]), (45, 60), "B: returns 'chase' sales")
top = fig.add_subplot(gs[0, 2]); bot = fig.add_subplot(gs[1, 2], sharex=top)
top.plot(months, sales, color=BLUE, lw=2.5, marker="o"); top.set_ylim(0, 500); top.set_title("C: separate panels", fontsize=12)
top.set_ylabel("Sales (k)"); top.tick_params(labelbottom=False)
bot.plot(months, returns, color=RED, lw=2.5, marker="s"); bot.set_ylim(0, 100); bot.set_ylabel("Returns")
fig.savefig("ch08v_dual_axis.png", dpi=110)
print("saved")
@@END
@@FIG dual_axis|Three panels showing the same sales and returns data: two dual-axis versions with different right axis ranges giving different stories, and a two-panel stacked version|Panels A and B draw identical numbers with different right-hand axis limits. Panel C is the honest alternative.
<p><strong>How to read it.</strong> Sales and returns are the same in A and B. In A the right axis runs 0 to 100, so returns look flat, which is right: they moved from 52 to 57 (+9.6%) while sales rose 30.0%. In B the right axis runs 45 to 60, so returns seem to zoom upward in step with sales, a story invented by the axis range. Panel C puts each series on its own panel with its own honest scale and lets the reader judge.</p>
<ol><li>The helper function <code>dual(ax, lim, title)</code> draws both lines; only <code>lim</code> (the right-hand y-limits) changes between A and B. That one argument changes the story, which is the whole point.</li><li><code>ax.twinx()</code> adds the second y-axis; colour the axis labels like their lines so the reader knows which is which.</li><li><code>layout="constrained"</code> asks matplotlib to fit all labels, including the second y-axis labels, without overlap. <code>fig.add_gridspec(2, 3, width_ratios=[...])</code> makes a 2 by 3 grid; <code>gs[:, 0]</code> means "all rows, column 0", so A and B each take a full column while C uses two rows of column 2 (GridSpec is covered in 8.7).</li><li><code>sharex=top</code> links the x-axes; <code>tick_params(labelbottom=False)</code> hides the duplicate month labels on the top panel.</li></ol>
@@CALL red|COMMON MISTAKE|Using a secondary axis to put "two things on one chart" for convenience. Wrong: sales on the left, returns on the right with ranges that make the lines touch. Right: stacked panels, or index both to 100 at the first month (<code>series / series.iloc[0] * 100</code>: in June sales are at 130.0 and returns at 109.6), which shows relative growth on one honest axis. The Pareto chart in 8.3 is the exception because the right axis is the running percent of the same bars.

<h3>Calendar and time heat: when does activity happen?</h3>
<p><strong>Answers:</strong> "Which days and hours are busy?" It lays time out as a grid (day of week by week, or hour by day of week) and colours each cell by volume. <strong>Do not use when:</strong> you need exact values (add numbers in the cells) or the grid has fewer than about 20 cells.</p>
@@PY calendar
#H
rng = np.random.default_rng(8)
days = pd.date_range("2025-01-01", "2025-06-30")
base = np.where(days.dayofweek >= 5, 15, 9.5)                      # weekends are busier
orders_per_day = rng.poisson(base)
daily = pd.Series(orders_per_day, index=days, name="orders")
print(daily.groupby(daily.index.dayofweek).mean().round(1).rename(index=dict(enumerate(["Mon", "Tue", "Wed", "Thu", "Fri", "Sat", "Sun"]))).to_string())
first_monday = days[0] - pd.Timedelta(days=days[0].dayofweek)
week = ((days - first_monday).days // 7).to_numpy()
grid = np.full((7, week.max() + 1), np.nan)
grid[days.dayofweek, week] = daily.values
fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(9, 3.4))
im = ax.imshow(grid, cmap="Blues", aspect="equal", vmin=0)
ax.set_yticks(range(7), ["Mon", "Tue", "Wed", "Thu", "Fri", "Sat", "Sun"])
month_start = [(d - first_monday).days // 7 for d in pd.date_range("2025-01-01", "2025-06-01", freq="MS")]
ax.set_xticks(month_start, ["Jan", "Feb", "Mar", "Apr", "May", "Jun"])
ax.tick_params(length=0)
for s in ax.spines.values(): s.set_visible(False)
cb = fig.colorbar(im, ax=ax, shrink=0.8, pad=0.02); cb.set_label("Orders per day")
ax.set_title("Saturdays and Sundays are the busiest days")
fig.tight_layout()
fig.savefig("ch08v_calendar.png", dpi=110)
print("saved")
@@END
@@FIG calendar|Calendar heat grid with rows for weekdays and columns for weeks from January to June, darker blue meaning more orders|Calendar heat map of daily orders (synthetic). Rows are weekdays, columns are weeks.
<p><strong>How to read it.</strong> Each small square is one day. Darker blue means more orders. The two bottom rows (Saturday and Sunday) are darker than the rest. The printed weekday averages confirm the picture: about 9 to 10 orders on weekdays and 13 to 14.5 on Saturday and Sunday.</p>
<ol><li><code>rng.poisson(lam)</code> draws counts of events with a given mean; it models "orders per day" well. The seeded generator makes the data identical on every run.</li><li>We need a grid with 7 rows (weekdays) and one column per week. <code>first_monday</code> is the Monday of the week containing 1 January, so the week number is <code>(date - first_monday).days // 7</code>.</li><li><code>grid[days.dayofweek, week] = daily.values</code> fills the cells by pairing each day's weekday row with its week column (fancy indexing); cells outside the date range stay NaN and are left blank.</li><li><code>ax.imshow(grid, cmap="Blues", vmin=0)</code> colours the grid; <code>vmin=0</code> keeps a day with zero orders at the lightest shade so the scale is honest.</li><li>The colour bar (<code>fig.colorbar</code>) is the legend for colour; always include one when colour carries numbers.</li></ol>
@@PY timeheat_sns
#HS
rng = np.random.default_rng(8)
n = 2500
hours = np.arange(8, 24)
w = np.exp(-0.5 * ((hours - 20) / 3.0) ** 2)                             # evening peak around 8 pm
hour = rng.choice(hours, n, p=w / w.sum())
weekday = rng.choice(7, n, p=[0.12, 0.12, 0.12, 0.13, 0.14, 0.19, 0.18])
tbl = pd.crosstab(weekday, hour).reindex(index=range(7), columns=range(8, 24), fill_value=0)
tbl.index = ["Mon", "Tue", "Wed", "Thu", "Fri", "Sat", "Sun"]
print("busiest cell:", tbl.stack().idxmax(), "with", tbl.stack().max(), "orders")
ax = sns.heatmap(tbl, cmap="Blues", cbar_kws={"label": "Orders"}, linewidths=0.5, linecolor="white")
ax.set(xlabel="Hour of day (24-hour clock)", ylabel="", title="Orders peak around 8 pm, strongest on Saturday")
ax.tick_params(length=0)
ax.figure.tight_layout()
ax.figure.savefig("ch08v_timeheat_sns.png", dpi=110)
print("saved")
@@END
@@FIG timeheat_sns|seaborn heat map of order counts for each weekday and hour of day with a strong evening band|Hour by weekday heat map of 2,500 synthetic orders. The dark band is the evening peak.
<p><code>pd.crosstab(weekday, hour)</code> counts how many rows fall in each weekday and hour pair, which is exactly the grid <code>sns.heatmap</code> draws. <code>reindex(columns=range(8, 24), fill_value=0)</code> forces one column for every hour from 8 to 23 (8 am to 11 pm) and fills any hour with no orders with 0, so no column is skipped. The hours are drawn with <code>rng.choice</code> using bell-shaped weights centred on 20. <code>linewidths</code> and <code>linecolor</code> draw the thin white gaps between cells. This hour-by-weekday heat map is the standard answer to "when should we send the notification" or "when should we staff the call centre".</p>
