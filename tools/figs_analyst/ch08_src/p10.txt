<h3>Three kinds of palette, and the grey-plus-accent rule</h3>
<p>The type of data decides the type of palette. Mixing them up is the most common colour error.</p>
@@TABLE
Data | Palette type | How many colours | Examples (matplotlib name)
Categories with no order (city, channel) | Qualitative: clearly different hues | 3 to 5 at most | the house palette; <code>"tab10"</code>, <code>"Set2"</code>
One number from low to high | Sequential: one hue, light to dark | 5 to 9 steps, or continuous | <code>"Blues"</code>, <code>"viridis"</code>
A number with a meaningful middle (growth, difference from target, correlation) | Diverging: two hues meeting at a neutral | Continuous, centred on the middle value | <code>"RdBu_r"</code>, <code>"coolwarm"</code>
Everything except the one thing that matters | Grey plus one accent | 2 | context grey <code>#9a9a9a</code>, accent blue <code>#2b4fd6</code>
@@END

<h3>Colour-blind safety, with numbers</h3>
<p>About 1 in 12 men and 1 in 200 women have a colour-vision deficiency, most often red-green (deuteranopia and protanopia). The fix is partly colour choice and partly <em>redundant coding</em>: also vary marker shape, line style or add direct labels. The block below simulates two common types with published matrices (Machado, Oliveira and Fernandes, 2009; an approximation, not a medical test) and measures how different the colours still look using &Delta;E, the distance between two colours in the CIE Lab space (about 2 is barely visible, 10 or more is clearly different).</p>
@@PY cvd
#H
from matplotlib.colors import to_rgb

def lin(c):
    c = np.asarray(c, float)
    return np.where(c <= 0.04045, c / 12.92, ((c + 0.055) / 1.055) ** 2.4)
def delin(c):
    c = np.clip(c, 0, 1)
    return np.where(c <= 0.0031308, c * 12.92, 1.055 * c ** (1 / 2.4) - 0.055)
def to_lab(rgb):
    M = np.array([[0.4124, 0.3576, 0.1805], [0.2126, 0.7152, 0.0722], [0.0193, 0.1192, 0.9505]])
    xyz = (M @ lin(rgb)) / np.array([0.95047, 1.0, 1.08883])
    f = np.where(xyz > 216 / 24389, np.cbrt(xyz), (24389 / 27 * xyz + 16) / 116)
    return np.array([116 * f[1] - 16, 500 * (f[0] - f[1]), 200 * (f[1] - f[2])])
DEUT = np.array([[0.367322, 0.860646, -0.227968], [0.280085, 0.672501, 0.047413], [-0.011820, 0.042940, 0.968881]])
PROT = np.array([[0.152286, 1.052583, -0.204868], [0.114503, 0.786281, 0.099216], [-0.003882, -0.048116, 1.051998]])
sim = lambda h, M: delin(M @ lin(to_rgb(h)))
grey = lambda h: np.repeat(delin(lin(to_rgb(h)) @ np.array([0.2126, 0.7152, 0.0722])), 3)
def pair_de(a, b):
    return np.linalg.norm(to_lab(a) - to_lab(b))

house = [BLUE, ORANGE, TEAL, RED, PURPLE]
classic = ["#d62728", "#2ca02c", "#1f77b4", "#ff7f0e", "#9467bd"]
conds = [("normal", lambda h: np.array(to_rgb(h))), ("deuteranopia", lambda h: sim(h, DEUT)),
         ("protanopia", lambda h: sim(h, PROT)), ("greyscale", grey)]
fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(8, 5))
y = 0
for pname, pal in [("House palette", house), ("Red/green palette", classic)]:

ax.set(xlim=(-4.6, 5), ylim=(-y + 0.3, 1)); ax.axis("off")
fig.tight_layout()
fig.savefig("ch08v_cvd.png", dpi=110)

<p><strong>How to read it.</strong> The printed numbers are the smallest pairwise &Delta;E among the colours under each condition. Honest result: <em>no five-colour palette survives colour blindness unaided</em>. Under normal vision the house palette's closest pair is &Delta;E 14.4 (clearly different), but under simulated deuteranopia its blue and purple collapse to 3.3 (nearly the same), and under protanopia the closest pair is 6.9. The classic palette fares no better where it counts: red and green, 120 apart for normal vision, fall to 7.3 under deuteranopia. The house palette's first three colours (blue, orange, teal) keep a large gap (smallest &Delta;E 36.5 or more in every condition), which is exactly why this chapter keeps most charts to three colours or fewer. Look also at the greyscale rows: all five house colours turn into similar mid-greys, so in a black-and-white print only labels and markers can tell series apart. The rules that follow: never put blue and purple side by side without another cue, label lines directly, and add a second cue (marker, dash, label) whenever two series must be told apart.</p>
<ol><li><code>lin</code> and <code>delin</code> convert between the stored sRGB values and linear light. The simulation matrices act on linear light, not on the stored numbers, a common mistake in hand-made simulations.</li><li><code>sim(h, DEUT)</code> multiplies a colour by the 3x3 matrix for deuteranopia (no working green receptors); <code>PROT</code> does the same for protanopia (no red).</li><li><code>to_lab</code> converts to CIE Lab, a colour space designed so that equal numeric distances look roughly equal to the eye. <code>pair_de</code> is the straight-line distance, called &Delta;E (CIE76).</li><li><code>grey</code> keeps only luminance (Y), i.e. what a black-and-white printer would do: the swatches that become the same grey cannot be told apart in print.</li><li>The last print line asks which of six chosen pairs of house colours are closest under deuteranopia, so you know which pair never to put next to each other without a second cue. (Blue and purple are the worst pair; orange and red are next but still clearly different.)</li></ol>
<h3>Contrast and legibility</h3>
<p>For text and thin lines the key number is <strong>contrast ratio</strong>: (L<sub>1</sub> + 0.05) / (L<sub>2</sub> + 0.05), where L is the relative luminance (0 black, 1 white) of the lighter and darker colour. The accessibility guideline WCAG asks for at least 4.5:1 for normal text and 3:1 for large text and graphics.</p>
@@PY contrast
import numpy as np
from matplotlib.colors import to_rgb

def luminance(hex_colour):
    c = np.array(to_rgb(hex_colour))
    c = np.where(c <= 0.04045, c / 12.92, ((c + 0.055) / 1.055) ** 2.4)
    return float(c @ np.array([0.2126, 0.7152, 0.0722]))

def contrast(a, b="#ffffff"):
    la, lb = sorted([luminance(a), luminance(b)], reverse=True)
    return (la + 0.05) / (lb + 0.05)

for name, h in [("light grey (context marks)", "#9a9a9a"), ("mid grey (text)", "#666666"), ("dark grey (text)", "#333333"),
                ("blue", "#2b4fd6"), ("orange", "#b84a22"), ("teal", "#357a70"), ("red", "#aa2d26"), ("purple", "#6a2fd0")]:
    ratio = contrast(h)
    verdict = "text ok" if ratio >= 4.5 else ("graphics ok" if ratio >= 3 else "too faint")
    print(f"{name:<27}{h}  {ratio:5.2f}:1  {verdict}")
@@END
<p>Read the table: the five accent colours all pass for text on white (5.0:1 or better). The light grey used for context marks is 2.8:1: fine for a gridline or a de-emphasised bar, <strong>not</strong> for text. That is why labels in this chapter use the darker grey <code>#666666</code> (5.7:1) when text must be grey. White text on a coloured bar needs the same check: white on the blue bar is 6.6:1, white on the light grey bar is 2.8:1 (too faint).</p>
@@CALL red|COMMON MISTAKE|Grey text on a light background, or white labels on yellow or light-grey bars. Wrong: <code>ax.text(..., color="#9a9a9a")</code>. Right: check the contrast ratio (4.5:1 or better), or use <code>#333333</code> for text and keep the pale grey for marks.
<h3>Typography and layout rules</h3>
@@TABLE
Element | Rule | Why
Title | Left-aligned, bold, one sentence stating the finding, 14 to 16 pt | Eyes start top-left; readers should not hunt for the point
Subtitle / axis labels | Regular weight, 11 to 12 pt, units included | Context without competing with the title
Tick labels | 10 to 12 pt, horizontal wherever possible | Rotated text is slow to read; flip the chart instead
Annotations | Same font as the chart, dark grey, close to the mark | One typeface per chart; no legend trips
Numbers | Thousands separators; consistent decimals; units in the label, not every tick | Fast, unambiguous reading
Legend | Delete it if direct labels fit; otherwise place it near the data | Every look away from the data costs attention
Gridlines | Faint (<code>#dddddd</code>), only in the direction that helps, behind the data | Guides without noise
Aspect ratio | Time series wider than tall (about 2:1); scatter close to square | Slopes of lines stay readable (banking to 45 degrees)
Whitespace | Margins around the plot, space between panels, no overlapping labels | Crowding is the main cause of a "busy" chart
Alignment | Align titles, axes and source notes to the same left edge | Looks deliberate; easy to scan
@@END
<h3>The declutter checklist</h3>
<ol><li>Delete the chart border and the top and right spines.</li><li>Delete or lighten gridlines; delete tick marks that carry no information.</li><li>Delete the legend if you can label directly; delete the y-axis if bars carry value labels.</li><li>Delete default titles; write the finding.</li><li>Replace many colours by grey plus one accent.</li><li>Sort. Round numbers to the precision the decision needs.</li><li>Squint test: blur your eyes at the picture. Is the main message still the first thing you see?</li></ol>

Comparing with the <em>next</em> category rather than the smallest is the cautious choice: against Grocery the ratio would be about 5 to 6 and would overstate the point.</li></ol>

<h3>Going deeper: colour spaces and perceptual uniformity</h3>
<p>RGB (red, green, blue amounts) describes what a screen emits, not what an eye perceives: equal steps in RGB look unequal. <strong>CIE Lab</strong> (L for lightness, a for green-red, b for blue-yellow) and its modern relatives (<strong>CIECAM02, OKLab, HCL</strong>) are built so that equal distances look about equal. A good sequential colormap should rise steadily in lightness L, so that a grey-scale print is still ordered. <code>viridis</code>, <code>magma</code> and <code>cividis</code> were designed this way (<code>cividis</code> also for colour-blind readers); <code>jet</code> is not, its lightness goes up and down, which creates fake boundaries. When you design your own palette, choose hues with <em>different lightness</em> as well: that is what keeps it readable when the hue difference is lost (colour blindness, projectors, print). Tools such as ColorBrewer and Viz Palette let you test a palette.</p>
