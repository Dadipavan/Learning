
@@H2 Chapter recap
<ul><li>Pick the chart from the <strong>question</strong>, using the 60-second table: compare, rank, trend, compose, distribute, relate, change, bridge, funnel, prioritise, cohort, place.</li><li>The eye reads <strong>position and length</strong> best, then angle and slope, then area, then colour shade. Put the main comparison on position or length.</li><li>Every figure has parts with a job: title (finding), subtitle and unit, axis labels, ticks, faint grid, marks, legend or direct labels, annotation, source note.</li><li>Bars start at zero; lines may not, but say so. Sort bars. Use rates, not counts, when groups differ in size. Never pie more than 3 slices; avoid dual axes and 3D.</li><li>matplotlib: use <code>fig, ax = plt.subplots()</code> and call methods on <code>ax</code>; set a house style once with <code>rcParams</code>; use <code>layout="constrained"</code>, <code>subplot_mosaic</code> and GridSpec for layouts; save with the right dpi (pixels = inches x dpi) or as vector.</li><li>seaborn: three families (relational, distribution, categorical) plus regression and matrix; axes-level functions take <code>ax=</code>, figure-level functions (<code>relplot</code>, <code>displot</code>, <code>catplot</code>, <code>pairplot</code>) make their own figure and facets; <code>barplot</code> averages by default.</li><li>plotly: a figure is JSON of traces and layout; express for speed, graph_objects for control; hover, facets and animation are its strengths; save HTML for interactivity and PNG (kaleido) for slides; Streamlit and Dash turn charts into apps.</li><li>Colour: qualitative (max 5), sequential, diverging; grey plus one accent; check colour-blind and greyscale safety and 4.5:1 text contrast; label directly.</li><li>Tell a story: title = finding (subject, verb, number, comparison); situation, complication, resolution; constant colours; end with the next step.</li><li>Be honest: lie factor near 1; same window for like-for-like; area not radius; true time axis; plot before you summarise (Anscombe).</li><li>Build dashboards from small printed tables, one function per panel type, a headline that states the finding, and numbers computed, never typed.</li></ul>

<h3>Choose and identify (easy)</h3>
@@QA 1 (easy).|Which chart answers "what share of order value comes through the app and what share through the web"? Which would you avoid?|A single big number or a donut with two parts labelled with percentages (65% app, 35% web), or a 100% stacked bar. Avoid a pie with many slices and anything 3D. Two parts is the one case where a pie or donut is acceptable.
@@QA 2 (easy).|You must compare average order value across 12 product categories with long names. Which chart?|A horizontal bar chart, sorted from largest to smallest, because 12 long labels will not fit on a vertical axis without rotation, and sorting makes the ranking readable. Start at zero and label the values.
@@QA 3 (easy).|Which chart and which number show whether weekly revenue moves with ad spend?|A scatter plot (ad spend on x, revenue on y) with a fitted line, and the correlation r. In 8.6 the 52-week example has r = 0.85, so a strong positive linear link; it is association, not proof of cause.
@@QA 4 (easy).|Which chart shows how many customers are still active k months after joining, for each join month?|A cohort retention heatmap: rows are join months, columns are months since joining, cells are the retained share, with the future (not yet observed) cells left empty.
@@QA 5 (easy).|Rank these from most to least accurately read: pie angle, bar length, bubble area, colour shade.|Bar length, then pie angle, then bubble area, then colour shade (Cleveland and McGill; position on a common scale would come before bar length). Use the strongest encoding for the main comparison.
@@QA 6 (easy).|A bar chart of Mumbai (3,047) and Pune (2,995) uses a y-axis starting at 2,900. What is the lie factor?|Visible heights: 3,047 - 2,900 = 147 and 2,995 - 2,900 = 95; visual ratio = 147 / 95 = 1.547. Real ratio = 3,047 / 2,995 = 1.017. Lie factor = 1.547 / 1.017 = 1.52. The picture exaggerates the gap about one and a half times. Start at zero and the factor is 1.
<h3>Compute and explain (medium)</h3>
@@QA 7 (medium).|A pie has three slices with values 50, 30 and 20. Give the angles.|Total 100. Angles = 360 x value / total: 180, 108 and 72 degrees (sum 360). The 108 versus 72 comparison is the kind a pie makes hard and bars make obvious.
@@QA 8 (medium).|Churn fell from 8% to 6%. Write the correct sentence.|"Churn fell by 2 percentage points (from 8% to 6%), a 25% relative decrease": (6 - 8) / 8 = -0.25. "Fell by 2%" is ambiguous and usually wrong.
@@QA 9 (medium).|Compute by hand the box plot elements for 3, 5, 6, 8, 9, 12, 40.|Median = 8 (4th of 7). With interpolated quartiles (numpy default): Q1 sits at position 1.5, halfway between 5 and 6, so Q1 = 5.5; Q3 at position 4.5, halfway between 9 and 12, so Q3 = 10.5. IQR = 5. Fences: 5.5 - 7.5 = -2 and 10.5 + 7.5 = 18. The lower whisker ends at 3, the upper at 12, and 40 is an outlier drawn as a dot. The mean is 83 / 7 = 11.9, bigger than the median because of 40.
@@QA 10 (medium).|1,000 values have IQR 40. What bin width does Freedman-Diaconis give, and how many Sturges bins?|FD width = 2 x 40 / 1000<sup>1/3</sup> = 80 / 10 = 8. Sturges: log2(1000) + 1 = 9.97 + 1 = 10.97, so 11 bins. FD depends on the spread, Sturges only on n; for skewed data FD is usually the better start.
@@QA 11 (medium).|A figure is 6.4 x 3.6 inches. What pixel size results at 150 dpi? At what dpi would it be 1,920 pixels wide?|At 150 dpi: 6.4 x 150 = 960 by 3.6 x 150 = 540 pixels. For 1,920 pixels wide: 1,920 / 6.4 = 300 dpi.
@@QA 12 (medium).|White text sits on a bar whose relative luminance is 0.20. Compute the contrast ratio and judge it.|Ratio = (1 + 0.05) / (0.20 + 0.05) = 1.05 / 0.25 = 4.2. It misses 4.5:1 for normal text, passes the 3:1 limit for large text and graphics. Darken the bar or use dark text.
@@QA 13 (medium).|A waterfall starts at 120 and has steps +15, -22, +9. What is the end value, and what must you check before drawing?|120 + 15 - 22 + 9 = 122. Check that start + sum of steps equals the end total you report (and that every contributing category is included, including small ones, after any rounding).
@@QA 14 (medium).|A funnel has 8,000 visits, 2,400 carts, 960 checkouts, 480 paid. Give the step conversions and the overall conversion. Where do you look first?|Visit to cart 2,400 / 8,000 = 30%; cart to checkout 960 / 2,400 = 40%; checkout to paid 480 / 960 = 50%; overall 480 / 8,000 = 6%. The lowest rate is the first step (30%), which also loses the most people (5,600), so start there, then ask which step is cheapest to improve.
@@QA 15 (medium).|Return reasons have counts 50, 30, 10, 6, 4. Give the Pareto cumulative percentages and the conclusion.|Total 100. Cumulative: 50%, 80%, 90%, 96%, 100%. The top two reasons account for 80% of returns, so fix them first.
@@QA 16 (medium).|A bubble chart gives A the value 1 and B the value 4, sizing the radius in proportion to the value. By how much does B's area exceed A's, and what is the lie factor?|Area is proportional to radius squared, so the area ratio is 4 squared = 16 against a real ratio of 4. Lie factor = 16 / 4 = 4. Size by area: radius proportional to the square root of the value.
@@QA 17 (medium).|A slide says "returns rose 300%". The counts were 2 and 6. What is the correct statement and why is the slide misleading?|6 / 2 - 1 = 2.0 = +200%, not 300% (300% would be 8). More important: the base is tiny. Say "returns rose from 2 to 6 (n = 140 orders)", and show the rate (1.4% to 4.3%).
<h3>What does this print? Fix this code. (medium to hard)</h3>
@@Q 18 (medium).|What does this print?
<pre class="code lang-text"><code>import numpy as np
x = np.array([10, 20, 40, 80])
print(np.log10(x).round(2).tolist())
print(np.diff(np.log10(x)).round(2).tolist())</code></pre>
@@A <code>[1.0, 1.3, 1.6, 1.9]</code> and <code>[0.3, 0.3, 0.3]</code>. Each doubling adds log10(2) = 0.301 to the log, so doublings are equal steps on a log axis: that is why exponential growth is a straight line there.
@@QA 19 (medium).|What does <code>pd.Series([3, 1, 2], index=list("xyz")).sort_values().index.tolist()</code> print, and why does it matter for a bar chart?|<code>['y', 'z', 'x']</code>: the values sorted ascending are 1 (y), 2 (z), 3 (x), and the index travels with them. Sorting the Series (not the two lists separately) keeps labels and bars paired.
@@Q 20 (medium).|This code raises an error. Name it and fix it.
<pre class="code lang-text"><code>fig, ax = plt.subplots(1, 2)
ax.plot([1, 2, 3], [3, 1, 2])</code></pre>
@@A <code>AttributeError: 'numpy.ndarray' object has no attribute 'plot'</code>. With 2 panels, <code>ax</code> is an array of two axes. Use <code>ax[0].plot(...)</code>, or unpack: <code>fig, (ax1, ax2) = plt.subplots(1, 2)</code> and <code>ax1.plot(...)</code>. This is the first quiet trap of the subplots grid.
@@Q 21 (medium).|The saved file <code>a.png</code> is blank white. Why?
<pre class="code lang-text"><code>plt.plot([1, 2, 3])
plt.show()
plt.savefig("a.png")</code></pre>
@@A <code>plt.show()</code> displays and then closes the figure; the later <code>savefig</code> saves a new empty figure. Save first (<code>fig.savefig("a.png")</code>) and then show, and prefer the object style (<code>fig, ax = plt.subplots()</code>).
@@QA 22 (hard).|A colleague wants city totals but gets city means. Find the bug in the code and fix it, then check the totals.|<code>sns.barplot(data=orders, x="city", y="amount")</code> uses the mean with an error bar by default. The fix is <code>estimator="sum", errorbar=None</code>. The code below applies it and compares with <code>groupby</code> (the heights of the bars are read back from the plot):
@@PY practice_sum
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
import seaborn as sns
import matplotlib
matplotlib.use("Agg")
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

orders = pd.DataFrame({"city": ["Delhi", "Delhi", "Pune", "Pune", "Pune", "Mumbai"],
                       "amount": [799, 2499, 998, 1400, 597, 1299]})
fig, ax = plt.subplots()
sns.barplot(data=orders, x="city", y="amount", order=["Delhi", "Mumbai", "Pune"], estimator="sum", errorbar=None, ax=ax)
heights = [round(p.get_height()) for p in ax.patches]
print("bars (Delhi, Mumbai, Pune):", heights)
print("groupby :", orders.groupby("city")["amount"].sum().loc[["Delhi", "Mumbai", "Pune"]].tolist())
print("mean (the default would show):", orders.groupby("city")["amount"].mean().loc[["Delhi", "Mumbai", "Pune"]].round(0).tolist())
@@END
<p>The bars equal the groupby sums (3,298; 1,299; 2,995). The default would have drawn the means 1,649; 1,299; 998, which look like a different company. Always read one bar against the table.</p>
@@QA 23 (hard).|Write the seaborn code (four lines after the imports) for small multiples: one panel per city, monthly sales lines, shared y-axis, titles equal to the city names. Run it on the 18 values below split into three made-up cities.|Use <code>relplot</code> with <code>col</code>; the data must be tidy (one row per month and city).
@@PY practice_facets
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
import seaborn as sns
import matplotlib
matplotlib.use("Agg")
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

rng = np.random.default_rng(8)
tidy = pd.DataFrame({"month": np.tile(pd.date_range("2025-01-01", periods=6, freq="MS"), 3),
                     "city": np.repeat(["Delhi", "Mumbai", "Pune"], 6),
                     "sales_k": np.round(np.concatenate([np.linspace(15, 30, 6), np.linspace(23, 10, 6), np.linspace(5, 7, 6)])
                                         + rng.normal(0, 1, 18), 1)})
g = sns.relplot(data=tidy, x="month", y="sales_k", col="city", kind="line", height=2.4, aspect=1.2, color="#2b4fd6")
g.set_titles("{col_name}")
g.set_axis_labels("", "Sales (Rs thousand)")
g.figure.savefig("ch08v_practice_facets.png", dpi=110)
print(type(g).__name__, "| panels:", g.axes.shape, "| shared y range:", tuple(round(v) for v in g.axes[0, 0].get_ylim()))
@@END
<p>Line 1 builds the grid by <code>col="city"</code>; line 2 sets each panel title to the city name; line 3 labels the axes once; line 4 saves. The y range is shared across panels by default (the printed range is the same for every panel), which is what makes the panels comparable.</p>
<h3>Design and interview (hard)</h3>
@@QA 24 (hard).|Design a one-page dashboard for a call-centre manager who asks "are we answering calls fast enough, and when do we need more staff?"|Questions to charts: (1) KPI tiles: calls handled, average wait, abandon rate, service-level % (calls answered in 20 s) against target. (2) Line of daily abandon rate with a dashed target line and the worst day annotated. (3) Heatmap of calls by weekday and hour (sequential palette, numbers in cells) to show when to staff up. (4) Horizontal bars of average wait by team, sorted, worst team highlighted. (5) Histogram or box plot of handle time (skewed, report the median). Layout: tiles on top, trend and heatmap in the middle row, breakdowns at the bottom. Title: the finding (for example "Waits breach target on weekday evenings"), computed from the data. Data prep: one tidy calls table; one groupby per panel; print each before drawing.
@@QA 25 (hard).|A slide shows a 3D exploded pie of 9 sales channels in rainbow colours with percentages inside the slices. List the redesign steps.|(1) Replace with a horizontal bar chart sorted by value; (2) drop 3D and the explosion; (3) grey for all bars, one accent for the top channel (or for the one the message is about); (4) direct value labels, no legend; (5) group the smallest channels into "Other" if they are under about 3% each; (6) finding title with a computed number ("Three channels bring in 70% of sales"); (7) source and date footnote.
@@QA 26 (hard).|Forty regional managers who do not code need the same dashboard every Monday, with a different filter per region. Which tool: matplotlib script, Power BI, Streamlit?|Power BI (Chapters 9 to 11): scheduled refresh, row-level security so each manager sees only their region, subscriptions by e-mail, no hosting to maintain. Streamlit/Dash can do it but you must host, authenticate and maintain it. A matplotlib script that mails a PDF is fine for a single fixed report with no filtering. Choose by who needs to ask questions and who maintains the thing.
@@QA 27 (hard).|Interview: "Why avoid dual-axis charts, and when are they acceptable?"|Because each axis range is chosen by the author, and changing one range makes the lines cross, diverge or "track" each other, so the chart can show almost any relationship (8.4 panels A and B). Better: two stacked panels sharing the x-axis, or index both series to 100. Acceptable when the second axis is an exact re-expression of the first (Celsius and Fahrenheit, Rs and dollars at a fixed rate; <code>secondary_yaxis</code>) or in a Pareto chart where the line is the running percentage of the same bars.
@@QA 28 (hard).|Interview: "Your chart shows Delhi sales up 5%, but the finance team says down 3%. What do you check?"|(1) The window: same months, same year-on-year basis? (2) The measure: gross or net of returns, and currency or tax? (3) The filter: all channels, all statuses (Delivered only)? (4) The denominator and base: percent of which year? (5) Duplicates or missing days after a join or refresh. (6) Whether the chart rounds or uses an average of monthly percentages instead of a total ratio. Reconcile one month by hand against the source, then fix the definition and write it in the footnote.
@@QA 29 (hard).|Interview: "When do you use matplotlib, seaborn and plotly?"|matplotlib for full control and print-quality static figures (custom annotations, layouts, dashboards as images, anything seaborn does not cover). seaborn for fast statistical exploration on tidy DataFrames (distributions, groups, regression lines, facets), because it aggregates and draws confidence bands for you, and returns matplotlib objects you can still edit. plotly when readers need hover, zoom, filters or a web page (and as the chart engine of Dash/Streamlit apps). A common workflow: explore in seaborn, finish the report figure in matplotlib, publish the interactive version in plotly or Power BI.
