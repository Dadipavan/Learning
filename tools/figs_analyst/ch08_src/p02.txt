<h3>Numbered line-by-line explanation</h3>
<ol><li>The first lines build <code>monthly</code>: <code>pd.period_range</code> makes 18 month labels, <code>to_timestamp()</code> turns them into dates, and the 18 sales values become a <code>pd.Series</code> indexed by date. Slices like <code>monthly.iloc[:6]</code> take the first six rows and <code>monthly.iloc[12:18]</code> the last six.</li><li><code>fig.subplots_adjust(left, right, top, bottom)</code> sets the empty margins as fractions of the figure. We make room at the top (title and subtitle) and at the bottom (source).</li><li><code>ax.plot(x, y, color=, marker="o", lw=2.5, label=)</code> draws one line. <code>label</code> is the name the legend will use. Grey for last year, blue for this year: the old series is context, the new one is the message.</li><li><code>ax.set_xticks(x, labels)</code> puts month names at positions 0 to 5. Passing positions and labels together keeps them paired.</li><li><code>ax.set_ylim(250, 500)</code> fixes the visible y range. This is a line chart (position encodes value), so a non-zero start is acceptable, but always say so; see 8.12.</li><li><code>ax.set_title(..., pad=30)</code> moves the title up to leave room for the subtitle written with <code>ax.text(x, y, s, transform=ax.transAxes)</code>, where <code>transAxes</code> means "x and y are fractions of the axes box, 0 to 1".</li><li><code>ax.grid(axis="y")</code> and <code>set_axisbelow(True)</code> draw faint horizontal guides behind the data, never in front.</li><li><code>ax.annotate(text, xy=point, xytext=text_position, arrowprops=...)</code> writes a note and an arrow. <code>xy</code> is what you point at; <code>xytext</code> is where the words sit.</li><li><code>fig.text(0.15, 0.04, ...)</code> writes on the figure itself (fractions of the whole canvas), which is where a source note belongs.</li><li><code>badge(...)</code> is only for this teaching picture: it draws a red circle with a number using an annotation with a circular <code>bbox</code>.</li></ol>
<p>The printed check: June 2025 is 455 and June 2024 is 400, so 455 / 400 - 1 = 13.7% (the annotation); January is 350 / 310 - 1 = 12.9%. The title says "10% to 14%", and the six monthly growth rates run from 10.3% (Mar) to 13.7% (Jun). Always check that the title is true to the numbers.</p>
<h3>Units, formats and labels: small details that cost trust</h3>
<ul><li><strong>Say the unit once, in the axis label or subtitle.</strong> "Rs thousand" means 455 is Rs 455,000. If you divide by 1,000 on the chart, say so.</li><li><strong>Use thousands separators</strong> (3,298 not 3298) and a consistent number of decimals. Percent axes should say "%"; a change between two percentages is in <em>percentage points</em>, not percent.</li><li><strong>Dates:</strong> show the smallest useful unit. For monthly data, "Jan 24" or "Jan" with the year once; never "2024-01-01 00:00:00".</li><li><strong>Category labels:</strong> if they are long, flip the chart horizontal instead of rotating text.</li><li><strong>Legend or direct labels:</strong> a legend forces the eye to travel; a label at the end of each line does not (8.10).</li></ul>

<h3>Going deeper: ink, and the figure as an accessible object</h3>
<p>Edward Tufte called the share of ink that shows data the <em>data-ink ratio</em>: everything that is not data (borders, shadows, heavy gridlines, redundant legends) should be erased unless it helps reading. Do not take this to the extreme; a faint gridline and an axis label are not waste. Accessibility adds another layer: put a plain-language description of the chart in the surrounding text or the <code>alt</code> attribute (as every figure in this chapter has), do not rely on colour alone, and keep text at 11 pt or larger on the final printed size.</p>
