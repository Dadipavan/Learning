
@@H2 8.8 seaborn in Depth: Families, Figure-Level Functions and Themes
@@CALL blue|INTUITION|matplotlib asks "where do I draw this rectangle?". seaborn asks "which column goes on x, which on y, which sets colour?". It reads a tidy table (one row per observation, one column per variable), does the grouping and statistics for you (means, confidence bands, density curves) and hands back a matplotlib chart you can still edit. Think of matplotlib as the pen and seaborn as a set of stencils for the shapes analysts draw every day.
<h3>The three families</h3>
<p>Every seaborn plotting function belongs to a family. Pick the family from your question, then the function inside it.</p>
@@TABLE
Family | Question | Axes-level functions | Figure-level function
Relational | How do two numbers relate? | <code>scatterplot</code>, <code>lineplot</code> | <code>relplot</code> (<code>kind="scatter"</code> or <code>"line"</code>)
Distribution | How is one number (or a pair) spread? | <code>histplot</code>, <code>kdeplot</code>, <code>ecdfplot</code>, <code>rugplot</code> | <code>displot</code> (also <code>jointplot</code>, <code>pairplot</code>)
Categorical | How does a number differ between categories? | <code>stripplot</code>, <code>swarmplot</code>, <code>boxplot</code>, <code>violinplot</code>, <code>boxenplot</code>, <code>pointplot</code>, <code>barplot</code>, <code>countplot</code> | <code>catplot</code> (<code>kind=</code> any of those)
Regression | Is there a straight-line trend? | <code>regplot</code>, <code>residplot</code> | <code>lmplot</code>
Matrix | A grid of numbers as colour | <code>heatmap</code> | <code>clustermap</code>
@@END
<p>The first two columns are the thinking, the last two are the code. Notice the pattern: each figure-level function is a wrapper that can draw any axes-level function of its family, split into panels.</p>
@@PY sns_families
#HS
#ORD
#WEEKLY
fig, axs = plt.subplots(1, 3, figsize=(12, 3.9), layout="constrained")
# Relational: x, y, plus colour (hue) and area (size) for two more variables
sns.scatterplot(data=weekly, x="ad_spend", y="revenue_k", hue="discount", size="orders", sizes=(15, 110),
                palette="viridis", legend=False, ax=axs[0])
axs[0].set(title="Relational: scatterplot", xlabel="Ad spend (Rs thousand)", ylabel="Revenue (Rs thousand)")
# Distribution: empirical cumulative distribution, one line per channel
sns.ecdfplot(data=orders, x="amount", hue="channel", palette={"App": BLUE, "Web": ORANGE}, linewidth=2.5, ax=axs[1])
axs[1].set(title="Distribution: ecdfplot", xlabel="Order amount (Rs)", ylabel="Share of orders up to this amount")
# Categorical: mean per category with a 95% bootstrap interval
sns.pointplot(data=orders, x="category", y="amount", hue="channel", palette={"App": BLUE, "Web": ORANGE},
              order=["Electronics", "Home", "Fashion", "Grocery"], hue_order=["App", "Web"], dodge=0.3, errorbar=("ci", 95), seed=8, ax=axs[2])
axs[2].set(title="Categorical: pointplot", xlabel="", ylabel="Average order (Rs)")
sns.move_legend(axs[1], "lower right", title=None, frameon=False)
sns.move_legend(axs[2], "upper right", title=None, frameon=False)
fig.savefig("ch08v_sns_families.png", dpi=110)
print(type(axs[0]).__name__, "| median App:", orders.loc[orders.channel == "App", "amount"].median(),
      "| median Web:", orders.loc[orders.channel == "Web", "amount"].median())
print("saved")
@@END
@@FIG sns_families|Three panels: a scatter plot with colour and size, an empirical cumulative distribution with two lines, and a point plot with error bars by category|One example from each family on the same canvas. The first panel uses colour for discount and bubble size for orders.
<ol><li>Relational: <code>hue="discount"</code> maps a continuous column to colour (a sequential colormap, here viridis) and <code>size="orders"</code> maps another column to marker area. One scatter plot now carries four variables. Use this sparingly: more than three encodings overload the reader, and a continuous hue is better explained with a colour bar than the crowded legend seaborn would build (so the legend is switched off here).</li><li>Distribution: <code>ecdfplot</code> plots, for each amount on the x-axis, the share of orders at or below it. It needs no bins and no smoothing, and two groups can be compared exactly: the curve that is further right has larger orders. Reading off the 0.5 line gives the median.</li><li>Categorical: <code>pointplot</code> draws the mean of each group as a dot and a 95% bootstrap confidence interval as a line, which is the honest alternative to bars when the question is "do the groups differ?". Wide, overlapping intervals (here, most categories) mean you cannot tell.</li><li>All three functions are <strong>axes-level</strong>: they take <code>ax=</code> and draw into the axes you give them, so you can mix them in one matplotlib figure.</li></ol>
<h3>Axes-level versus figure-level: the one rule to learn</h3>
@@TABLE
 | Axes-level | Figure-level
Examples | <code>scatterplot</code>, <code>histplot</code>, <code>boxplot</code>, <code>heatmap</code> | <code>relplot</code>, <code>displot</code>, <code>catplot</code>, <code>lmplot</code>, <code>pairplot</code>, <code>jointplot</code>
Returns | A matplotlib <code>Axes</code> | A seaborn grid object (<code>FacetGrid</code>, <code>PairGrid</code>, <code>JointGrid</code>) that owns the whole figure
Draws into your axes | Yes: <code>ax=ax</code> | No: it creates its own figure
Size is set by | <code>plt.subplots(figsize=...)</code> | <code>height=</code> and <code>aspect=</code> per panel
Panels by column value | Not built in | <code>col=</code>, <code>row=</code>, <code>col_wrap=</code>
Legend | Inside the axes; move with <code>sns.move_legend</code> | Outside the plot by default
Use when | You want one plot or a custom layout | You want a grid of panels in one call
@@END

@@FIG sns_facet|A two by three grid of histograms of order amount, rows App and Web, columns Delhi, Mumbai and Pune|One displot call with row and col makes six histograms. Same bins and same axes make them comparable.
<ol><li><code>sns.displot(..., col="city", row="channel")</code> splits the data by those two columns and draws one histogram per combination: the figure-level power. The returned <code>g</code> is a <code>FacetGrid</code>.</li><li><code>height=2.0, aspect=1.5</code> size each <em>panel</em> (2 inches tall, 3 inches wide), so the figure grows with the number of panels. This is why <code>figsize</code> does nothing here.</li><li><code>g.set_axis_labels</code>, <code>g.set_titles</code> and <code>g.set</code> apply a setting to every panel at once. <code>margin_titles=True</code> writes the row name in the right margin instead of repeating it above every panel.</li><li>The printed table shows the sample size of each panel (for example, App in Delhi has the most). Panels with a few dozen orders will look jagged; always know your n.</li><li>By default all panels share axes (<code>sharex=True</code>, <code>sharey=True</code>), which is what makes small multiples honest. Pass <code>facet_kws={"sharey": False}</code> only when you must, and say so.</li></ol>

<p><strong>How to read it.</strong> The diagonal shows each column's own distribution; each other cell shows two columns against each other. <h3>Hue, style, size: what each aesthetic should carry</h3>
@@TABLE
Argument | Maps a column to | Good for | Watch out
<code>hue</code> | Colour | A category (qualitative palette) or a number (sequential palette) | More than 5 categories; red and green together
<code>style</code> | Marker shape or line dash | A category; duplicates hue for accessibility | More than 4 shapes
<code>size</code> | Marker area or line width | A positive number | Small differences are invisible (8.1)
<code>col</code>, <code>row</code> | Panel | A category with 2 to 12 levels | Panels that are too small to read
<code>order</code>, <code>hue_order</code> | Order of the categories | Always set for ordered categories (months, age bands) | Alphabetical default
<code>palette</code> | Colours for the hue levels | A name (<code>"colorblind"</code>) or dict {level: colour} | A name with no hue (deprecated, see the errors block)
@@END
<h3>Themes, contexts and palettes</h3>
@@PY sns_theme
import seaborn as sns

sns.set_theme(style="white", context="notebook", palette="colorblind", font_scale=1.0)
print("colorblind palette:", sns.color_palette("colorblind").as_hex()[:6])
print("default notebook font size:", sns.plotting_context("notebook")["axes.labelsize"],
      "| talk:", sns.plotting_context("talk")["axes.labelsize"],
      "| poster:", sns.plotting_context("poster")["axes.labelsize"])
print("styles:", ["white", "whitegrid", "dark", "darkgrid", "ticks"])
print("blues (5 steps):", sns.color_palette("Blues", 5).as_hex())
print("diverging:", sns.diverging_palette(250, 30, n=5).as_hex())
@@END
<ul><li><strong>Style</strong> (<code>"white"</code>, <code>"whitegrid"</code>, <code>"ticks"</code>, <code>"dark"</code>, <code>"darkgrid"</code>) sets background, grid and spines. For reports <code>"white"</code> or <code>"whitegrid"</code> with despined top and right edges (<code>sns.despine()</code>) is the cleanest.</li><li><strong>Context</strong> (<code>"paper"</code>, <code>"notebook"</code>, <code>"talk"</code>, <code>"poster"</code>) scales all text and line widths together: 11 pt for a document, 16.5 pt (notebook 12 x 1.5 scale) for slides. The printed label sizes show the scale.</li><li><strong>Palette</strong> names: <code>"colorblind"</code> (qualitative, safe), <code>"Blues"</code> (sequential), <code>"vlag"</code> or <code>"RdBu_r"</code> (diverging), <code>"viridis"</code> and <code>"rocket"</code> (perceptually uniform). <code>sns.color_palette(name, n)</code> returns n colours; <code>.as_hex()</code> shows them.</li><li><strong>Calling <code>set_theme</code> resets matplotlib rcParams.</strong> Pass your own rc dict through <code>rc={...}</code> (as in the chapter header) or call it once, before everything else.</li></ul>
@@PY sns_errors
import warnings
import pandas as pd
import seaborn as sns
import matplotlib
matplotlib.use("Agg")
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

df = pd.DataFrame({"city": ["Delhi", "Pune", "Delhi", "Pune"], "amount": [10, 20, 30, 40]})
def attempt(label, fn):
    try:
        with warnings.catch_warnings(record=True) as w:
            warnings.simplefilter("always")
            fn()
        for item in w:
            print(f"{label}\n   {item.category.__name__}: {' '.join(str(item.message).split())[:100]}")
    except Exception as e:
        print(f"{label}\n   {type(e).__name__}: {str(e)[:110]}")

attempt("1 wrong column name", lambda: sns.barplot(data=df, x="City", y="amount"))
attempt("2 palette without hue", lambda: sns.barplot(data=df, x="city", y="amount", palette="Set2"))
fig, ax = plt.subplots()
attempt("3 ax= on a figure-level function", lambda: sns.displot(data=df, x="amount", ax=ax))
attempt("4 hue column name typo", lambda: sns.scatterplot(data=df, x="amount", y="amount", hue="Amount"))
@@END
@@TABLE
Symptom | Cause | Fix
<code>ValueError: Could not interpret value ... for x</code> | Column name wrong (case, spaces) or <code>data=</code> missing | <code>print(df.columns.tolist())</code>; pass <code>data=df</code>
<code>FutureWarning: Passing `palette` without assigning `hue`</code> | seaborn 0.13 wants hue whenever a palette is given | <code>hue="city", legend=False</code>
<code>UserWarning: displot is a figure-level function and does not accept the ax= parameter</code> | Figure-level functions create their own figure, so the plot does not land in your axes | Use the axes-level function (<code>histplot</code>, <code>boxplot</code>, <code>scatterplot</code>) with <code>ax=</code>
Confidence band differs between runs | The bootstrap is random | <code>seed=</code> in <code>lineplot</code>/<code>pointplot</code>, or <code>errorbar=None</code>
Bars have a black line at the top | <code>barplot</code> shows a mean with error bars by default | <code>errorbar=None</code> for sums and pre-aggregated data; set <code>estimator="sum"</code> to sum
Colours look washed out | Default <code>saturation=0.75</code> | <code>saturation=1</code>
Title or legend cut off after <code>savefig</code> | Legend outside the axes | <code>bbox_inches="tight"</code>
@@END
@@CALL red|COMMON MISTAKE|Letting <code>barplot</code> average silently. <code>sns.barplot(data=orders, x="city", y="amount")</code> draws the <em>mean</em> order per city with an error bar, not the total. If you wanted totals, say so: <code>estimator="sum", errorbar=None</code>, or aggregate with pandas first. Always check one bar against <code>groupby(...).agg(...)</code>.
<h3>Going deeper: what seaborn does under the hood</h3>
<p>Under the hood every seaborn plot follows the same recipe: map data variables to visual properties (<em>semantics</em>), split the data by them, apply a <em>statistic</em> (mean, density estimate, bootstrap interval) and draw a <em>mark</em>. Since version 0.12 the <code>seaborn.objects</code> interface (<code>so.Plot(df, x=, y=).add(so.Line())</code>) exposes that recipe directly and is the modern direction, but the function-based API above is what you will meet in 95% of real code. Two practical consequences: bootstrap-based intervals cost time on large data (use <code>errorbar=("sd")</code> or <code>None</code> past a few hundred thousand rows), and any statistic seaborn computes is also computable by you with pandas, so you can always verify a bar or a band.</p>
