<h3>Scatter plot: do two numbers move together?</h3>
<p><strong>Answers:</strong> "Is there a relationship between X and Y, how strong, are there outliers or clusters?" <strong>Do not use when:</strong> one axis is a category (use box or strip), or you have millions of points (use hexbin or a density plot; see the performance note in 8.13). Remember: a pattern is a question, not an explanation. Correlation is not causation.</p>
@@PY scatter
#H
#WEEKLY
r = np.corrcoef(weekly["ad_spend"], weekly["revenue_k"])[0, 1]
slope, intercept = np.polyfit(weekly["ad_spend"], weekly["revenue_k"], 1)
print(f"r = {r:.2f}   revenue = {intercept:.1f} + {slope:.2f} x ad_spend   r squared = {r ** 2:.2f}")
resid = weekly["revenue_k"] - (intercept + slope * weekly["ad_spend"])
worst = resid.abs().idxmax()
print(f"biggest miss: week {worst}, ad spend {weekly.loc[worst, 'ad_spend']}, revenue {weekly.loc[worst, 'revenue_k']}, "
      f"expected {intercept + slope * weekly.loc[worst, 'ad_spend']:.1f}")

@@FIG scatter|Scatter plot of 52 weeks of ad spend against revenue with an upward orange trend line and one outlier week annotated|Each dot is one week. The orange line is the least-squares fit; r is the correlation.
<p><strong>How to read it.</strong> The cloud climbs from lower left to upper right: more spend, more revenue. <h3>Correlation matrix: every pair at once</h3>
<p><strong>Answers:</strong> "Which of these numeric columns move together?" Each cell is Pearson's r between two columns. <strong>Do not use when:</strong> variables are categories, relationships are curved (r only sees straight lines), or you have hundreds of columns (cluster first). Use a <strong>diverging</strong> palette centred on 0 and show each pair once.</p>
@@PY corr_sns
#HS
#WEEKLY

                 square=True, linewidths=1, linecolor="white", cbar_kws={"label": "Correlation (r)", "shrink": 0.8})
ax.set(title="Visits and orders move with revenue; discount links to returns")
ax.tick_params(length=0)
plt.setp(ax.get_xticklabels(), rotation=30, ha="right")
ax.figure.savefig("ch08v_corr_sns.png", dpi=110, bbox_inches="tight")
print("saved")
@@END
@@FIG corr_sns|Lower-triangle correlation heat map of six weekly marketing columns in red and blue with numbers in each cell|Correlation matrix of the weekly table. Red is a positive link, blue negative, white near zero.
<p><strong>How to read it.</strong> The diagonal is 1 (each column with itself). Orders and revenue: r = 0.97, almost a straight line. Visits and orders: 0.93. The discount is nearly unrelated to revenue (0.12) but strongly related to returns (0.72): in this data, deep-discount weeks bring more returns. <h3>Cohort retention heatmap: do newer customers stay longer?</h3>
<p><strong>Answers:</strong> "Of the customers who joined in month X, what share was still active k months later?" Rows are cohorts (the join month), columns are months since joining, cells are retention percentages. Read down a column to see whether newer cohorts do better. <strong>Do not use when:</strong> cohorts are tiny (percentages jump around) or you only want one average curve. Chapter 12 shows how to build the cohort table with SQL and pandas.</p>
@@PY cohort
#HS
rng = np.random.default_rng(8)
cohorts = ["Jan", "Feb", "Mar", "Apr", "May", "Jun"]
size = [520, 560, 610, 580, 640, 700]
base = np.array([1.0, 0.46, 0.36, 0.31, 0.28, 0.26])
rows = []
for i in range(6):
    r = base.copy()
    r[1:] = np.clip(r[1:] * (1 + 0.04 * i) + rng.normal(0, 0.012, 5), 0, 1)     # later cohorts retain a bit better
    r = np.concatenate([r[: 6 - i], np.full(i, np.nan)])                         # months not observed yet stay empty
    rows.append(r)
ret = pd.DataFrame(rows, index=[f"{c} (n={n})" for c, n in zip(cohorts, size)], columns=[f"M{k}" for k in range(6)])
print((ret * 100).round(0).to_string(na_rep="-"))
m1 = ret["M1"]
ax = sns.heatmap(ret.drop(columns="M0") * 100, annot=True, fmt=".0f", cmap="Blues", vmin=20, vmax=60,
                 linewidths=1.5, linecolor="white", cbar_kws={"label": "Customers still active (%)"},
                 annot_kws={"fontsize": 12, "fontweight": "bold"})
ax.set(xlabel="Months since joining", ylabel="Cohort (month joined)",
       title=f"Month-1 retention rose from {m1.iloc[0]:.0%} to {m1.iloc[4]:.0%}")
ax.tick_params(length=0)
ax.figure.savefig("ch08v_cohort.png", dpi=110, bbox_inches="tight")
print("saved")
@@END
@@FIG cohort|Cohort retention heat map with six cohort rows and months one to five as columns, darker blue meaning higher retention, triangular shape because later cohorts have fewer months|Cohort retention (synthetic). Month 0 is always 100% so it is left out. The empty lower-right triangle is the future.
<p><strong>How to read it.</strong> Row = people who joined in that month. Reading right along a row shows how that cohort decays: from 44% after one month to 23% after five (January joiners). Reading <em>down</em> a column compares cohorts at the same age: month-1 retention climbs from 44% (January joiners) to 54% (May joiners), so newer customers stay somewhat better. The triangle is empty because June joiners have not yet lived five months. In real data, check for what changed when the line bends (a new onboarding flow, a price change).</p>
<ol><li>The loop builds each cohort's decay curve from a base curve multiplied by a growing factor <code>(1 + 0.04 * i)</code> plus noise. This is only to make synthetic data with a built-in improvement.</li><li><code>np.concatenate([r[: 6 - i], np.full(i, np.nan)])</code> keeps only the months already observed and leaves the rest NaN (empty). Blank cells for unobserved months are essential; filling them with zero would suggest customers vanished.</li><li><code>ret.drop(columns="M0")</code> removes the all-100% column, which would waste the dark end of the colour scale. <code>vmin=20, vmax=60</code> uses the full colour range for the interesting values.</li><li>The title is computed from the table, so if the data changes the headline changes with it.</li></ol>

<h3>Small multiples: one panel per group</h3>
<p><strong>Answers:</strong> "How does the pattern look in each group?" when overlaying the groups would make spaghetti. Same chart, same scales, repeated in a grid (Tufte's "small multiples"). <strong>Do not use when:</strong> there are only 2 or 3 groups that overlay cleanly, or you cannot afford the space for more than about 12 panels.</p>
@@PY smallmult
#H
#ORD
orders["month"] = orders["date"].dt.to_period("M").dt.to_timestamp()
cat_m = (orders.pivot_table(index="month", columns="category", values="amount", aggfunc="sum") / 1000)
cat_m = cat_m[["Electronics", "Home", "Fashion", "Grocery"]]
print(cat_m.round(1).agg(["min", "max"]).to_string())
x = np.arange(len(cat_m))
fig, axs = plt.subplots(2, 2, figsize=(8, 5), sharex=True, sharey=True)
for ax, cat in zip(axs.ravel(), cat_m.columns):
    for other in cat_m.columns:
        ax.plot(x, cat_m[other], color="#dddddd", lw=1.5)               # all categories as faint context
    ax.plot(x, cat_m[cat], color=BLUE, lw=3)                           # this panel's category on top
    ax.set_title(cat, fontsize=12)
    ax.set_xticks(x, [d.strftime("%b") for d in cat_m.index])
for ax in axs[:, 0]: ax.set_ylabel("Sales (Rs thousand)")
fig.suptitle("Electronics swings most; Grocery stays small", x=0.01, ha="left", fontweight="bold", fontsize=14)
fig.tight_layout()
fig.savefig("ch08v_smallmult.png", dpi=110)
print("saved")
@@END
@@FIG smallmult|A two by two grid of line charts, one per category, each with the same axes and all four lines faint grey in the background|Small multiples: same scale in every panel, the panel's own line in blue, the others faint grey for context.
<p><strong>How to read it.</strong> Every panel uses the same x and y scale (<code>sharex</code>, <code>sharey</code>), so heights can be compared across panels. In each panel the blue line is that category and the grey lines are the others, so you always see where it sits in the group. Electronics (top left) swings from 11k to 31k; Grocery (bottom right) never leaves the bottom.</p>
<ol><li><code>plt.subplots(2, 2, sharex=True, sharey=True)</code> makes four panels with a common scale. Shared scales are the rule that makes small multiples honest; only break it on purpose and say so.</li><li><code>axs.ravel()</code> flattens the 2 by 2 array of axes into a list so a simple loop can walk over them with <code>zip</code>.</li><li>The inner loop draws all four categories in pale grey first, then the panel's own category in blue and thick on top (later calls draw above earlier ones).</li><li><code>fig.suptitle(..., x=0.01, ha="left")</code> sets a left-aligned overall title.</li></ol>

<h3>Map awareness: charts of places</h3>
<p><strong>Answers:</strong> "How does it vary by region?" Maps are tempting, but they have three traps. (1) <strong>Area bias</strong>: big regions look important whether or not they matter; a small dense city disappears. (2) <strong>Counts versus rates</strong>: a map of raw counts mostly maps population. Show a rate (orders per 1,000 residents) instead. (3) <strong>Colour</strong>: choropleths (shaded regions) use colour, the weakest encoding. <strong>Do not use a map when</strong> the places have no real geographic story: a sorted bar of regions is clearer. When you do want a map-like view, a <em>tile grid</em> gives every region the same size.</p>
@@PY tilemap
#H
region = pd.DataFrame({"orders_k": [640, 60, 570, 330, 250, 540], "pop_m": [320, 46, 190, 280, 250, 270]},
                      index=["North", "Northeast", "West", "Central", "East", "South"])
region["per_1000"] = (region["orders_k"] / region["pop_m"]).round(2)
print(region.to_string())
print("rank by count :", region["orders_k"].sort_values(ascending=False).index.tolist())
print("rank by rate  :", region["per_1000"].sort_values(ascending=False).index.tolist())
pos = {"North": (1, 0), "Northeast": (2, 0), "West": (0, 1), "Central": (1, 1), "East": (2, 1), "South": (1, 2)}
fig, axs = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(9, 4.2))
for ax, col, title, cmap, fmt in [(axs[0], "orders_k", "Orders (thousand): maps population", "Greys", "{:.0f}"),
                                  (axs[1], "per_1000", "Orders per 1,000 residents", "Blues", "{:.1f}")]:
    vals = region[col]
    for name, (cx, cy) in pos.items():
        shade = plt.get_cmap(cmap)(0.15 + 0.7 * (vals[name] - vals.min()) / (vals.max() - vals.min()))
        ax.add_patch(plt.Rectangle((cx, cy), 0.95, 0.95, color=shade))
        dark = (vals[name] - vals.min()) / (vals.max() - vals.min()) > 0.55
        ax.text(cx + 0.475, cy + 0.6, name, ha="center", va="center", fontsize=10, color="white" if dark else "#222")
        ax.text(cx + 0.475, cy + 0.32, fmt.format(vals[name]), ha="center", va="center", fontsize=13,
                fontweight="bold", color="white" if dark else "#222")
    ax.set(xlim=(0, 3), ylim=(3, 0), aspect="equal", title=title); ax.axis("off")
    ax.set_title(title, fontsize=12, loc="left")
fig.tight_layout()
fig.savefig("ch08v_tilemap.png", dpi=110)
print("saved")
@@END
@@FIG tilemap|Two tile-grid maps of six regions: left shaded grey by order count with North darkest, right shaded blue by orders per thousand residents with West darkest|Tile grid: every region gets the same size. Left: raw counts. Right: the rate per 1,000 residents. The leader changes. Synthetic numbers.
<p><strong>How to read it.</strong> On the left, North has the most orders (640 thousand), but North also has 320 million residents. On the right, the rate per 1,000 residents tells a different story: West leads with 3.0, North only ties South at 2.0, and Central (about 1.2) and East (1.0) are the weakest. The Northeast, tiny in count, has a rate of 1.3, above Central and East. The raw map mostly measures population; the rate map measures behaviour.</p>
<ol><li><code>region["orders_k"] / region["pop_m"]</code> divides thousand orders by million residents, which is exactly orders per 1,000 residents (thousand per million = per thousand).</li><li>The two printed rankings (count versus rate) prove the point before any drawing: the leader changes from North to West, and North falls to a tie for second.</li><li><code>plt.Rectangle((x, y), w, h)</code> is one tile; <code>pos</code> places each region on a rough geographic layout (north at the top). Every tile has the same size, which removes area bias.</li><li>The colour is picked by scaling the value to 0.15 to 0.85 of the colormap and the text turns white on dark tiles, for contrast.</li></ol>
