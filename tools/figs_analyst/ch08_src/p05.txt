@@CALL blue|INTUITION|An average is a single-number summary, like describing a city by its mean temperature. It hides the winters and the summers. A distribution chart shows every season at once: where most values sit, how far they spread, and whether a few extreme values stretch one side. Most real business money data is lopsided: lots of small orders and a few huge ones. If you only quote the mean you will mislead.
<p>All the charts in this section draw the same column, <code>amount</code> in the 300-row orders table. Before the code, here are the numbers, because a good habit is to <strong>describe first, draw second</strong>.</p>
<h3>Histogram: how are the values spread?</h3>
<p><strong>Answers:</strong> "What is the typical value, how wide is the spread, is it lopsided, are there outliers?" A histogram cuts the number line into equal-width bins and draws one bar per bin whose height is the count of values inside. <strong>Do not use when:</strong> you have fewer than about 30 values (use a dot plot), or you want to compare many groups (use box or violin).</p>
@@PY hist
#H
#ORD
amt = orders["amount"]
print(amt.describe().round(0).to_string())
print(f"share under Rs 1,000: {(amt < 1000).mean():.0%} | mean {amt.mean():.0f} | median {amt.median():.0f}")
for width in (50, 200, 1000, 3000):
    counts, _ = np.histogram(amt, bins=np.arange(0, amt.max() + width, width))
    print(f"bin width {width:>4}: {len(counts):>3} bins, tallest bin holds {counts.max()} orders")
fig, ax = plt.subplots()
ax.hist(amt, bins=np.arange(0, 5800, 200), color=BLUE, edgecolor="white")
ax.axvline(amt.median(), color=ORANGE, lw=2.5)
ax.axvline(amt.mean(), color=RED, lw=2.5, ls="--")
ax.annotate(f"median {amt.median():,.0f}", xy=(amt.median(), 54), xytext=(1900, 54), color=ORANGE, va="center",
            fontweight="bold", arrowprops=dict(arrowstyle="-", color=ORANGE))
ax.annotate(f"mean {amt.mean():,.0f}", xy=(amt.mean(), 45), xytext=(1900, 45), color=RED, va="center",
            fontweight="bold", arrowprops=dict(arrowstyle="-", color=RED))
ax.set_xlabel("Order amount (Rs)"); ax.set_ylabel("Number of orders")
ax.set_title("Most orders are small; a few big ones pull the mean up")
fig.tight_layout()
fig.savefig("ch08v_hist.png", dpi=110)
print("saved")

@@CALL red|COMMON MISTAKE|Accepting the default number of bins, or reporting only the mean. With too few bins you hide a second peak; with too many you see noise. Wrong: <code>plt.hist(x)</code> (10 bins whatever the data). Right: try 3 widths, pick the one where the shape is stable, and report the median beside the mean.
<h3>Going deeper: how many bins?</h3>
<p>There are rules of thumb. <strong>Sturges</strong>: bins = log<sub>2</sub>(n) + 1, good for small, bell-shaped data. <strong>Freedman-Diaconis</strong>: bin width = 2 x IQR / n<sup>1/3</sup>, which uses the spread of the middle half of the data (IQR = Q3 - Q1) and so copes better with skew. numpy offers them as <code>bins="sturges"</code> and <code>bins="fd"</code>.</p>
@@PY binrules
import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(8)
amt = np.round(rng.lognormal(6.8, 0.8, 300))
q1, q3 = np.percentile(amt, [25, 75])
iqr = q3 - q1
n = len(amt)
fd_width = 2 * iqr / n ** (1 / 3)
print(f"n={n}  Q1={q1:.0f}  Q3={q3:.0f}  IQR={iqr:.0f}")
print(f"Freedman-Diaconis width = 2 x {iqr:.0f} / {n}^(1/3) = {fd_width:.0f}")
print("Sturges bins:", int(np.ceil(np.log2(n) + 1)))
print("numpy 'fd' bins:", len(np.histogram_bin_edges(amt, bins="fd")) - 1,
      "| numpy 'sturges' bins:", len(np.histogram_bin_edges(amt, bins="sturges")) - 1)
@@END
<p>Check by hand: 300^(1/3) = 6.69, so the width is 2 x IQR / 6.69 = 0.299 x IQR; the printed numbers satisfy that. The rules disagree (Sturges counts bins, FD sets a width), and that is fine: they give a starting point, your eye decides.</p>

<h3>KDE: a smoothed histogram</h3>
<p><strong>Answers:</strong> the same question as the histogram, but the smooth curve makes it easy to <em>overlay several groups</em> and compare their shapes. A kernel density estimate (KDE) puts a small bell curve on each data point and adds them up. <strong>Do not use when:</strong> the data is bounded (the curve spills past the limits, e.g. below Rs 0), has fewer than about 30 values, or you must show exact counts.</p>
@@PY kde
#H
#ORD
from scipy.stats import gaussian_kde
grid = np.linspace(0, 4500, 400)
colors = {"Electronics": BLUE, "Fashion": ORANGE, "Grocery": PURPLE}
fig, ax = plt.subplots()
for cat, color in colors.items():
    x = orders.loc[orders["category"] == cat, "amount"].to_numpy()
    dens = gaussian_kde(x)(grid)
    ax.plot(grid, dens, color=color, lw=2.5)
    ax.fill_between(grid, dens, color=color, alpha=0.12)
    peak_x = grid[dens.argmax()]
    ax.text(peak_x + 70, dens.max(), f"{cat} (n={len(x)})", color=color, va="bottom", fontweight="bold", fontsize=11)
    print(f"{cat:<12} n={len(x):>3}  median={np.median(x):>6.0f}  curve peaks near Rs {peak_x:.0f}")
ax.set_xlabel("Order amount (Rs)"); ax.set_ylabel("Density"); ax.set_yticks([])
ax.set_title("Grocery orders are small and tight; Electronics are big and varied")
ax.set_xlim(0, 4500)
fig.tight_layout()
fig.savefig("ch08v_kde.png", dpi=110)
print("saved")
@@END
@@FIG kde|Three smooth density curves for order amount by category: Grocery a tall narrow peak near 200, Fashion in the middle, Electronics a low wide curve|KDE curves by category. Area under each curve is 1, so heights compare shapes, not counts.
<p><strong>How to read it.</strong> Taller and narrower means more orders packed into a small range. Grocery (purple) peaks sharply near Rs 214 because grocery orders are all small. Electronics (blue) is low and wide: order sizes vary a lot. The y-axis has no numbers on purpose; "density" has no business meaning, only the shape matters. Each curve integrates to 1, so a category with few orders is not drawn smaller. The <code>n=</code> in each label keeps you honest about how many orders each curve rests on.</p>
<ol><li><code>gaussian_kde(x)</code> from scipy builds the estimator from the values; calling it on <code>grid</code> returns the density at 400 evenly spaced points. Its default bandwidth (how wide each little bell is) uses Scott's rule.</li><li><code>fill_between(grid, dens, alpha=0.12)</code> shades under each curve lightly. Light fill lets overlapping curves stay readable.</li><li><code>grid[dens.argmax()]</code> finds where each curve peaks; the label is placed there, so each curve is named directly.</li><li>The printed peaks are rough: the curve peak (mode) is not the median, which is the number to quote.</li><li><code>set_yticks([])</code> hides the density axis because its units are not meaningful to a reader.</li></ol>

<h3>Box plot and violin plot: compare distributions across groups</h3>
<p><strong>Answers:</strong> "How do medians and spreads differ between many groups?" A <strong>box plot</strong> draws the middle half of the data as a box (from Q1, the 25th percentile, to Q3, the 75th), the median as a line, whiskers out to the last point inside 1.5 times the box height (IQR), and dots for anything beyond. A <strong>violin</strong> draws the KDE shape mirrored around the middle, so it also shows two humps if there are two. <strong>Do not use when:</strong> a group has fewer than about 10 points (draw the points), or the audience has never met a box plot (explain it once in a caption).</p>
@@PY boxstats
import numpy as np
x = np.array([12, 15, 18, 20, 22, 25, 60])
q1, med, q3 = np.percentile(x, [25, 50, 75])
iqr = q3 - q1
low_fence, high_fence = q1 - 1.5 * iqr, q3 + 1.5 * iqr
inside = x[(x >= low_fence) & (x <= high_fence)]
print(f"Q1={q1}  median={med}  Q3={q3}  IQR={iqr}")
print(f"fences: {low_fence} to {high_fence}")
print(f"whiskers end at {inside.min()} and {inside.max()}; outliers: {x[(x < low_fence) | (x > high_fence)].tolist()}")
@@END
@@PROBLEM 8.5.1|Compute by hand the box plot of the seven values 12, 15, 18, 20, 22, 25, 60.
@@STEPS
Step 1: sort and median ## Already sorted. The median is the 4th of 7 values: 20.
Step 2: quartiles ## numpy's method interpolates: Q1 sits at position 0.25 x (7 - 1) = 1.5 (counting from 0), halfway between 15 and 18, so Q1 = 16.5. Q3 at position 4.5, halfway between 22 and 25, so Q3 = 23.5.
Step 3: IQR ## 23.5 - 16.5 = 7.
Step 4: fences ## Low fence = 16.5 - 1.5 x 7 = 6.0. High fence = 23.5 + 10.5 = 34.0.
Step 5: whiskers and outliers ## The upper whisker stops at the largest value inside the fences: 25. The lower whisker stops at 12. 60 is above 34, so it is plotted as a dot.
Check ## The block above prints exactly these numbers.
Meaning ## The "typical" range of the middle half is 16.5 to 23.5, and one value (60) is flagged as unusual. Whiskers are not min and max; they are the last point inside the fences.
@@END
<p>Different tools use slightly different quartile methods (interpolated, exclusive, inclusive), so the same 7 numbers can give Q1 of 15 or 16.5. Pandas and numpy default to the interpolated method shown. Mention the method if it matters.</p>

<h3>Going deeper: reading shape and choosing between them</h3>
<p>Skew: if the median line sits near one end of the box or the upper whisker is much longer than the lower, the data is skewed. Bimodality (two humps) is invisible in a box plot and obvious in a violin or KDE, so when in doubt about the shape, draw the violin or histogram first and the box plot second. For very large groups, the letter-value (boxen) plot, <code>sns.boxenplot</code>, draws more quantile boxes and handles the extra tail data better than the 1.5 x IQR rule, which flags many points as outliers once n is large.</p>
