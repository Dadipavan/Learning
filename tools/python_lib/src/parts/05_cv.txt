
<h2>19.5 Cross-Validation, GridSearchCV and RandomizedSearchCV</h2>
<p>One train/test split gives one number, and one number can be lucky. <strong>Cross-validation (CV)</strong> splits the training data into k folds, trains k times (each time holding out a different fold), and gives you k scores. The mean tells you the typical quality, and the spread tells you how much to trust it. Hyper-parameter searches are CV run once for every candidate setting.</p>

<p>Note on scoring names: scikit-learn maximises scores, so error metrics are negated. <code>"neg_mean_squared_error"</code> and <code>"neg_root_mean_squared_error"</code> are negative numbers, and "higher is better" holds for all of them.</p>
<h3>Example 1: cross_val_score versus cross_validate</h3>
@@PY
from sklearn.datasets import load_breast_cancer
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression
from sklearn.model_selection import cross_val_score, cross_validate
from sklearn.pipeline import make_pipeline
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler

X, y = load_breast_cancer(return_X_y=True)
pipe = make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), LogisticRegression(max_iter=1000))

print(cross_val_score(pipe, X, y, cv=5).round(3))
res = cross_validate(pipe, X, y, cv=5, scoring=["accuracy", "roc_auc", "f1"],
                     return_train_score=True)
print(sorted(res.keys()))
for k in ("test_accuracy", "train_accuracy", "test_roc_auc", "test_f1"):
    print(f"{k:15s} mean={res[k].mean():.3f}")
@@END
<ol>
<li>The pipeline scales then fits logistic regression. Because the scaler is inside, it is re-fitted in every fold on that fold's training rows (no leakage).</li>
<li><code>cross_val_score(..., cv=5)</code> returns five accuracy scores, one per fold. With an integer <code>cv</code> and a classifier it uses stratified folds without shuffling.</li>
<li><code>cross_validate</code> accepts a list of metrics and returns a dictionary of arrays: one entry per metric as <code>test_*</code>, optionally <code>train_*</code>, plus <code>fit_time</code> and <code>score_time</code>.</li>
<li>We print the sorted keys so you can see what is available, then the mean of four of them.</li>
<li>A train accuracy only slightly above the test accuracy means little overfitting. A large gap means the model memorised.</li>
</ol>
<p><strong>Reading the output:</strong> the five fold accuracies range from 0.974 to 0.991, so even a good model moves by about two points depending on the fold. Mean test accuracy is 0.981 against 0.989 on the training folds, a gap of under one point, so overfitting is small. ROC AUC (0.995) and F1 (0.985) tell the same story from other angles.</p>
<h3>Example 2: choosing the right splitter (groups and time)</h3>
<p>Standard k-fold assumes rows are independent. When several rows come from the same person, or rows are ordered in time, a random split leaks. Here each of 60 customers has 20 rows, and the label is a property of the customer, unrelated to the features beyond the customer's "signature".</p>
@@PY
import numpy as np
from sklearn.ensemble import RandomForestClassifier
from sklearn.model_selection import GroupKFold, KFold, TimeSeriesSplit, cross_val_score

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
n_groups, per = 60, 20
signature = rng.normal(size=(n_groups, 5))            # fixed fingerprint per customer
label_of_group = rng.integers(0, 2, n_groups)          # label unrelated to the fingerprint
groups = np.repeat(np.arange(n_groups), per)
X = signature[groups] + rng.normal(scale=0.1, size=(n_groups * per, 5))
y = label_of_group[groups]

rf = RandomForestClassifier(100, random_state=0)
random_cv = KFold(5, shuffle=True, random_state=0)
print("random KFold :", cross_val_score(rf, X, y, cv=random_cv).mean().round(3))
print("GroupKFold   :", cross_val_score(rf, X, y, cv=GroupKFold(5), groups=groups).mean().round(3))

for tr, va in TimeSeriesSplit(n_splits=3).split(np.arange(12)):
    print("train", tr.tolist(), "-> validate", va.tolist())
@@END
<ol>
<li>Each customer has a random 5-number fingerprint, and a label that is a coin flip per customer. By construction nothing in the fingerprint predicts the label for a <em>new</em> customer.</li>
<li>Each of a customer's 20 rows is the fingerprint plus small noise. So rows from one customer are near-duplicates.</li>
<li>With shuffled <code>KFold</code>, a customer's rows land in both training and validation. The forest simply recognises the fingerprint and recalls the label, scoring very high.</li>
<li><code>GroupKFold</code> with <code>groups=</code> keeps all rows of a customer in the same fold. Validation customers are truly unseen, and the score falls to chance level (about 0.5).</li>
<li><code>TimeSeriesSplit</code> yields expanding training windows that always come before the validation window. The printed index lists show it for 12 ordered rows.</li>
</ol>
<p><strong>Reading the output:</strong> shuffled KFold reports 0.992, a near-perfect score for a problem where the true answer is a coin flip. GroupKFold reports 0.515, which is the honest number. The time-series splits show the training window growing (3, 6, then 9 rows) and always ending before the validation rows begin.</p>
<div class="callout red"><div class="lab">COMMON MISTAKE</div><div class="txt"><strong>Wrong:</strong> <code>cross_val_score(model, X, y, cv=KFold(5, shuffle=True))</code> on data with repeated customers, patients, sensors or time order. <strong>Symptom:</strong> CV score of 0.98, production score of 0.55. <strong>Fix:</strong> ask "will the new data contain entities already in training?". If not, use <code>GroupKFold</code>; if rows are ordered in time, use <code>TimeSeriesSplit</code>.</div></div>
<h3>Example 3: GridSearchCV on a whole pipeline</h3>
@@PY
import pandas as pd
from sklearn.datasets import load_breast_cancer
from sklearn.model_selection import GridSearchCV, train_test_split
from sklearn.pipeline import Pipeline
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler
from sklearn.svm import SVC

X, y = load_breast_cancer(return_X_y=True)
X_tr, X_te, y_tr, y_te = train_test_split(X, y, stratify=y, random_state=0)

pipe = Pipeline([("scale", StandardScaler()), ("svm", SVC())])
grid = {"svm__C": [0.1, 1, 10], "svm__gamma": ["scale", 0.01, 0.1]}
gs = GridSearchCV(pipe, grid, cv=5, scoring="roc_auc")
gs.fit(X_tr, y_tr)

print("best params:", gs.best_params_)
print("best CV auc:", round(gs.best_score_, 4))
top = pd.DataFrame(gs.cv_results_).sort_values("rank_test_score").head(3)
print(top[["param_svm__C", "param_svm__gamma", "mean_test_score", "std_test_score"]].round(4).to_string(index=False))
print("test auc   :", round(gs.score(X_te, y_te), 4))
@@END
<ol>
<li>We split off a test set first. The search must never see it.</li>
<li>The pipeline has two named steps. In the grid, a parameter of a step is written as <code>stepname__parameter</code>, so <code>svm__C</code> means the <code>C</code> of the step called <code>svm</code>.</li>
<li>The grid has 3 values of C times 3 of gamma, which is 9 candidates; with 5 folds, 45 fits, plus one final refit on all training rows (<code>refit=True</code> is the default).</li>
<li>The metric <code>"roc_auc"</code> is used to rank candidates.</li>
<li><code>best_params_</code> and <code>best_score_</code> report the winner and its mean CV score.</li>
<li><code>cv_results_</code> is a dictionary of arrays; as a DataFrame sorted by <code>rank_test_score</code> we see the top three settings with their means and standard deviations.</li>
<li><code>gs.score(X_te, y_te)</code> scores the refitted best pipeline on the untouched test set. This is the number to report.</li>
</ol>
<p><strong>Reading the output:</strong> the winner is C=1 with gamma left at <code>scale</code>, CV AUC 0.9959, and the held-out test AUC is 0.9927, close to the CV figure, so the search did not overfit the folds. The top two candidates tie to four decimals (0.9959 each, with standard deviations of about 0.005 to 0.006), so the "best" is not meaningfully better than the second. When means differ by less than their standard deviations, prefer the simpler or cheaper setting.</p>
<h3>Example 4: RandomizedSearchCV with distributions</h3>
<p>When there are many settings, a grid explodes (5 values for each of 4 settings is 625 candidates). Random search samples a fixed number of combinations and is usually just as good. Continuous settings like <code>C</code> should be sampled on a log scale.</p>
@@PY
from scipy.stats import loguniform, randint
from sklearn.datasets import make_classification
from sklearn.ensemble import HistGradientBoostingClassifier
from sklearn.model_selection import RandomizedSearchCV, train_test_split

X, y = make_classification(3000, 20, n_informative=8, flip_y=0.05, random_state=0)
X_tr, X_te, y_tr, y_te = train_test_split(X, y, random_state=0)

dist = {"learning_rate": loguniform(0.02, 0.3),
        "max_leaf_nodes": randint(8, 64),
        "min_samples_leaf": randint(10, 80),
        "l2_regularization": loguniform(1e-3, 10)}
rs = RandomizedSearchCV(HistGradientBoostingClassifier(max_iter=150, random_state=0),
                        dist, n_iter=12, cv=3, scoring="accuracy", random_state=0)
rs.fit(X_tr, y_tr)
print({k: (round(v, 3) if isinstance(v, float) else int(v)) for k, v in rs.best_params_.items()})
print("CV:", round(rs.best_score_, 3), " test:", round(rs.score(X_te, y_te), 3))
@@END
<ol>
<li><code>loguniform(a, b)</code> samples numbers so that every power of ten between a and b is equally likely, which is right for rates and penalties. <code>randint(a, b)</code> samples whole numbers.</li>
<li>The dictionary maps each parameter name to a list or a distribution.</li>
<li><code>n_iter=12</code> says to try 12 random combinations, so the cost is fixed regardless of how many settings there are. <code>random_state=0</code> makes the sample repeatable.</li>
<li>After the search, <code>best_params_</code> holds the winner; we round for printing.</li>
<li>CV score and test score are printed side by side as a sanity check.</li>
</ol>
<p><strong>Reading the output:</strong> the CV score of the winner is 0.925 and the test accuracy is 0.919. The test number is a little lower, the typical size of the winner's curse.</p>
<div class="callout green"><div class="lab">KEY IDEA</div><div class="txt">The CV score of the <em>winning</em> candidate is slightly optimistic, because you picked it for being the best of many noisy scores. For an honest estimate use a test set that the search never touched, or <strong>nested CV</strong> (a search inside an outer cross-validation).</div></div>
<h3>Example 5: reading a validation curve</h3>
@@PY
import numpy as np
from sklearn.datasets import make_moons
from sklearn.model_selection import validation_curve
from sklearn.tree import DecisionTreeClassifier

X, y = make_moons(600, noise=0.35, random_state=1)
depths = np.arange(1, 16)
tr, va = validation_curve(DecisionTreeClassifier(random_state=0), X, y,
                          param_name="max_depth", param_range=depths, cv=5)
best = depths[va.mean(axis=1).argmax()]
print("best depth:", best, " val acc:", va.mean(axis=1).max().round(3))
print("depth 15  : train", tr.mean(axis=1)[-1].round(3), " val", va.mean(axis=1)[-1].round(3))
@@END
<ol>
<li><code>validation_curve</code> trains the model with each value of one setting, with 5-fold CV, and returns two arrays of shape (values, folds): training scores and validation scores.</li>
<li>We average over folds (axis 1) and find the depth where the validation mean peaks.</li>
<li>We also show depth 15, where the tree is far too flexible.</li>
</ol>
<p><strong>Reading the output:</strong> validation accuracy peaks at 0.86 for depth 5. At depth 15 the tree scores 0.997 on training folds but only 0.812 on validation folds, a gap of 18 points.</p>
<figure><img src="images/lib19_validation.png" alt="Line chart of training and validation accuracy against tree depth; training rises to 1 while validation peaks then falls" loading="lazy"/><figcaption>Validation curve for a decision tree on noisy moons. The band is one standard deviation across folds.</figcaption></figure>
<p><strong>How to read it:</strong> on the left (shallow trees) both lines are low: the model is too simple (underfitting). Moving right, training accuracy keeps climbing to 1.0 but validation peaks and then declines: the model is memorising noise (overfitting). The best setting is where validation peaks, marked by the dashed line. A big gap between the two lines is the signal to simplify or add data.</p>
<p class="problem"><span class="pl">Problem 19.5.1</span> You grid-search 4 values of one setting and 3 values of another, with 5-fold CV and <code>refit=True</code>. How many model fits happen?</p>
<table class="steps"><tr><th>Candidates</th><td>4 × 3 = 12 combinations.</td></tr><tr><th>CV fits</th><td>12 × 5 = 60 fits (each on 80% of the training rows).</td></tr><tr><th>Refit</th><td>One more fit of the best candidate on all training rows.</td></tr><tr><th>Answer</th><td>61 fits. Adding a third setting with 5 values would make it 60 × 5 + 1 = 301, which is why random search exists.</td></tr></table>
