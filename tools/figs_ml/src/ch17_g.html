17.6 Scaffolding a Project: Brief, Baseline Ladder and Experiment Log

The projects in 17.2 to 17.5 are finished examples. The hard part when you start your own is the blank page. This section is the scaffold: a one-page brief you fill in before any code, a baseline ladder that tells you when to stop, and an experiment log that makes your README honest.

The baseline ladder

Run rungs in order and stop when the next rung adds less than the noise: (1) predict the prior or the mean, (2) a simple rule a person would use, (3) a linear model, (4) a boosted tree model, (5) anything fancier. Problem 17.6.1 Read the log and decide where to stop, given that the seed-to-seed noise of PR-AUC on this data is about 0.02.

Step 1Rung 1 (prior) scores 0.103, which equals the positive rate; this is the floor any model must beat.
Step 2Logistic regression reaches 0.816: 7.9 times the floor. This is the big jump; the problem is learnable.
Step 3Histogram boosting reaches 0.875, a gain of 0.059, which is about three times the noise, so it is real.
AnswerStop at boosting: it is clearly above the linear model, and a further rung would need to beat 0.875 by more than 0.02 to be worth its cost. Record the decision in the log.
CheckIf the third row had read 0.825 (within the noise), the right choice would be logistic regression: simpler, faster, easier to explain.

The experiment log and the README

Keep a table with one row per run: id, date, what changed (one change only), data version, metric with spread, verdict. Your README's results section is then a copy of the final rows, plus a sentence on what did not work. A project that shows three failed ideas and why is more convincing than one that shows only the winner.

COMMON MISTAKE
Choosing the metric after seeing the results. Write it in the brief first, then you cannot be accused (including by yourself) of picking the flattering one.
COMMON MISTAKE
Changing three things in one run (new features, new model, new split). If the score moves you cannot say why, and the log row is useless.

Check yourself

  1. What is the floor for a 5%-positive problem measured by PR-AUC? Answer: about 0.05, the positive rate.
  2. Why log failed experiments? Answer: it prevents repeating them and shows the reader that the final choice was earned.
