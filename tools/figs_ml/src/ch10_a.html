10.5 Comparing Models Across Many Datasets: Wilcoxon, Friedman and Nemenyi

INTUITION
So far you compared two models on one dataset using repeated folds. A different question is "is forest generally better than logistic regression?" The unit of evidence is now the dataset, not the fold. One dataset gives you one score per model, and datasets differ wildly in difficulty (0.97 on one, 0.47 on another). So you cannot average raw scores. You compare ranks within each dataset: who came first, who came second. Rank tests ignore the size of the gaps, which makes them robust to a few extreme datasets.

Three tools, in order of use. The Friedman test asks one question: are all models equal? It ranks the models inside each dataset, averages the ranks, and checks whether the average ranks differ more than chance allows. If it says yes, the Nemenyi post-hoc test tells you which pairs differ: two models differ if their average ranks are further apart than a critical difference CD = qα √(k(k+1) / (6N)), for k models and N datasets (q0.05 = 2.343 for k = 3). For exactly two models use the Wilcoxon signed-rank test on the per-dataset score differences.

COMMON MISTAKE
Running every pairwise test at 0.05 and reporting the ones that pass. With 3 models you run 3 tests; with 10 models, 45. The chance that at least one passes by luck grows fast. Either use Friedman then Nemenyi, or correct the pairwise p-values (Bonferroni or the less strict Holm).
COMMON MISTAKE
Treating the 5 folds of 12 datasets as 60 independent samples. The unit of evidence for "better in general" is the dataset. Folds inside one dataset share rows, and the datasets here are only 12. Twelve is a small sample: the Friedman result (p = 0.039) is a modest finding, not a proof, and a different set of 12 datasets could move it either way.
GOING DEEPER
Wilcoxon needs at least about 6 datasets to reach p < 0.05 at all (with 5 pairs the smallest two-sided p is 0.0625). Demšar's 2006 paper is the standard reference for this recipe, and the critical-difference diagram, a line of models positioned by mean rank with bars joining models that are not separated, is the usual way to present it. If your datasets are not a representative sample of the problems you care about, the result tells you nothing about your own data: run the single-dataset paired comparison of 10.4 on your own data as well.

Check yourself

  1. Why rank instead of averaging scores? Answer: dataset difficulty differs wildly, and one dataset with a huge gap would dominate an average. Ranks give each dataset one equal vote.
  2. With N = 20 datasets and k = 4 models (q = 2.569) what is the critical difference? Answer: 2.569 × √(4×5 / (6×20)) = 2.569 × 0.408 = 1.05.
